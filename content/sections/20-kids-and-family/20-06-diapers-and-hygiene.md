# Diapering and keeping small children clean

**What this page is for.** CDC warns that even a microscopic amount of poop can carry millions of
germs. In a house with a child in diapers, diapering is the busiest route for germs to travel from
bottom to mouth, and in a grid-down house (no running water, rationed washing, everyone in one
room) that is exactly how a household-wide outbreak starts (→ 13-11, → 12-05). This page is the
routine, the hygiene and the skin. The diaper supply and the laundry system are on → 13-08.

> **The control is simple, and it does not relax when water is short: wash hands with soap and
> water after every single change** (CDC; → 13-02), clean the changing surface, and contain the
> waste (→ 13-01).

## Do this now

1. **Set up one changing station** with everything within arm's reach and **handwashing right
   there** (→ 13-02). If washing means walking to another room, it will not happen every time.
2. **Get a lidded pail** and put it where a toddler cannot reach it (→ 20-02).
3. **Stock barrier ointment** (petroleum jelly or zinc oxide). Preventing rash matters much more
   without a doctor (→ 12-07).
4. **Post the diapering steps** (below) on the wall. Order matters, and it slips at 3 a.m.

## The routine

From CDC's hygiene guidance; read the original if you can and post it. What matters most off-grid:

1. **Everything ready before you start:** clean diaper, wipes, ointment, bag. **Never walk away
   from a child on a changing surface.**
2. **Contain the soiled diaper at once:** into the lidded pail or a bag (→ 13-01, → 13-08).
3. **Clean the child front to back**, thoroughly.
4. **Clean the changing surface after every change.** This is the step that gets skipped, and it
   is how the surface becomes a germ route (CDC, When and How to Clean and Disinfect Your Home).
5. **Wash your hands with soap and water, every time.** Hand sanitiser is a supplement, not a
   replacement (CDC, About Handwashing).
6. **Wash the child's hands too.** They have touched everything and they go in the mouth.

## Why this matters more without the grid

- **Water is rationed**, so skipping the wash is tempting. Do not. A tippy-tap at the station
  makes it almost free (→ 13-02).
- **Solids go into the waste system** (→ 13-01), not the laundry. Shake or scrape first.
- **Cloth diapers make the laundry infection control** (→ 13-04): hot water, thorough rinsing,
  complete drying.
- **Everyone in one room** (→ 08-01) is the wrong set-up for stopping an outbreak (→ 13-11).
- **A toddler touches and mouths everything**: the household's most efficient germ carrier, which
  is an age, not a criticism.
- **A sick toddler dries out fast** (→ 12-05), so preventing illness is worth a great deal.

## Diaper rash: ordinary now, a real problem off-grid

Changes may be less frequent, drying incomplete and washing imperfect.
- **Change often.** A wet diaper in a cold house is a rash cause and a heat drain (→ 11-02).
- **Dry the skin completely** before the new diaper.
- **Diaper-free time** on a towel in the warm room: the most effective single thing.
- **Barrier ointment at every change** once a rash starts. Petroleum jelly works.
- **Rinse cloth diapers thoroughly.** Soap left in the cloth is a common cause of rash that will
  not clear (→ 13-04).
- **Dry in the sun when you can**: sunlight helps bleach and sanitise cloth.
- **Watch for rash that is not improving, spreads, has open sores, or comes with fever.** It may
  be a yeast or bacterial infection (→ 12-04).

**Telling yeast rash apart:** **beefy red with a sharp edge, small red "satellite" spots beyond
the main patch, worst in the skin folds** (an ordinary irritant rash spares the folds), and **not
better after 2 to 3 days of barrier cream and diaper-free time.** Yeast needs an antifungal cream
(clotrimazole 1% or miconazole 2%, sold over the counter and stocked on → 12-07), **used as its
label directs**, with barrier ointment over the top; boil-wash the cloth diapers. Spreading
pus-filled blisters, crusting or fever is bacterial, not yeast: → 12-04.

## Washing a small child with little water

- **A basin wash covers most of it:** face, hands, bottom, feet, skin folds. Very little water,
  real results (CDC, Personal Hygiene During an Emergency; → 13-02).
- **Warm the water.** Nobody, least of all a toddler, cooperates with a cold wash in a cold house.
- **Full baths become occasional**, and that is fine. Hands, face and bottom come first.
- **Hands before every meal**: the most valuable wash of the day.
- **Teeth twice a day** (→ 12-06).
- **Nails short**: dirt under nails, and scratching.
- **Lice and scabies** spread in crowded conditions; stock the treatments (→ 12-07).
- **Dry thoroughly and dress warmly afterwards** in a cold house (→ 11-02).

## Toilet learning, by age

- **Toddler (1 to 3):** **a crisis is a bad time to start toilet learning.** Wait for stability
  unless the child is clearly ready and leading.
- **Preschool and school-age:** **going backwards under stress is normal and common** (→ 20-08).
  Treat it as unremarkable and keep some larger cloth options and spare clothes; making it a
  problem makes it worse.
- **A cold, dark trip to a bucket toilet is frightening for a small child** (→ 13-01, → 13-05).
  Keep the toilet indoors, near the warm room, with a light the child can use (→ 10-01), a step
  and a seat reducer if you have one.
- **Older children and teens** need privacy for toileting and washing even in one shared room
  (→ 21-07); a hung blanket is enough. Menstrual supplies for older girls: → 13-08.

## Nights

- **Night changes in a cold, dark house are easy to put off.** Set up so they are fast: everything
  in reach, a headlamp on a hook, the pail right there.
- **Overnight, a well-fitted absorbent diaper matters**: a good use of a disposable when supply
  allows (→ 13-08).
- **A wet child overnight in a cold room loses real heat** (→ 11-02).

**Scavenge or substitute:**
- **Wipes:** cloth squares and warm water: cheaper, kinder to skin, reusable (→ 13-08). Cut from
  flannel or old towels (→ 16-04).
- **Changing pad:** a folded towel over a plastic sheet or a cut trash bag.
- **Pail:** any bucket with a lid.
- **Barrier:** petroleum jelly, lard or tallow (→ 10-04), or a plain oil.
- **Handwashing:** a tippy-tap, a jug with a nail hole, a bottle with a pinhole (→ 13-02).
- **Diapers:** flannel, towels, folded T-shirts (→ 13-08).
- **What you cannot substitute:** handwashing. That is the whole control.

## When it runs out

- **Disposables and wipes run out; cloth does not** (→ 13-08). The change is from a supply
  problem to a water, soap and drying problem: a better problem, but a real one (→ 13-04, → 11-03).
- **Count disposables in days of a toddler.** It is a sobering number. Put it on → 23-02.
- **Use disposables where they count most:** overnight, during illness, in a cold snap when
  drying is impossible, and on the move. Cloth the rest of the time.
- **Never ration handwashing after changes, or change frequency.** Both cost far less than the
  illness or rash they prevent.
- **This problem ends on its own.** A child is out of diapers in a few years.

## By phase

- **A (shelter in place):** cloth as the default **while** water, fuel for a hot wash and indoor
  drying all hold; fall back on the disposable buffer when they do not (→ 13-08, → 13-04,
  → 03-01). Set up the station properly in the first week.
- **B (moving):** **disposables, without hesitation.** You cannot wash and dry cloth on the move,
  and a wet child in the cold is in danger (→ 11-02). Pack out the waste or bury it properly, well
  away from water (→ 13-07); carry hand sanitiser for when washing is impossible.
- **C (establish):** cloth throughout, with more water and drying space and a proper set-up near
  the stove (→ 13-04). Diaper waste is human waste and goes in the same system, away from water
  (→ 13-07).
