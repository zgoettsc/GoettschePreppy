# Teaching children the plan

**What this page is for.** A child who knows what to do is safer, calmer and easier to keep safe
than one who does not. Teaching is a real safety layer, **but it sits on top of barriers and
supervision** (→ 20-02), never in place of them. This page lists what to teach at each age, how
to teach it so it sticks, and the drills to run.

**The tone matters as much as the content.** Taught as competence and routine, this makes a
child feel capable. Taught as danger and fear, it makes anxious children who follow rules less
well (→ 20-08, → 21-01). **Frame everything as "here's what we do", not "here's what could happen
to you."**

**Your children:** {{#each children as c}}{{c.label}} ({{c.age_text}}); {{/each}}{{#each teens as t}}{{t.label}} ({{t.age_text}}); {{/each}}{{#if !has.child}}{{#if !has.teen}}none entered.{{/if}}{{/if}}

## Do this now

1. **Teach each child who can talk five things this month:** their full name and their parents'
   full names; any allergies they have; the fire rule; the water rule; and what to do if lost
   (stay put, blow the whistle).
2. **Give them equipment that goes with the rules:** their own **headlamp**, so they never need
   to carry a flame, and their own **whistle** on their jacket (→ 10-01, → 15-07). Children keep
   rules they have tools for.
3. **Practise the shelter drill and the "we got separated" drill as games** (→ 17-06, → 24-02).
   Twice, once at night.
4. **Teach the "grown-up won't wake up" drill** (below). Hang a whistle at the child's height and
   tape an address card beside the phone first, so the steps have tools.
5. **Teach "no puffy coat in the car seat"** as their rule too: coat off, harness snug, blanket
   over the top (AAP; → 11-02).
6. **Put a medical card in each child's pocket** and tell them what it is for (→ 15-03, → 25-09).

## What to teach, by age

### Infant and toddler (under 3)

Almost nothing yet, and that is the planning point. **The environment does the work**: barriers,
latches, lids and constant supervision (→ 20-02). Routine and predictability build their sense of
safety (→ 21-04). They learn by watching older children, which cuts both ways: an older child
keeping the rules helps. Re-teach as they grow; in a year or two they will be learning the
preschool list.

### Preschool (3 to 5)

CDC's development guidance describes preschoolers as able to follow simple rules, remember
routines and learn best by repetition and play rather than explanation (CDC, Positive Parenting
Tips).

| Teach | How | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Full name and parents' full names** | Repetition, a song | Separation (→ 15-03) |
| **Their allergies, if any** | Repetition; a card in the pocket | Life-critical (→ 02-03) |
| **"I only eat food a grown-up gives me"** | Absolute, repeated, no exceptions | Allergies, spoiled food, foraged plants |
| **The fire rule:** fire stays where a grown-up puts it | Show the hot-zone tape; give a headlamp | Daily hazard (→ 12-03) |
| **The water rule:** never near a pool, pond or bucket without a grown-up | Repetition; gates stay latched | Drowning (→ 20-02) |
| **The shelter drill:** where we go, what we do | Practise as a game, twice | → 17-06 |
| **Lost:** stay put, blow the whistle three times, keep blowing | Practise outdoors | → 15-07 |
| **"Grown-up won't wake up"** | The script below, every few months, as a game | Life-critical |
| **"Only people on my list"** | Recite the names | → 00-04 |
| **"Tell a grown-up if..."** hurt, cold, tingly, scared, sees something wrong | Praise every time they do | Turns them into a sensor |
| **Where the kits are**, and that they can fetch an adult | Show them | → 12-08 |
| **The meeting place** | Walk there; make it a landmark they know | → 15-03 |

### School-age (6 to 12)

Everything on the preschool list, plus:
- **Home address and a phone number by heart**, and the out-of-area contact (→ 00-04).
- **How to call 911 and what to say** (AAP, Teaching Your Child How to Use 911): their name,
  the address, what happened, and stay on the line. A mobile phone with no service plan can still
  call 911, but only where it has a signal (FCC).
- **The family communications plan** (→ 15-03): meeting places, radio channel, signals.
- **Basic first aid:** pressure on bleeding, cooling a burn, the recovery position (→ 12-01).
- **Carrying and checking their own go-bag** (→ 22-03).
- **Reading the household's own signs:** the carbon monoxide alarm sound and what to do (get
  everyone out), the smoke alarm sound.

### Teen (13 to 17)

Treat teens as junior adults in the plan:
- **The whole family plan** (→ 00-04), the go/no-go triggers (→ 01-02) and their own role
  (→ 00-07).
- **First aid and CPR training**, ideally a real class (→ 12-18).
- **Radio use** (→ 15-02) and the comms plan.
- **How to shut off the utilities** (→ 16-12) and use an extinguisher (→ 29-12).
- **Dangerous jobs** on the household's own worksheet (→ 30-25), and firearm safety rules even in
  a house with no firearms (→ 29-11).
- **How to give help when an adult is down**, including any household member's emergency
  medicine they have been trained to give (→ 02-03, → 02-01).

## How to teach it so it sticks

- **Repetition beats explanation.** "We do this" works far better than "here's why" until about
  school age.
- **Make it a game.** Time the shelter drill. Race to the meeting place. Practise the whistle
  outside. Play is how young children learn.
- **Give a job in the drill** (→ 20-07): carrying a small bag, holding the flashlight. A child with
  a role takes part; a child with no role panics or resists.
- **Praise the behaviour, every time**, above all telling an adult something.
- **Never make a child afraid to report.** "You are never in trouble for being lost, for telling
  us you're cold, for saying you ate something." A child who fears trouble hides things, and a
  hidden food exposure or a hidden fall is dangerous.
- **Keep it short and concrete.** "We stay behind the tape when the fire is on." Not a lecture.
- **Model it.** Children copy what adults do, not what they say.
- **Re-teach as they grow** (→ 25-10). A rule taught at four does not fit at six.

## Allergies: the conversation for a child who has one

{{#if has.anaphylaxis}}Your household's allergy list: {{#each allergies as a}}{{a.member}}: {{a.allergen}} ({{a.severity}}); {{/each}}{{/if}}

- **The rule is absolute and simple:** "I only eat food a grown-up who knows my allergies gives
  me." Not "be careful". Not "check first".
- **Teach the names** of their allergens as they can manage them. Where an allergy covers a whole
  group (for example tree nuts), teach the whole group as a "no"; a small child cannot tell one nut
  from another in someone else's kitchen. Exceptions are the adults' to manage (→ 02-04).
- **Teach them to say "I'm allergic"** to any adult offering food.
- **Teach what a reaction feels like** and to tell an adult **at once**: tingly mouth, itchy
  throat, tummy hurts, feels funny (CDC, Recognizing and Responding to Anaphylaxis). Frame it as
  "tell us right away and we'll fix it."
- **Do not make a young child responsible for avoidance.** The adults own the safe-food chain
  (→ 02-04). The child's job is to eat only what they are handed and to speak up.
- **Teach every other adult who might feed them:** neighbours, relatives, anyone (→ 18-01). That
  is a bigger risk than the child's own behaviour.

## The "grown-up won't wake up" drill

**Why it exists.** Any adult can collapse: a fall, a seizure, carbon monoxide, a heart problem, a
diabetic low. A young child cannot treat it, but **can be drilled to get help**. The AAP says
young children can learn a simple, rehearsed get-help script, and its first lesson is calling 911
(AAP, Teaching Your Child How to Use 911). While phones work, that is the step that saves the
adult.
{{#if has.condition.CL-01}}**Your household includes insulin-treated diabetes:** a severe low is
the likeliest reason this drill is ever used (→ 02-01). The treatment is an adult's trained job;
the child's job is getting the adult.{{/if}}{{#if has.condition.CL-11}} **Your household includes
a seizure disorder** (→ 02-11): add "move things away and do not hold them down" only for older
children taught by an adult.{{/if}}

**Teach it word for word (put in the names that fit your family):**

```
IF A GROWN-UP WON'T WAKE UP, OR ACTS FUNNY OR WOBBLY:
  1. SHOUT for the other grown-up, as loud as you can.
  2. If no one comes: CALL 9-1-1 on the phone.
     Say: "MY ______ WON'T WAKE UP."
     Then read the ADDRESS CARD by the phone. DON'T hang up.
  3. BLOW THE WHISTLE (the hook by the door). Keep blowing.
  4. STAY with the little ones and KEEP SHOUTING and BLOWING.
     That IS the job.
  5. ONLY if it is DAYTIME and a grown-up said TODAY that the phones
     are broken: go to ________________ and say
     "MY ______ WON'T WAKE UP."
```

Step 5's place: {{ contacts.meet.near_home }} (the near-home meeting place in → 00-04), or a
neighbour named here: ______________________.

- **The phone gesture.** Most mobile phones have an emergency SOS shortcut (often holding the side
  button with a volume button) as well as dialling 911. Write the exact steps for each phone in
  the house: phone 1 ______________ ; phone 2 ______________ . **Practise the gesture with the
  call cancelled, or on a pretend phone. Never dial 911 to practise** (AAP).
- **The address card.** The street address in large print, taped at the child's eye level by the
  phone and the whistle hook, read aloud together until they can say it. A child can read it
  back even if they cannot remember it. Address: __________________________________ {{photo address-card}}
- **Why "stay" comes before "go".** A young child cannot weigh dark, cold or traffic before
  crossing streets alone; they will do whatever they were last praised for. So the default is
  **stay, shout, whistle.** Leaving is the exception, true only when it is daylight **and** an
  adult said that morning, "the phones are broken today; if someone won't wake up, you go to
  ______." When phones are down, one adult says that line every morning; when they work, nobody
  does, and step 5 does not exist.
- **What the child must never do: touch the adult's medicine.** Not pens, pumps, syringes or
  pills. **Their job is noise and feet, never medicine** (→ 20-02). Older children and teens may
  be trained by an adult to give a specific emergency medicine (→ 02-03, → 02-01); that is a
  separate, adult-taught skill.
- **Practise every few months, as a game, never as a fright** (→ 24-02): announced, short, ended
  with a high five, stopped if the child is scared. A grown-up "sleeps" on the floor; the child
  runs the steps. **Never tell them the grown-up could really die.** The frame is "this is how you
  get the grown-ups." After any real event, debrief with → 21-03.

## Pets in an alarm

{{#if has.pets}}Teach it as its own line, because in a real alarm a child **will** think of the
pets:{{else}}If the household ever has pets, teach this as its own line:{{/if}}

> "When the alarm goes, the pets look after themselves. Your job is YOU. Nobody chases a pet,
> not you and not the grown-ups."

Carriers can wait at the shelter spot with doors open; an animal that comes gets crated; one that
does not waits for the all-clear (→ 20-09). Seconds spent chasing an animal are seconds the
family is not in shelter. The comfort to say, because it is often true: animals are good at
finding their own hiding places.

## "Only people on my list": separation and reunification

The child's side of the reunification plan (→ 00-04):
- **The rule: "If my grown-ups don't come, I go only with people on my list."** Practise
  reciting the names the same way as their own name. A stranger, even a kind one, is not on the
  list. A family password the list knows is an easy check.
- **Write the collection plan in pen** for a school or workday event:

```
IF IT HAPPENS ON A SCHOOL OR WORK DAY:
  Collects the children: ______________ (default: whichever adult is
     closer at that hour)
  Goes home / to the meeting place: ______________
  If school or childcare is unreachable: staff follow their release
     list. These names MUST be on it: ________________________
  Any child's emergency medicine plan on file there: YES / NO
```

- **When a child starts a new school or childcare,** re-do the pickup list, the medicine plan with
  the school nurse, and the reunification point, and re-run this page and → 00-04 that month.

## Drills: actually run them

| Drill | How often | What you will learn |
|---|---|---|
| **Shelter** (tornado, storm; → 17-06; NWS Tornado Safety) | Twice now, then each season | It takes longer than you think; a small child freezes; the light is dead |
| **Fire escape** (→ 00-04) | Twice a year | Two ways out; where you meet |
| **Grown-up won't wake up** | Every few months, as a game | Whether they can work the phone, find the whistle, read the card, and stay |
| **"We got separated"** (→ 15-03) | Once, then yearly | They do not remember the meeting place |
| **Lost: stay put and whistle** (→ 15-07) | Outdoors, a few times | They want to walk toward you; that is the habit to break |
| **Blackout evening** (→ 24-04) | Yearly | Everything |

**Debrief each one positively:** "You did great. Next time let's try X." Never scare them with
what would have happened.

## The balance to hold

The household is preparing for serious things and children absorb some of that regardless
(→ 20-08). **The goal is a child who feels capable and safe, not one who is anxious and always on
watch.**
- Tell them the adults have a plan and their job is small and specific.
- Answer questions honestly at their level, and do not volunteer more (→ 20-08).
- Keep normal life normal: play, stories, routine, treats (→ 21-06).
- Watch for signs it is landing badly: new fears, sleep problems, going backwards, clinginess
  (→ 20-08).

**Scavenge or substitute:** the teaching costs nothing and is the most valuable thing on this
page. A card can be an index card in a zip bag, or written inside a jacket, or written on the
child's arm in marker (a real disaster-response practice). Any whistle works. **What you cannot
substitute:** teaching every adult who might feed an allergic child.

## When it runs out

- **Teaching does not run out; it compounds.** Everything a child learns now they keep and build
  on.
- **What fades is memory and fit.** A drill practised once is forgotten. **Re-teach and re-drill
  every year** (→ 25-10), and as each younger child comes into range.
- **The failure:** teaching once, feeling done, and finding in a real event that the child does
  not remember. Schedule the re-drills.
- **Confidence compounds fastest.** A child who has done the shelter drill six times goes to
  shelter calmly instead of freezing, which frees adult time and attention exactly when both are
  scarce.

## By phase

- **A (shelter in place):** teach and drill now, while it can be play.
- **B (moving):** the training pays off. Before setting out, re-brief: where we are going, what
  to do if separated, the car-seat rule (→ 01-03, → 15-03).
- **C (establish):** new hazards, new rules (→ 20-02). Re-teach everything at the new site on day
  one, walk the boundaries together, and hand out new jobs (→ 20-07).
