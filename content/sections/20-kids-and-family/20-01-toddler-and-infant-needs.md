# Babies, toddlers and young children: what they need

**What this page is for.** The youngest people in a house have the smallest reserves of
everything. They dry out fast, lose and gain heat fast, and cannot tell you what is wrong. This
page lists what they need, what cannot be improvised, and how to watch a child who cannot
speak for themself. It is written in age bands. Your household's bands are listed first; the
others stay on the page for visiting children, grandchildren and children you may take in.

> **SAFETY.** Small children dehydrate fast (→ 12-05). **No honey for a child under 1 year**
> (botulism; CDC, Botulism Prevention). **No loperamide (an anti-diarrhoea medicine) under age
> 2** (MedlinePlus, Loperamide); WHO says anti-diarrhoea drugs of this kind should not be given
> to infants or children with diarrhoea at all (WHO, The Treatment of Diarrhoea). **Children do not have access to firearms,
> fuel, medications or chlorine products** (floor, → 30-01): small children explore by mouth and
> by hand, and in a crisis the adults are busier.

## Do this now

1. **Ask your child's doctor now, while you can:** "If we cannot buy formula, milk, diapers or
   this child's medicine for a month, what do we do?" Write the answers on → 20-05 and → 25-09.
2. **Stock the things you cannot improvise:** the right milk for each child's age (→ 20-05),
   diapers (→ 13-08), oral rehydration salts (ORS, → 12-05), any child's medicine, and the
   foods each child reliably eats.
3. **Buy the next two clothing and shoe sizes** for each child (→ 11-02). Growth is the one
   shortage nothing prevents.
4. **Write each child's feeding and medical details on a card** for any caregiver (→ 15-03,
   → 25-09), including every allergy.
5. **Work out on paper how the kitchen runs with no fridge and rationed water** (→ 13-10,
   → 04-17), before you have to.

## Age bands

### Infant (under 12 months)

- **Milk is the whole diet for the first months:** breast milk or infant formula, nothing
  else (CDC). Formula is never diluted and never homemade (→ 20-05).
- **Solid foods start around 6 months** alongside milk, soft and mashed (CDC, Foods and Drinks
  for 6 to 24 Month Olds).
- **Safe water** for anything that goes in a bottle or cup (→ 03-04). Babies are the least able
  to survive a water-borne illness.
- **No honey under 1 year** (botulism). Honey appears in this binder as a wound dressing and a
  cough remedy (→ 12-02, → 12-13); the age rule matters more than the memory.
- **Sleep:** the American Academy of Pediatrics says a baby sleeps in the parents' room on a
  separate firm, flat surface, and that parents avoid bed-sharing with a baby (AAP, How to Keep
  Your Sleeping Baby Safe). Sharing an adult bed for warmth is a trade-off that the card
  only offers for children over 12 months (→ 30-10). {{choice DC-09}} → 30-10.
- **Watch:** fewer wet diapers plus unusual sleepiness is an emergency (→ 12-05).

### Toddler (1 to 3)

- **Around 12 months a child moves to mostly family foods.** That is a real advantage in a
  crisis: the child increasingly eats what everyone eats.
- **Milk for 12 to 23 months is whole milk** (CDC: whole cow's milk from 12 months, not
  before), about 16 ounces (2 cups) a day (AAP, Recommended Drinks for Children Age 5 and
  Younger), not the nonfat dry milk that most pantries stock. Nonfat or low-fat milk from age 2
  (AAP). Stock and substitutes: → 20-05, → 04-14.
- **Soft, mashable, small.** Choking is the hazard at this age (list below).
- **Mobile, curious, mouths everything.** This is the band at highest risk on almost every
  hazard in → 20-02: buckets of water, heaters, fuel, button batteries.
- **Sleep:** if the household is considering bed-sharing for warmth, the conditions are on the
  card (over 12 months, firm surface, no heavy covers over the child, sober adults).
  {{choice DC-09}} → 30-10.

### Preschool (3 to 5)

- **Eats family food**, still on the choking list until at least 4 (AAP).
- **Can learn rules and a few jobs** (→ 20-04, → 20-07): "I only eat food a grown-up gives
  me", the fire line, the water rule.
- **Still cannot report illness well.** A change in behaviour is often the first sign.

### School-age (6 to 12) and teen (13 to 17)

Older children need the same never-rationed food and safe water, and can do much more for
themselves. Their pages are → 20-03 (routine and schooling), → 20-04 (teaching the plan),
→ 20-07 (jobs) and → 20-08 (fear). Teens eat as much as adults or more (→ 04-01).

## Choking: the foods to change for children under 4

The American Academy of Pediatrics lists these foods to avoid or change for young children
until 4 years of age or older, depending on the child (AAP, Choking Prevention), printed so this
page works alone:

- Hot dogs; meat sticks or sausages.
- Hard, gooey or sticky candy; chewing gum.
- Nuts and seeds (especially sunflower seeds).
- Whole grapes.
- Raw vegetables, such as carrot sticks; raw fruit chunks, such as apple pieces.
- Popcorn; marshmallows.
- Thick chunks of peanut butter or other nut butters: **spread thinly instead.**
- Chunks of meat; chunks of cheese or string cheese.

Round, firm foods such as hot dogs or grapes can be served if **completely chopped into tiny
pieces**. The AAP advises cutting food for infants and young children into pieces **no larger
than one-half inch**, having children sit down to eat, and supervising every meal. It also lists
household choking hazards: balloons (a risk up to age 8), coins, marbles, small balls, toys
with small parts, pen caps and **button or coin batteries**, which also burn if swallowed
(→ 20-02). **What to do if a child chokes, and infant and
child CPR, is → 12-18.** Read it before you need it.

## Food rules in a crisis

- **Variety matters.** A stored-food diet is narrow (→ 04-18). Fats, protein and vitamins need
  deliberate attention for growing children.
- **Safe water** for drinking and for mixing any milk powder (→ 03-04, CDC).
- **New foods and allergies.** Introducing a common allergen (peanut, egg and others) to a baby
  or toddler is something to plan with the child's doctor while one is reachable, especially if
  a brother or sister has a food allergy (FDA, Food Allergies).
- **Traded or scavenged food.** Food from another kitchen carries unknown ingredients and
  unknown handling. Home-canned low-acid food from someone else follows the questionable food
  card (→ 30-15) and → 18-02.

### Two food chains in one kitchen (when one person has a food allergy)

1. **Cook one base meal that is safe for the allergic person**, then adapt it for the youngest
   (mash, soften, portion). Making the whole household's food allergen-safe by default is the
   simplest system there is.
2. **Feed a toddler away from any food containing the allergen.** A toddler's hands on a shared
   surface carry it everywhere.
3. **Wash hands and surfaces between**, with soap (→ 13-02), even when water is short.
4. **Label everything.** In a tired household, labels prevent the mistake.

## Sleep and warmth

- **The heated room** (→ 08-01) is where everyone sleeps in a cold house: warmer and safer than a
  cold bedroom.
- **A cold house is answered with warm sleepwear and a wearable blanket (sleep sack), not loose
  blankets** on a baby (AAP; → 11-02). Check for overheating: sweating, a hot chest, flushed
  skin (AAP).
- **Hot-water bottles and warmed stones pre-warm the bed and come out before a child gets in.**
  A sleeping child can be burned by one without waking (→ 12-03, → 21-07).
- **A heater burning while people sleep** is a separate trade-off on its own card
  (→ 30-02). {{choice DC-01}} → 30-02.
- **Routine matters enormously** for children and for the adults' sanity (→ 21-04, → 21-07).

## Watching a child who cannot tell you

**Check the body, not the answer** (→ 11-02, → 12-05):

- **Dehydration:** fewer wet diapers, no tears, dry mouth, sunken eyes, floppy or unusually
  sleepy, refusing to drink. **In a baby, unusual sleepiness plus fewer wet diapers is an
  emergency.**
- **Too cold:** cold belly and back of the neck (not hands), unusual sleepiness, weak cry, pale
  or bright red skin (→ 11-02).
- **Too hot:** hot skin, flushing, floppiness, fewer wet diapers (→ 11-08).
- **Illness:** fever, a change in feeding, a change in behaviour. **A change in behaviour is
  often the first sign.**
- **Allergic reaction:** watch any child eating a food for the first time (→ 02-03).

**If a child under 2 has diarrhoea** (WHO, The Treatment of Diarrhoea, as printed on → 12-05):
give **50 to 100 mL (a quarter to half a cup) of ORS after each loose stool**, in sips from a
spoon, on top of normal feeding. Breast milk continues throughout. A child already showing
dehydration signs needs more, as set out on → 12-05. **No loperamide under 2** (MedlinePlus).

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- **Food:** mashed, softened versions of the household's food. This is the direction feeding
  goes anyway after 12 months (CDC): cooked grains, mashed beans, soft cooked vegetables, soft
  fruit.
- **Diapers:** cloth from flannel, towels or sheets (→ 13-08).
- **Carrier:** a long, strong cloth tied as a wrap.
- **Clothing:** cut down from adult clothing (→ 11-02, → 11-07).
- **Comfort:** a familiar cloth, a made toy, a routine. Predictability is itself a comfort.
- **What you cannot substitute:** infant formula (→ 20-05), and a doctor's advice on
  introducing allergens. Ask now.

## When it runs out

- **Milk, diapers and clothing sizes run out on a schedule you can predict.** Put all three on
  the consumables table with dates (→ 23-02). They are the most predictable depletion clocks in
  the binder; use that.
- **Ration everything else, never a child's food or fluid**, and never a nursing parent's.
- **Substitute:** family food mashed; cloth diapers; cut-down clothing; and the community.
  Children's supplies are the easiest barter category there is (→ 18-02): everyone has outgrown
  gear and everyone needs the next size.
- **This problem eases with time.** A 1-year-old becomes a 2-year-old, needs less special
  supply and eats more of what everyone eats. That is unusual in this binder and worth knowing.

## By phase

- **A (shelter in place):** stock deeply, set up the kitchen routine and the safety barriers
  (→ 20-02). Use the time while stores work to buy ahead on sizes and supplies.
- **B (moving):** small children are carried, which shapes everything. An adult carrying a child
  makes heat; the child does not (→ 11-02). Disposables, not cloth. Breast milk or ready-to-feed
  formula, ORS and safe water are the critical load. In the car each child is in a seat, harness
  snug, no puffy coat under it, blanket over the top (AAP; → 11-02, → 22-05).
- **C (establish):** more room and more hazards (→ 20-02). Food improves as a garden and animals
  come in (→ 05-01, → 05-04).
