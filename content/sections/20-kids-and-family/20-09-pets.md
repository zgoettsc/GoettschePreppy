# Pets: food, water, medicine, moving, and hard choices

**What this page is for.** Each pet is another mouth, another water need, a waste problem and,
if you ever have to move, an animal that must be contained and carried. Planned now, pets are a
comfort in a hard time. Unplanned, they become an improvised problem at the worst moment. This
page covers dogs, cats and other house pets: food and water arithmetic, medicines, carriers,
moving, and what to do if you cannot keep them (decision card DC-10, → 30-11).

> **SAFETY.**
> - **Antifreeze kills pets** and tastes sweet to them (ASPCA, Ethylene Glycol Poisoning in
>   Pets). Store vehicle fluids sealed and out of reach; wipe up drips (→ 06-06).
> - **Never give a pet a human medicine, or one animal's medicine to another species**, unless a
>   veterinarian said to. Many are toxic, especially to cats.
> - **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products** (floor,
>   → 30-01). That includes pet medicines and any firearm used on this page.
> - **Children never watch a pet's death.** → 20-08, → 21-03.

## Do this now

1. **Stock pet food the way you stock human food:** deep, rotated, oldest first, in sealed
   rodent-proof containers.
2. **Count pets in the water plan** (→ 03-01). {{#if has.pets}}Your household's daily water floor,
   pets included, is **{{ derived.water.floor_gal_day }} gallons**.{{/if}}
3. **Refill every pet prescription** and ask the vet for a buffer supply; keep a written list of
   what each animal takes (FEMA/Ready.gov).
4. **One carrier per cat or small animal, and a leash and harness per dog**, stored where you can
   reach them in a hurry, not buried in a closet.
5. **ID:** collar tags and registered microchips, with an out-of-area contact on the
   registration (FEMA/Ready.gov). Take a photo of each pet with a family member. {{photo pet-id-photo}}
6. **Decide now, calmly, as adults, what you would do if you could not keep them** (→ 30-11), and
   ask your vet the questions at the end of this page while you can.

## Food

- **Dry food** stores longest and simplest. **Canned food carries water**, a real advantage when
  water is short. A mix is sensible.
- **Rotate.** The fats in pet food go rancid. Store cool, dark and sealed, in a bucket with a
  gasket lid or the original bag inside a tote (→ 04-11).
- **Sudden diet changes upset stomachs**, and old animals most of all. Stock the food they eat now.
- **Cats are obligate carnivores:** they cannot live on grain and vegetable scraps. If food runs
  short, protein is what matters: plain cooked meat and organ meat, not bread or rice.
- **Dogs** eat a wider diet, but plain cooked meat, fish, eggs and cooked grains are safer stretch
  foods than seasoned table scraps.

**Foods never to give pets** (ASPCA, People Foods to Avoid Feeding Your Pets): alcohol; yeast
dough; chocolate, coffee and caffeine; avocado; citrus; grapes and raisins; **onions, garlic and
chives**; milk and dairy; macadamia and other nuts; raw or undercooked meat, raw eggs and raw
bones; **xylitol** (a sweetener in sugar-free gum and some peanut butters); salt and salty foods.

{{#if has.anaphylaxis}}**A severe food allergy in the house:** pet bowls live on the floor where
small children live. Pet foods and scraps that contain a household allergen (fish, egg, peanut
and others) are handled under the food-allergy module's separate-cookware rules: their own bowl
and pan, hands washed, pets fed away from the allergic person (→ 02-04).{{/if}}

**Food arithmetic, per animal:** days of food on hand = food stored ÷ food per day. Write it on the
consumables table (→ 23-02).

## Water

- **Pets drink treated water**, the same as people. Raw pool, pond or puddle water can make an
  animal sick, and there may be no vet (→ 03-01, → 03-04).
- **The binder's water plan adds a quarter-gallon a day for each pet** (→ 03-01). CDC advises
  storing extra water for pets beyond the household's own (CDC, How to Create and Store an
  Emergency Water Supply). **A large dog drinks far more:** measure your own animal. Fill the bowl
  from a measured jug for a few ordinary days and see what is gone. Mine: ______ cups a day.
- **More in heat, more on dry food, more when nursing or ill.**
- **Older cats and any animal with kidney disease need more water, not less, when water is
  rationed**, and dry-food-only speeds dehydration. Feed canned food when water is tight, and never
  ration a kidney-compromised animal's water below its normal intake.

## Medicines and health

- **List every pet medicine** (the log below) with the vet's instructions as written. This binder
  gives no animal doses.
- **Ask the vet for a refill buffer** while vets are open, and how long each medicine keeps.
- **Keep pets healthy now:** vaccines, weight, teeth, parasite control. A healthy animal needs less
  later.
- **Basic pet first aid:** gauze, self-adhering wrap, nail clippers, tweezers, a brush.
- **Signs that need help now:** not eating or drinking for a day or more (sooner for a cat or a
  small animal), straining to urinate (above all a male cat), repeated vomiting, collapse.

## Litter and waste

- **Litter runs out fast.** Substitutes: sand, dry dirt, sawdust or wood pellets (pellets work
  well), shredded paper, dried peat, wood ash used sparingly (dusty).
- **Cat and dog waste goes with human waste** (→ 13-01), never into a garden or near food or water.
- **Toxoplasmosis** spreads from cat feces. Keep litter away from food preparation and wash hands
  after handling it (CDC, Preventing Toxoplasmosis). {{#if has.pregnant}}**Someone in your household
  is pregnant:** CDC advises a pregnant person avoid changing cat litter; if she must, gloves and
  handwashing, and change it daily.{{else}}CDC advises a pregnant person avoid changing cat litter.{{/if}}
- **A cat finds a corner** if the box is unusable. Keep it maintained.

## Keeping them safe and quiet

- **Keep pets indoors or contained.** Loose pets in a crisis get lost, injured or killed, and they
  advertise that a house is occupied and supplied (→ 14-03).
- **Cold:** pets seek the heated room, which is fine. Keep them away from open flame, stoves,
  heaters and candles; a curious cat and an unattended flame is a house fire.
- **Noise:** a barking dog or yowling cat carries. Calm and contained matters for security.
- **Stressed animals bite.** Keep dogs and children apart when either is frightened, tired or
  eating (→ 20-02).
- **Other pets:** birds, reptiles, fish and small mammals often depend on power: heat lamps,
  aquarium pumps and filters. Plan how you would keep them warm or aerated without power, and how
  you would move them, or plan to rehome them early.
- **Radiation or fallout emergencies:** bring pets inside with you; if they were outside, wiping or
  washing their fur removes fallout dust (CDC, Pets and Radiation Emergencies; → 27-05).
- **In a storm alarm, nobody chases a pet** (→ 20-04). Carriers can wait at the shelter spot.

## If you have to move

- **FEMA's rule while help exists: if local officials ask you to evacuate, your pets evacuate too.**
  Many public shelters and hotels do not allow pets, so plan a pet-friendly place in advance
  (FEMA/Ready.gov). Write it here: ______________________.
- **Carriers, one per cat or small animal**, and leash and harness for each dog, by the door.
  Catching frightened animals at departure is a delay you can prevent.
- **Pet kit, alongside the human go-bags** (FEMA/Ready.gov): several days of food in a waterproof
  container, several days of water and a bowl, medicine, first aid, collar with ID, a backup leash,
  carrier, sanitation supplies (litter and tray, bags), copies of records in a waterproof bag, a
  photo of you with the pet, and familiar bedding or a toy (→ 22-03). {{photo pet-kit}}
- **Cold weather:** cover and insulate carriers; an animal in a carrier in a cold vehicle chills
  fast.
- **Be honest in the plan.** If leaving on foot, or with no room, is ever the only option, pets are
  a real constraint. That is exactly what DC-10 is for.

## When you cannot keep them (DC-10)

A long emergency may leave too little food or water for people and pets, or force a move the pets
cannot make. FEMA's advice is to plan to take them; when that is impossible the choices are
hard: keep them and share scarce food and water; release them, knowing most house pets do not
survive long alone; or end their lives humanely. The full card, with what each option costs, is
→ 30-11.

**If releasing:** leave as much dry food and water as can be spared, a way in and out of shelter,
and a collar with an ID tag; release near people who may take them in (→ 30-11).

## A humane end: the method section for DC-10

Read this once now, calmly, so it is never read for the first time in a crisis.

> **A veterinarian is the right choice whenever one exists.** If any vet, clinic, shelter or
> animal control officer can be reached, even far away and even by trading, that is the answer.

> **Before the collapse:** state and local laws govern how an animal may be killed and where a
> firearm may be fired. While government works, use a veterinarian or animal control.

**What the AVMA says** (American Veterinary Medical Association, Guidelines for the Euthanasia of
Animals, 2020 edition; the full document is bound behind this page in the Large download):

- **The preferred method for dogs, cats and other small pets is an injection of a barbiturate
  drug, given by or under a veterinarian.** Federal drug law restricts these drugs to people
  registered with the Drug Enforcement Administration. A vet can sedate the animal first so it is
  calm, and the family can be present.
- **For dogs and cats, gunshot is "acceptable with conditions".** The AVMA says it should only be
  done **by highly skilled people trained in the use of firearms** (it names animal control and law
  enforcement officers and trained veterinarians), only where firearm use is legal, and that it
  **may be appropriate in remote areas or emergencies where withholding death would leave the animal
  in prolonged, unrelieved pain and suffering, or where human life is in imminent danger.** It is
  not for routine use and not when other methods are available.
- **For cats, the AVMA recommends sedation first whenever possible** (for example, medicine added to
  food), because cats can be difficult to shoot humanely. That medicine comes from a vet: ask
  now.
- **Unacceptable**, by the AVMA's list: **drowning; cold (hypothermia); household chemicals,
  disinfectants, cleaning agents and pesticides;** and injecting drugs by routes or into awake
  animals outside the methods it describes. Do not improvise with human medicines either: a failed
  attempt causes great suffering.
- **Other pets** (rabbits, birds, rodents, reptiles, fish): the AVMA gives each species its own
  methods, which differ from those for dogs and cats. This binder does not print them. Ask your vet
  now, and write the answer below.

**If the household has chosen this option, has no vet, and someone with real firearm skill is
available** (if no one in the household has that skill, ask someone who does before attempting:
a law officer, animal control officer or experienced hunter):

1. **Decide in advance who.** The calmest adult with firearm skill. **Children elsewhere, out of
   sight and hearing** (→ 30-11).
2. **Firearm safety** (AVMA, as published; → 29-11): treat every firearm as loaded; know where the
   muzzle points and never let it point at yourself or anyone; finger off the trigger until ready;
   be sure of the target and what lies beyond it; unload when not in use.
3. **Outdoors, in a place no one else can enter**, with a safe backstop of earth behind and below
   (AVMA: outdoors, in an area where public access is restricted).
4. **Calm the animal.** The AVMA says animals used to people should be handled calmly and
   reassuringly beforehand. A favourite food, a familiar voice, a quiet place.
5. **Placement** (AVMA Figures 8 and 9, after the Canadian Veterinary Journal firearm guidelines):
   {{figure fig-avma-gunshot-sites-dog-cat}}
   - **Dog:** midway between the level of the eyes and the base of the ears, slightly off the
     midline, with the aim directed across the dog toward the spine.
   - **Cat:** a point slightly below a line drawn between the inner bases of the ears, or where
     lines from the outer corner of each eye to the base of the opposite ear cross.
6. **Never hold the muzzle against the animal.** The AVMA warns the barrel can burst. Its general
   guidance is to hold the muzzle 1 to 2 feet from the head, at a right angle to the skull.
7. **Confirm death before anything else** (AVMA): no heartbeat or pulse, no breathing, no blink
   when the surface of the eye is touched, no response to a firm toe pinch, and greying gums. No
   single sign except stiffening of the body (rigor mortis) confirms death on its own. **If any
   sign of life remains, act again at once.** The AVMA says that if one method is proving
   difficult, another should be tried immediately.
8. **Burial.** Bury deeply, away from water and wells (→ 13-07). The AVMA notes that remains can
   carry residues (lead from bullets; drugs from a vet's injection) that poison scavengers and
   pets; if the ground is frozen, cover and store the remains securely until deep burial is
   possible.
9. **Afterwards:** tell the children the truth simply (→ 20-08), mark the loss (→ 21-06), and
   expect grief, including in the adult who did it (→ 21-03).

**Scavenge or substitute:**
- **Litter:** sand, dirt, sawdust, wood pellets, shredded paper.
- **Food:** plain cooked meat and organ meat; fish and game scraps from hunting and fishing
  (→ 05-07, → 05-08), prepared without onion, garlic or salt. Not grain-based human food as a cat's
  staple.
- **Carrier:** a sturdy tote with air holes and a secured lid, as a last resort.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** days of pet food on hand, on the consumables table (→ 23-02).
- **Ration:** animals tolerate a modest cut in portion better than a sudden change of food, but cats
  cannot go without protein. Stretch with cooked meat scraps rather than starch.
- **Resupply:** hunting, trapping and fishing scraps (→ 05-07, → 05-08, → 05-15) are real pet food.
- **The honest note:** in a very long event, feeding pets is a real cost. Decide the household's
  position in advance (→ 30-11), not in a moment of scarcity.

## By phase

- **A (shelter in place):** the easiest phase: stock, keep them in, manage litter and noise.
- **B (moving):** carriers, warmth, water and a plan. This is where pets are hardest, and where
  DC-10 matters most.
- **C (establish):** cats earn their keep as rodent control around stored food (→ 04-11). Keep new
  arrivals contained until settled, or they wander off.
