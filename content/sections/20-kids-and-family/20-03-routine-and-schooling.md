# Children's routine and schooling

**What this page is for.** Routine is not a comfort item. It is one of the most protective
things you can give children in a crisis, and one of the cheapest. A predictable day tells a
child that the adults have things in hand, and that calms fear better than reassurance does
(→ 20-08, → 21-04). It protects the adults too: a household with a shape to its day decides
better, sleeps better and fights less (→ 21-02, → 21-05). This page gives a daily shape and
what "school" looks like at each age when there is no school.

## Do this now

1. **Buy paper, pencils, crayons and books**, a large box of them. They are cheap, they do not
   expire, and in a long confinement they matter enormously.
2. **Buy physical learning material for the coming years** of each child: alphabet and number
   sets and early readers for the young ones, workbooks and reference books for older ones,
   books each child will want again and again.
3. **Write a rough daily shape** (below) and try it for a week now. You will find what does not
   work while it is still easy to change.
4. **Protect the two routines that matter most: bedtime (→ 21-07) and toothbrushing (→ 12-06).**
   They quietly lapse and cost the most.

## Why routine protects children

CDC's child development guidance describes young children as thriving on predictability
(CDC, Positive Parenting Tips). In a crisis:
- **A known day lowers anxiety:** the child does not have to wonder what is happening.
- **It saves adult capacity:** a day with a shape needs fewer decisions.
- **It marks time.** Days without structure blur together, which wears down everyone (→ 21-02).
- **It gives a sense of progress**, not just endurance.
- **It survives the loss of everything else.** Routine costs nothing.

## A workable daily shape

Adapt it. The point is that there **is** one.

| Block | What | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Wake with the light** | Up when it is light (→ 10-03) | In winter that is late, and that is fine |
| **Morning: chores and work** | Water, wood, fire, animals, cooking. **Children have jobs** (→ 20-07) | Daylight is for work |
| **Learning block** | At a table, at the best window (length by age, below) | Short and every day beats long and now and then |
| **Midday: main meal** | The big cooking event (→ 07-01) | Everyone together |
| **Afternoon: outdoor time** | Even in winter, especially in winter, when safe | → 11-02, → 12-10 |
| **Late afternoon: projects, mending, prep** | Adults work; children play or help | Before the light goes |
| **Evening: together in the warm room** | Light on, everyone together (→ 08-01, → 10-03) | The social block |
| **Bedtime routine** | **Wash, teeth, story, bed. Same order every night** | **Defend this one** (→ 21-07, → 12-06) |

**The two anchors are morning and bedtime.** If everything else falls apart, keep those.

## Learning, by age

### Infant and toddler (under 3)

They learn by play, talk and being read to. **Talk to them constantly**: hearing language is the
main thing at this age. Name things, sing, read the same books again and again.

### Preschool (3 to 5): keep it modest

At this age "school" is mostly play, language and habits. You do not need a curriculum; you need
consistency and materials.
- **20 to 30 minutes a day is plenty**, and consistency beats length.
- **Letters, numbers, drawing, counting things, and being read to.** Reading aloud is the single
  most valuable learning activity at this age.
- **Everything is a lesson:** counting firewood, measuring water, sorting seeds (→ 05-03),
  reading a thermometer, watching plants grow.
- **Practical skills:** knots (→ 16-03), sewing with a blunt needle (→ 16-04), helping cook,
  feeding animals (→ 20-07). Allergy rules apply in the kitchen (→ 02-04).
- **Physical play matters.** A small child kept indoors all winter with no way to move is hard
  to live with.

### School-age (6 to 12)

- **Reading, writing and arithmetic every day**, in a short block (an hour or so, split up, is
  realistic). Use whatever books and workbooks you have; older children can read to younger
  ones.
- **The household is the curriculum:** measuring and doubling recipes (→ 25-02), weather records
  (→ 17-10), the garden calendar (→ 25-01), simple repairs (→ 16-05), map reading (→ 25-04).
- **Keep a journal** of the event: writing practice now, family history later (→ 25-05).
- **Real skills with real tools come with training and the household's own rule** (→ 30-25).

### Teen (13 to 17)

- **Teens can run their own learning** with books and a weekly check-in. Agree goals with them.
- **Give real responsibility and real skill:** first aid (→ 12-01), radio (→ 15-02), cooking for
  the household, gardening, tool work under the household's job rules (→ 30-25).
- **Teaching younger children** is good for both (→ 18-07).
- **Teens lose the most socially** in a long event: friends, school, plans. Name that loss
  (→ 21-03) and protect some time and privacy for them.

## The long-confinement problem

- **Somewhere to move indoors.** Children need to move. Set aside a space and let them.
- **Get outside every day it is safe** (→ 11-02; limits → 12-10). Even 20 minutes changes a day.
  Cold is manageable with the right clothing; darkness and confinement are the real problem.
- **Vary the days:** a special meal, a project, a game, a new job (→ 21-06).
- **Light for the evening block** (→ 10-03). A lit, warm, gathered evening is worth spending
  scarce light on.
- **Stories, games and music:** cards, dice, drawing, singing, reading aloud. Stock them now.
- **Accept that some days are bad**, for children and adults (→ 21-02).

## Routines that protect health

These lapse silently in a hard stretch and cost real damage:
- **Toothbrushing twice a day** with fluoride toothpaste (CDC, Oral Health). A stored-food diet is
  high in starches and sugars and there may be no dentist (→ 12-06).
- **Handwashing at the key moments** (CDC, About Hygiene; → 13-02): it is how outbreaks are
  prevented (→ 13-11).
- **Bedtime and sleep** (→ 21-07).
- **Meals at regular times**, which also matters for anyone with diabetes (→ 02-01, → 02-05).
- **Getting dressed each morning**, even when nobody is going anywhere. It marks the day as a day.

## The adults' side

- **Assign the routine; do not just hope for it** (→ 00-07): who does the morning fire, who does
  the learning block, who does bedtime.
- **Trade off.** Adults with small children in one room for months need breaks from each other.
  Put them on the schedule (→ 21-05).
- **Protect some adult time** in the evening after the children are down.
- **Make one day a week different** (→ 21-06). A week identical to the last is how time
  collapses.

## The children's box

When the household moves, one labelled tote is the children's box, claimed now so it is not
argued over at load time (→ 22-05, → 01-06).
- **Contents:** a rotating dozen books, paper, pencils and crayons, small games (cards, dice,
  dominoes), and **duplicates of the important comfort objects** (→ 20-08).
- **Packed light enough that a child can help carry it**, with the contents list taped inside the
  lid.
- **It is not optional weight.** On the move this tote is the routine: the bedtime story and the
  learning block at a rest stop come out of it.

**Scavenge or substitute:**
- **Paper:** the backs of anything, cardboard, brown paper. Charcoal from the fire draws well
  (→ 06-01).
- **Books:** libraries, free boxes, neighbours. Book swapping is a natural community activity
  (→ 18-01, → 18-07).
- **Games:** cards, dice and pebbles cover a huge number of games. Learn a few good ones now.
- **Toys:** made from wood, cloth and string (→ 16-03, → 16-04). A small child plays with almost
  anything if an adult is engaged.
- **Curriculum:** the household itself: cooking, counting, measuring, weather, plants, animals,
  tools.
- **What you cannot substitute:** adult attention. That is the real input, and the scarcest one
  in a hard stretch, which is why the routine matters.

## When it runs out

- **Nothing here runs out fast.** Paper, pencils and books last, and routine costs nothing.
- **What wears away is the routine itself**, months in, when everyone is tired. The defence is
  having it written down and assigned (→ 00-07, → 21-04) rather than relying on willpower.
- **Ration light** (→ 10-03) **to the evening block and bedtime**; do the learning block by
  daylight at the window.
- **Substitute:** spoken learning (stories, songs, counting, memory games) needs no materials.
  Learn some stories.
- **The return:** a child who spends a crisis year learning, helping and feeling secure comes out
  of it better off, not worse. That depends far more on routine and adult attention than on
  supplies.

## By phase

- **A (shelter in place):** set the routine in the first week. A household that drifts for a
  month rarely recovers the structure.
- **B (moving):** routine collapses on the move and that is expected. Keep two things:
  **bedtime and food times.** A story and a steady bedtime help a young child enormously, even in
  a vehicle (→ 20-08); the children's box makes that possible.
- **C (establish):** more real work and real learning: animals, garden, building, weather. A rural
  household is a rich place for a child to learn (→ 20-07). Re-set the routine in the first week
  at the new site.
