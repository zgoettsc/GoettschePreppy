# Children and fear

**What this page is for.** Children know when something is wrong. They read adults with great
accuracy, and **being told nothing is more frightening than being told something**, because a
child fills the gap with something worse than the truth. This page covers what to say, what
distress looks like at each age, what helps, and when it is more than ordinary fear.

**The biggest single factor in how children come through a crisis is how the adults around them
are doing** (→ 21-01, → 21-02). That is the uncomfortable part of this page and the most useful.
Looking after your own state is child care.

> **If phones work:** the **988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988)** and SAMHSA's
> **Disaster Distress Helpline (1-800-985-5990, call or text)** are there for adults and older
> children alike. Write both on the wall (→ 12-15, → 25-03). For a medical or mental-health
> emergency in anyone, the first-response page is → 12-15.

## Do this now

1. **Agree between the adults what you will tell the children**, and use the same words. Children
   notice when adults disagree, and it worries them.
2. **Practise the honest, brief, reassuring version:** "Something happened with the power (the
   weather). We have a plan. Our job is X. You're safe with us."
3. **Protect the routine** (→ 20-03, → 21-04). It is the single most protective thing you can do
   for a frightened child, and it costs nothing.
4. **Cut children's exposure to disaster news and adult conversation** (→ 15-04). Small children
   absorb far more than people expect and have no way to make sense of it.

## What to tell them

**Honest, at their level, brief, reassuring.** In that order (CDC, Helping Children Cope During
and After a Disaster).

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| **Answer the question actually asked**, at their level | Volunteer more than they asked |
| **Say "I don't know" when you don't**, plus "and here's what we're doing" | Promise what you cannot keep ("everything will be fine") |
| **Name the plan:** "We're staying here, we have food and heat, our job is to keep the fire going" | Describe worst cases |
| **Give them a role** (→ 20-07) | Make them responsible for adult worries |
| **Name feelings:** "It is scary. It's okay to feel scared" | Dismiss them ("don't be silly") |
| **Be calm in front of them** | Have the frightened conversations where they can hear |
| **Repeat.** They will ask the same thing many times | Get impatient; repetition is how they process |

**For a small child, short is best:** "The power is out because of the storm. We have wood and
food and we're all together. It's going to be a bit different for a while. Your job is to help
with the kindling." Add more only if they ask.

**For older children and teens, more truth, and a voice:** tell them what is known, what is not,
and what the plan is; ask what they think; give them real work (→ 20-07) and a say in things that
affect them. Teens resent being managed and handle facts better than secrets.

### Why false reassurance backfires

- If it turns out not to be fine, the child learns you cannot be trusted on exactly the questions
  that matter most.
- Children can tell when adults are pretending, and the gap between your words and your face is
  itself frightening.
- "We have a plan and we're together" is both true and reassuring, and it survives things getting
  worse.

## What distress looks like, by age

### Infant and toddler (under 3)

- Clinginess, changed sleep, changed eating, more crying, harder to soothe.
- **They read the adults' feelings directly** and have no way to process them except through the
  adults.
- The help is the same at every level of the crisis: **calm adults, physical contact, routine.**

### Preschool (3 to 5)

Distress shows in **behaviour**, not words (CDC):
- **Going backwards**: toileting accidents, baby talk, wanting to be carried, thumb sucking.
  Very common, and **not a problem to correct.** Treat it as unremarkable and it usually passes.
- Clinginess and fear of separation.
- Sleep problems: trouble falling asleep, waking, nightmares (→ 21-07).
- New fears: the dark, noises, being alone, weather.
- More and bigger tantrums.
- Changes in eating.
- **Repetitive play about the event.** Normal: it is how children process. Do not stop it.
- Tummy aches and headaches with no medical cause.
- **Unusual quietness**, which is easy to miss because it is convenient.

### School-age (6 to 12)

- Worry about safety of family and pets; asking the same questions over and over.
- Trouble concentrating; falling behind in learning.
- Irritability, fights, withdrawal from play.
- Physical complaints; sleep trouble.
- Guilt ("it happened because I...") and magical thinking. Correct it gently and plainly.

### Teen (13 to 17)

- Withdrawal, irritability, risk-taking, defiance.
- Grief for lost friends, school and plans, often unspoken (→ 21-03).
- Sleeping too much or too little; changes in eating.
- Alcohol or drug use if any is within reach.
- **Hopelessness or talk of self-harm** (CDC, Risk and Protective Factors for Suicide): take it
  seriously at once (→ 12-15).

## What actually helps

1. **Routine, above everything** (→ 20-03, → 21-04). Predictability tells a child the adults have
   things in hand more convincingly than words.
2. **Physical closeness:** holding, carrying, sleeping near. For a baby or toddler this is the
   whole intervention.
3. **Calm adults.** The hardest and the most effective. Manage your own state (→ 21-01), have
   frightened conversations out of earshot, and take turns being the steady one (→ 21-05).
4. **A role** (→ 20-07). Contribution protects against helplessness.
5. **Play.** A young child should mostly still be a young child. Protect play time; it is how
   children recover.
6. **Honest, brief answers**, repeated as often as needed.
7. **Limit exposure** to news (→ 15-04), adult conversations and frightening details. Children do
   not need to know the supply arithmetic.
8. **Name feelings:** "You're scared. That makes sense. I'm here."
9. **Something to look forward to** (→ 21-06): a special meal, a story, a small celebration.
10. **Sleep** (→ 21-07). A tired child is a fragile child.
11. **For older children and teens: friends and other trusted adults** (→ 18-01). Any safe contact
    with peers helps.

## What to avoid

- **Adult conversations within earshot.** They hear everything, and understand less than you
  think while absorbing more than you think.
- **Using a child as an emotional support.** Even a teen is not your counsellor.
- **Punishing regression or fear-driven behaviour.** It makes it worse and damages trust.
- **Threats framed around danger.** "If you go near the pool you'll drown" makes anxiety and
  worse compliance than "we always hold a grown-up's hand near water" (→ 20-04).
- **Letting a child carry the household's medical worry.** A parent's medicine supply or a
  sibling's allergy is not a child's burden, and they cannot fix it.
- **Assuming a child is fine because they are not saying anything.**

## When it is more than ordinary fear

Get professional help if any exists, and use the lines above if phones work:
- Distress that is **severe, lasting, or getting worse over weeks**.
- **Complete withdrawal**, or loss of interest in play or friends.
- **Regression that does not ease** as things settle.
- **Any talk of self-harm, at any age.** Take it seriously (→ 12-15).
- **New and marked aggression.**
- **Sleep trouble that does not improve** with routine.
- **Anything that frightens you as a parent.** That instinct is worth acting on.

**Check the body first** (→ 12-15): **dehydration, infection, fever, pain, hunger, cold, carbon
monoxide, and low blood sugar in a child with diabetes** all change behaviour. Rule out the body
before deciding it is the mind.

## Death and loss

If someone dies, a pet dies, or the house is lost (→ 21-03):
- **Tell the truth simply, in concrete words.** "Died", not "went to sleep" or "we lost him".
  Soft words confuse and frighten small children; a child told someone "went to sleep" may become
  afraid of sleep.
- **Expect the same question again and again.** That is processing.
- **Let them see adults grieve**, in a manageable form. Children who never see adult feeling learn
  to hide their own.
- **Keep the routine** (→ 21-04). It is not disrespectful; it is what holds a child steady.
- **Some ritual helps:** a marker, a drawing, saying goodbye (→ 21-06).
- **If the household may have to give up or lose its pets** (→ 30-11), tell the children honestly
  and simply, and never let them watch a pet's death. → 21-03 covers pet loss.

**Scavenge or substitute:** everything that matters most here is free: routine, closeness, honest
words, calm, play and attention. Stories told aloud need no books. Made toys (→ 16-04, → 16-03),
and the making is itself the activity. **What you cannot substitute: a calm adult.** That is why
→ 21-01 and → 21-02 are part of the child-care plan.

## When it runs out

- **Nothing here runs out, but the adults do.** The limit is adult emotional capacity, and months
  into a long event it is scarce (→ 21-02).
- **Protect it on purpose:** take turns (→ 21-05), protect sleep (→ 21-07), get time apart, and
  treat the adults' state as household infrastructure, because for the children it is.
- **The failure:** exhausted adults, worn-down routine, frightened children, each making the
  others worse. **The way back in is the routine**: the cheapest thing to restore, and it steadies
  the rest.
- **Other adults help:** a neighbour, a relative, another family (→ 18-01). Even brief relief
  changes an adult's capacity, and children benefit from other trusted adults directly.
- **Children are resilient when the adults around them are steady, the routine holds, and they
  are told the truth in a form they can carry.** Those three are within your control.

## By phase

- **A (shelter in place):** the first days are the most frightening. Name the plan, start the
  routine, and switch off the news where children can hear.
- **B (moving):** a short ritual at the end of each day (a story, a song) steadies a child. Keep
  them close and tell them the next stop, not the whole journey (→ 01-03).
- **C (establish):** a new place is both a loss and a relief. Re-start the routine in the first
  week and give children their own corner and jobs (→ 20-07).
