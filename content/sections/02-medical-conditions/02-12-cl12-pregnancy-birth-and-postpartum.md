# CL-12. Pregnancy, birth and the weeks after

**What this page is for.** Giving birth without a skilled attendant is one of the most dangerous
events this binder covers. The reason mothers and babies now usually survive is trained
attendants, clean technique, medicines to stop bleeding, and surgery when needed; none of those
can be improvised from a page. **So the plan is to be near working care at the due date.** This
module is everything around that plan: the due-date clock, the warning signs that mean act now,
what to stock, and, for the day help does not arrive, what a calm adult can do for a normal birth
and the first hour after. The general women's health page is → 12-12; feeding the baby is
→ 02-13.

> **SAFETY**
> - **Learn the urgent maternal warning signs (part 5) now, and post them.** CDC says they can
>   signal a life-threatening condition at any time in pregnancy and in the year after birth
>   (CDC, Urgent Maternal Warning Signs). Take "something does not feel right" seriously.
> - **Heavy bleeding after birth kills fastest.** Soaking through one or more pads in an hour, or
>   passing clots bigger than an egg, is an emergency (CDC).
> - **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products.** Iron
>   overdose is particularly dangerous for children, and a child who eats adult vitamins such as
>   prenatal vitamins can take a severe overdose (MedlinePlus, Iron Overdose; floor, → 30-01).
> - **This page gives no medicine doses.** Medicines used in birth and after are prescribed; ask
>   for the plan now (part 7).

## Do this now: the baby is coming and no help has arrived

Send someone for anyone nearby with birth experience (→ 18-03), then:
1. **Clean hands, clean place.** Wash hands and nails well; clean gloves if you have them.
   Clean sheet or plastic under the mother, clean cloths, a warm room (→ 08-01).
2. **Let her choose her position.** Upright, kneeling, side-lying, whatever helps. Encourage her
   to push when her body tells her to. **Do not pull on the baby.**
3. **As the head comes out, support it gently.** If the cord is looped loosely around the neck,
   slip it over the head. The shoulders and body usually follow with the next push.
4. **Dry the baby at once** with a clean cloth and lay it **skin to skin on the mother's chest**,
   both covered, head covered (→ figure). A wet baby loses heat fast.
5. **Is the baby breathing or crying?** If not: keep rubbing the back and drying firmly; wipe the
   mouth and nose. **Still not breathing: start infant breaths and CPR** (→ 12-18).
6. **The cord can wait.** WHO recommends clamping the cord late, about **1 to 3 minutes** after
   birth, and not earlier than 1 minute unless the baby needs to be moved at once for help with
   breathing (WHO, Prevention and Treatment of Postpartum Haemorrhage, 2012). When it has
   stopped pulsing, tie it tightly
   with clean (boiled) string in two places a few finger-widths apart and cut between the ties
   with a boiled blade (→ figure). There is no hurry to cut.
7. **The afterbirth (placenta) comes by itself**, usually with more cramps and a gush of blood.
   **Do not pull on the cord**: where skilled birth attendants are not available, WHO does not
   recommend cord traction (WHO, Postpartum Haemorrhage, 2012). Keep the placenta in a bowl to show anyone who arrives; a missing
   piece can cause bleeding (Hesperian, A Book for Midwives).
8. **Watch the bleeding and the womb.** Put the baby to the breast (it helps the womb tighten).
   Feel below the navel: the womb should be **firm**. If it is soft and she is bleeding, **rub it
   firmly** through the belly until it hardens. WHO recommends checking the womb's firmness
   through the belly after every birth, and uterine massage to treat heavy bleeding (WHO,
   Postpartum Haemorrhage, 2012). Heavy bleeding (soaking a
   pad in an hour, clots bigger than an egg, faintness, a racing pulse): **this is the
   emergency. Get her to any care that exists**, keep rubbing, lay her flat with legs raised,
   keep her warm.

## 1. What it is

Pregnancy is counted in weeks from the first day of the last menstrual period. A normal
pregnancy runs 38 to 42 weeks; a baby born before 37 weeks is premature (MedlinePlus,
Gestational Age). **Labour** is the
work of the womb pushing the baby out; **the postpartum period** is the weeks after birth, which
carry their own risks. WHO names the most common direct causes of injury and death: excessive
blood loss, infection, high blood pressure, unsafe abortion and obstructed labour (WHO, Maternal
Health).

## 4. Day by day without it

**Pregnancy without prenatal care** means nobody is checking blood pressure, growth, the baby's
position or the blood count. The household can do some of this:
- **Blood pressure:** a home cuff, checked regularly. Rising pressure with headache, vision
  changes or swelling of face and hands is a warning sign (part 5).
- **Food and water:** a pregnant woman needs more of both. **Folic acid:** CDC advises 400
  micrograms every day for anyone who could become pregnant, to prevent serious birth defects of
  the brain and spine (CDC, About Folic Acid). A stored-food diet has gaps (→ 04-18). Your
  household's water floor already adds for pregnancy (→ 03-01).
- **Rest and lighter work** in late pregnancy; no heavy lifting or long marches.
- **The baby's movements:** a change, especially less movement, is a warning sign (CDC).

**Medicines in pregnancy:** many common medicines are not safe. FDA advises avoiding
non-steroidal pain relievers such as ibuprofen and naproxen from **20 weeks** of pregnancy,
because they can cause rare but serious kidney problems in the unborn baby and low amniotic
fluid, unless a health professional advises it (low-dose aspirin for certain pregnancy
conditions, under a professional's direction, is the exception) (FDA, NSAIDs in Pregnancy,
2020). Ask the pharmacist
or prescriber before any medicine, herb or supplement.

**After the birth:** bleeding (like a heavy period, getting lighter over days to weeks) is
normal; very heavy bleeding is not (part 5). The mother needs rest, food, fluids, help with the
baby, and someone watching her mood. Nursing raises her food and water needs: the USDA/HHS
Dietary Guidelines estimate about **330 extra calories a day in the first 6 months and 400 in the
second 6 months** of breastfeeding (Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020–2025).

## 5. Warning signs that mean an emergency

**CDC's urgent maternal warning signs, in pregnancy and the year after birth** (CDC, Urgent
Maternal Warning Signs and Symptoms, as published, shortened):
- **Headache that won't go away or gets worse**, the worst of her life, or with blurred vision.
- **Dizziness or fainting.**
- **Changes in vision**: flashing lights, blind spots, blurred or double vision.
- **Fever of 100.4 °F (38 °C) or higher.**
- **Extreme swelling of hands or face.**
- **Thoughts of harming herself or the baby** (→ 12-15).
- **Trouble breathing**, or needing to prop up to breathe lying down.
- **Chest pain or a fast-beating heart.**
- **Severe nausea and throwing up**: unable to drink for more than 8 hours or eat for more than
  24 hours.
- **Severe belly pain that doesn't go away.**
- **Baby's movement stopping or slowing** during pregnancy.
- **Vaginal bleeding or fluid leaking** during pregnancy.
- **After birth: heavy bleeding** (soaking one or more pads in an hour), **clots bigger than an
  egg**, or bad-smelling discharge.
- **Swelling, redness or pain of one leg or arm** (pregnancy up to 6 weeks after birth): a clot.
- **Overwhelming tiredness.**

**The dangers of labour itself**: labour that does not progress (WHO lists obstructed labour
among the main killers; WHO, Maternal Health), a baby lying the wrong way, bleeding before
birth, fits (eclampsia). **None of these has a home
answer.** That is why the plan is to be near care.

**Newborn danger signs** (Hesperian, A Book for Midwives): not feeding, very sleepy or floppy,
breathing fast or grunting or with the chest pulling in, cold or hot to touch, yellow skin in
the first day, a red or smelly cord stump, fits. Keep the baby warm and feeding, and seek care.

## 6. Sick-day basics

- **Vomiting and diarrhoea in pregnancy dehydrate fast**; small sips of fluid or oral
  rehydration often (→ 12-05). Not keeping fluids down for 8 hours is on CDC's warning list.
- **Fever in pregnancy or after birth** may mean infection of the womb, bladder, breast or a
  wound. After birth, fever with belly pain or smelly discharge is serious (→ 12-04). Using
  antibiotics without a prescription is a household card: {{choice DC-16}} (→ 30-17).
- **Painful, red, hot area on a breast** while nursing: keep nursing or expressing from that side,
  rest, fluids; fever or spreading redness needs care (→ 02-13).

## 8. Sourcing when the pharmacy is closed

- **Prenatal vitamins, folic acid and iron** are over the counter and keep well; stock them even
  with no current pregnancy.
- **The scarce resource is skill, not supplies.** Midwives, nurses, doctors and experienced
  mothers in the community matter more than anything you can buy (→ 18-01).
- **Prescription medicines for birth** come only through a prescriber. Someone else's
  prescription is a card: {{choice DC-15}} (→ 30-16).

## 9. Power and devices

No device is needed for a normal birth. **Warmth is the power need**: a warm room for the birth
and for the newborn, who loses heat faster than anyone (→ 08-01, → 11-02). Light for a night
birth: a headlamp frees both hands.

## 10. Travel and evacuation

- **Make the movement decision early, well before the due date, and toward care** (→ 01-02,
  → 01-05). A known due date is one of the strongest reasons to be near working care.
- **Avoid moving a woman late in pregnancy or soon after birth** unless staying is more
  dangerous. If you must: the birth kit, the warning-sign list, extra pads, and a plan for a
  roadside birth (→ 01-07, → 22-06).
