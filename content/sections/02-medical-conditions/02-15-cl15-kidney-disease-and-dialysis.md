# CL-15. Kidney disease and dialysis

**What this page is for.** This is the hardest case in the library, and the module says so
plainly. **Dialysis is a machine treatment that does the work of failed kidneys. A household
cannot provide it, and nothing in this binder replaces it.** What the binder can do is help you
buy days by eating and drinking the way the dialysis team prescribes for emergencies, recognise
the dangerous signs early, keep the paperwork that gets you treated at another unit, and decide
early, while you can still act, when to leave for working care. People with kidney disease who
are not on dialysis have a longer clock; this module covers them too.

{{#if has.condition.CL-15}}**Someone in your household has this condition.** This module is at the front of the medical section for you. Read part 4 and part 11 with every adult in the house.{{else}}**Nobody in your household is marked with this condition.** The module stays in the binder so you can help someone else, or add the condition later in settings.{{/if}}

> **SAFETY**
> - **The emergency diet and fluid limit must come from the person's own dialysis team.** Limits
>   differ from person to person, and the wrong ones can kill through fluid on the lungs or high
>   potassium. This page prints the worksheet (part 7), never the numbers.
> - **Never run a generator, charcoal, or any engine indoors, in a garage, or near an open
>   window.** Carbon monoxide kills in minutes with no warning; home dialysis machines and
>   cyclers tempt people to run a generator close to the house (floor, → 30-01).
> - **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products** (floor,
>   → 30-01).
> - **This page never calculates, adjusts or suggests a dose, a fluid amount or a diet number.**

## Do this now

**When dialysis is interrupted (unit closed, no power, cannot travel):**
1. **Call the dialysis unit, then the backup unit** on the numbers in part 7. Units have
   emergency plans and can often send you to another unit (CMS, Preparing for Emergencies: A
   Guide for People on Dialysis).
2. **Start the emergency diet and fluid limit the dialysis team gave you** (part 7). Start on the
   first missed day, not the third.
3. **Take the medicines as the team wrote** for a missed treatment (part 12).
4. **Watch for the danger signs** in part 5 several times a day. Weigh daily if you have a scale.
5. **Decide now how many missed treatments trigger leaving for working care** (part 11, → 01-02).
   Leave while the person can still travel.

**Bleeding from a fistula or graft** (the access in the arm): press firmly on the spot with a
clean cloth or gauze and keep pressing; do not lift to look (→ 12-02). Get help.

## 1. What it is

Healthy kidneys filter waste, extra fluid and salts such as potassium out of the blood. In
**chronic kidney disease (CKD)** they slowly lose that ability (NIDDK, Chronic Kidney Disease).
When they fail, a person needs **dialysis** or a transplant to live. **Hemodialysis** pumps blood
through a filter on a machine, usually three times a week at a unit or at home, through an access
in the arm (a fistula or graft) or a tube in the chest (a catheter) (NIDDK, Hemodialysis).
**Peritoneal dialysis (PD)** uses the lining of the belly as the filter: a cleansing fluid goes in
through a tube, sits, and is drained, either by hand several times a day or by a machine (a
cycler) overnight (NIDDK, Peritoneal Dialysis).

## 2. What runs out, and how fast

| What | Clock | When it is gone |
|---|---|---|
| Hemodialysis at a unit | The next scheduled session. **Every missed session counts.** | Fluid, potassium and waste build up within days (part 4). |
| Home hemodialysis | Power, treated water and supplies for each session. | As above. |
| Peritoneal dialysis bags and supplies | Days of bags on hand (part 7). Manual exchanges need no power. | As above, a little slower for some people. |
| PD cycler | Power every night. | Switch to manual exchanges if the team taught you how. |
| Medicines (phosphate binders, blood pressure pills, others) | Days on hand from the bottle. | Varies; ask the team. |
| Injected medicines given at the unit (for anaemia, for example) | Given at the unit. | Tiredness and breathlessness from anaemia over weeks. |
| CKD not on dialysis | Daily pills; the kidneys still work partly. | Slow worsening; dehydration and some medicines can cause a sudden drop. |

## 3. Storing the medication without power

- **Tablets:** original bottles, tightly closed, cool and dry, out of sun and away from children.
- **PD fluid bags:** store as the supplier's label says, off the floor, dry, away from freezing
  and heat. Do not use a bag that is leaking, cloudy or damaged (→ 29-14).
- **Refrigerated injections** (some anaemia medicines): follow the label's cold rules (→ 29-07).
  Whether to use one after a heat excursion is a card: {{choice DC-08}} (→ 30-09).
- **Flood water:** FDA says drugs in contact with flood or contaminated water should be thrown
  away (FDA, Safe Drug Use After a Natural Disaster). That includes PD supplies whose packaging
  was soaked.

## 4. Day by day without it

**Hemodialysis stopped.** What public sources describe as treatments are missed: extra fluid
stays in the body, so weight goes up, ankles and legs swell, and breathing gets harder,
especially lying flat. Potassium rises, which can cause weakness and a dangerous heart rhythm,
sometimes with little warning. Waste builds up: nausea, poor appetite, itching, a bad taste,
tiredness, then confusion (NIDDK, Hemodialysis; NIDDK, Eating & Nutrition for Hemodialysis).
**How fast depends on how much kidney function is left, on what is eaten and drunk, and on
the person.** Someone who still makes urine has more time than someone who makes none. Without any
dialysis, kidney failure is fatal; the emergency diet buys days, not weeks.

**PD stopped.** The same build-up, often a little slower. With bags on hand, manual exchanges
need no power, which makes PD the more resilient form if supplies hold.

**CKD not on dialysis.** Usually slow. The danger is a sudden drop in kidney function from
dehydration, vomiting and diarrhoea, or medicines that strain the kidneys (part 6).

**What helps, and is in your control:**
- **The dialysis team's emergency diet**, which limits fluid, potassium, salt and phosphorus to
  slow the build-up (CMS, Preparing for Emergencies). Use theirs.
- **Count every liquid.** NIDDK says anything liquid at room temperature counts toward the fluid
  limit, including soup, ice and gelatin (NIDDK, Eating & Nutrition for Hemodialysis).
- **No salt substitutes** unless the team says so: many contain potassium (NIDDK, Eating &
  Nutrition for Hemodialysis).
- **Most stored and canned food is salty**, which makes thirst and fluid harder to control
  (→ 04-18). Ask the dietitian now which stored foods fit.

## 5. Warning signs that mean an emergency

- **High potassium:** muscle weakness, numbness or tingling, a slow, irregular or racing
  heartbeat, feeling faint. It can stop the heart. Get the person to dialysis or care; if they
  collapse and are not breathing normally, start CPR (→ 12-18).
- **Fluid on the lungs:** breathless at rest, cannot lie flat, a wet cough, fast weight gain,
  swelling rising up the legs. Sit them upright and get care.
- **Waste build-up:** confusion, severe drowsiness, vomiting, twitching or a seizure.
- **Access trouble:** a fistula or graft that has lost its buzz (the "thrill" the team taught you
  to feel), or one that is red, hot, swollen or leaking pus; bleeding that will not stop with
  pressure (→ 12-02).
- **Infection:** fever or chills, especially with a catheter (→ 12-04). On PD, **cloudy drained
  fluid, belly pain or fever** can be infection of the belly lining; tell the team at once and
  keep the cloudy bag to show them (NIDDK, Peritoneal Dialysis).

## 6. Sick-day basics

- **Vomiting, diarrhoea and fever** dehydrate, and dehydration can damage kidneys further in
  CKD. For someone on dialysis with a fluid limit, rehydration is a prescriber question; ask
  now and write the answer in part 7.
- **Pain relievers:** NIDDK advises people with CKD to avoid NSAID pain relievers such as
  ibuprofen and naproxen (NIDDK, Managing Chronic Kidney Disease). Ask which pain reliever is
  safe and write it in part 7 (→ 12-13).
- **Many medicines are cleared by the kidneys** and build up when the kidneys fail. Do not start
  any new medicine, supplement, herbal product or laxative without checking with a pharmacist
  or prescriber while you can.

## 8. Sourcing when the pharmacy is closed

- **Dialysis cannot be scavenged or improvised.** The only source is a working unit, a working
  home system with its supplies, or a hospital. That is why part 11 matters.
- **Other units:** in a regional disaster, dialysis networks move patients to units that are
  open. Ask your unit now how that works where you live, and write it in part 7.
- **Medicines:** pharmacies and disaster medical points while they run. Someone else's
  prescription is a card: {{choice DC-15}} (→ 30-16). Many ordinary medicines are dangerous in
  kidney failure; check before using anything not prescribed for this person.
- **Expired medicines:** {{choice DC-04}} (→ 30-05).

## 9. Power and devices

- **Home hemodialysis machines and PD cyclers need mains power**, and home hemodialysis also
  needs treated water. Ask the supplier now what the machine draws, whether it may run from a
  generator or power station, and how long a session needs (→ 09-06, → 09-10, → 29-02).
- **Generator:** outdoors only, at least 20 feet from windows and doors, with the exhaust
  pointed away (CDC, Carbon Monoxide Poisoning: Basics; floor, → 30-01, → 09-04).
- **Manual PD exchanges need no power.** Learn them now if the team will teach you.
- **Scale and blood pressure cuff** run on small batteries; keep spares. Protect a spare cuff
  from EMP in a metal box (→ 27-03).

## 10. Travel and evacuation

- **Leaving for working dialysis is often the right call**, and early is better than late
  (→ 01-07, → 01-02). Pick the destination by where dialysis is running.
- Carry: the treatment orders, access type, last labs, medicine list, unit and backup phone
  numbers (→ 25-09), medicines, emergency-diet food, a measuring cup, and for PD the bags and
  supplies for the trip plus a spare day (→ 22-06).
- Protect the access arm: no tight straps, no heavy bags on it.
