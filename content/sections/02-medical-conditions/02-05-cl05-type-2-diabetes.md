# CL-05. Type 2 diabetes (tablets and non-insulin medicines)

**What this page is for.** Type 2 diabetes is the most common long-term condition in US
households. Most people manage it with food, activity and tablets, sometimes with a weekly or
daily injection that is not insulin. It is far more forgiving than type 1 when medicine runs
short, but it is not harmless: some medicines cause dangerous low sugar, very high sugar can
cause a collapse, and wounds heal badly. This module covers what to keep, what changes when
supplies stop, and the questions to settle with the prescriber now. If insulin is part of the
treatment, read → 02-06 as well.

> **SAFETY**
> - **Some diabetes tablets can cause low blood sugar**: NIDDK names two types, sulfonylureas and
>   meglitinides, and says skipped or delayed meals and more physical activity than usual make
>   lows more likely (NIDDK, Low Blood Glucose). Know whether the person's do; the first aid is
>   below.
> - **Do not stop or cut medicines on your own** to make them last. Ask the prescriber now what
>   to do if supply runs short, and write the answer in part 6.
> - **This page never calculates, adjusts or suggests a dose.**

## Do this now

**Low blood sugar (shaky, sweaty, confused, odd behaviour) in someone on diabetes medicine:**
1. If they can swallow safely: **CDC's 15-15 rule**: 15 grams of fast carbohydrate (glucose
   tablets or gel by their label, or half a cup, 4 ounces, of juice or regular soda), wait 15
   minutes, check; if still below 70 mg/dL, repeat (CDC, Treatment of Low Blood Sugar). **If the
   person takes a medicine that slows the digestion of carbohydrate, use glucose tablets or
   gel**: NIDDK says other sugars will not act fast enough (NIDDK, Low Blood Glucose).
2. **Cannot swallow or not awake: nothing by mouth.** On their side. Glucagon if the prescriber
   supplied it, exactly as its label shows; CDC says to get emergency medical care right after a
   glucagon injection, so send for any that exists.
3. Food once they are awake. Some tablets (sulfonylureas) raise insulin over several hours
   (NIDDK): **watch them through the night.**

**Very high sugar with confusion, extreme thirst, drowsiness or vomiting:** fluids by small sips
if awake, check sugar, follow the sick-day plan (part 6), and seek care if any exists.

## 1. What it is

In type 2 diabetes, the body still makes insulin but uses it poorly, and over time makes less.
Blood sugar runs high, which over time can cause heart disease, vision loss and kidney disease (CDC,
Type 2 Diabetes).

## 2. What runs out, and how fast

| What | Clock | When it is gone |
|---|---|---|
| Tablets | Days on hand from the bottle (part 7). Most tablets keep well when stored cool and dry. | Sugar rises over days to weeks. |
| Non-insulin injections (for example weekly pens) | Pens left; most need a fridge before first use (label). | Sugar rises over weeks. |
| Meter strips and lancets | Count them. | No way to see lows and highs. |
| Fast sugar, if the medicine can cause lows | Cheap now. | Lows cannot be treated. |
| Foot-care supplies | Socks, dressings, nail clippers. | Small wounds become serious. |

## 3. Storing the medication without power

- **Tablets:** in their original bottles, tightly closed, cool and dry, out of sunlight and away
  from children. Not in a bathroom, a car or a hot attic. Crumbling, discoloured or sticky
  tablets are not used.
- **Injected non-insulin medicines** (pens taken daily or weekly): each label gives the fridge
  rule before first use and the number of days the pen may be kept out of the fridge after.
  **Read the box and write the days here** ________ (→ 29-14). Whether to use a pen past its
  out-of-fridge window after a heat excursion is the household's card: {{choice DC-08}}
  (→ 30-09). Do not use one that has frozen unless its own label says otherwise.
- The cold-storage ladder for any refrigerated medicine is on → 29-07 and → 12-21.

## 4. Day by day without it

What public sources describe when diabetes tablets stop:
- **Days to weeks:** sugar rises. Thirst, peeing often, tiredness, blurry vision (CDC, Diabetes
  Symptoms).
- **Weeks:** infections become more likely; CDC lists frequent urinary tract and yeast
  infections among the signs of high sugar. Skin and foot wounds heal slowly.
- **Danger case:** very high sugar with dehydration, especially in older people, during illness
  or heat, can lead to confusion, drowsiness and collapse. It needs fluids and medical care.

**What helps, and is in your control:** regular meals rather than feast and famine, fewer sugary
and starchy foods, daily walking or work, sleep, and plenty of water. Hard physical work in a
crisis lowers sugar. **A sudden change in diet or work can push someone on sugar-lowering
tablets into a low**, so check more often when the routine changes, and ask the prescriber how
to adjust (→ 04-18).

**Feet.** Numbness or tingling in the hands or feet is one of the symptoms CDC lists as more
common in type 2 diabetes (CDC, Diabetes Symptoms). Damaged nerves mean a person may not feel a blister or a splinter. Off-grid life is
hard on feet: wet boots, long walks, cold. **Look at the feet every day**, keep them dry and
clean, wear socks and shoes always, and treat any wound at once (→ 11-04, → 12-04).

## 5. Warning signs that mean an emergency

- **Low blood sugar** (below 70 mg/dL, CDC): shaky, sweaty, fast heartbeat, confused, irritable,
  dizzy; then seizure or unconsciousness (CDC, Low Blood Sugar). First aid in "Do this now".
- **Very high sugar with dehydration:** extreme thirst, dry mouth, confusion, drowsiness, weakness.
- **Ketoacidosis (DKA)** is less common in type 2 but happens, including with some tablets. ADA
  notes ketones are most likely in people taking insulin or an **SGLT2 inhibitor** (a tablet
  class; the label names it) (ADA, DKA). FDA has warned that SGLT2 inhibitors can cause
  ketoacidosis, sometimes with sugar that is not very high (FDA, SGLT2 Drug Safety Communication,
  2015). Signs: vomiting, belly pain, deep fast breathing, fruity breath, confusion (CDC).
- **Foot or skin infection:** spreading redness, heat, swelling, pus, fever, a smell (→ 12-04).

## 6. Sick-day basics

CDC's sick-day steps for people with diabetes (CDC, Managing Sick Days): keep taking diabetes
medicine as usual unless the prescriber's plan says otherwise; **check sugar every 4 hours**;
drink plenty of water; try to eat; if meals are impossible, about **50 grams of carbohydrate
every 4 hours** (for example 1½ cups of unsweetened applesauce or fruit juice); weigh daily;
check temperature morning and evening.

**Some medicines are commonly paused when a person cannot keep fluids down**, because of
dehydration or kidney strain; which ones depends on the person. Oral rehydration solution (the
WHO home recipe: 1 litre safe water, 6 teaspoons sugar, half a teaspoon salt; WHO,
Cholera Q&A; safety notes on → 12-05) treats dehydration; count its sugar.

## 8. Sourcing when the pharmacy is closed

- **Pharmacies, clinics, disaster medical points** while they run, with a printed prescription.
- **Many type 2 tablets are common generics.** Other households may hold the same medicine.
  Using someone else's prescription is a card: {{choice DC-15}} (→ 30-16). Same drug, same
  strength, and the prescriber's agreement if any prescriber can be reached.
- **There is no safe over-the-counter or herbal substitute.** Supplements sold "for blood sugar"
  are not tested replacements.
- **When scavenging**, only labelled, sealed bottles; check the name and strength against the
  person's row in part 12.

## 9. Power and devices

A glucose meter runs for months on a small battery; keep spares, and a spare meter in a metal
box against an EMP (→ 27-03). Refrigerated pens need the cold ladder (→ 29-07, → 09-10). If the
person uses a sensor, see → 02-02 for device advice.

## 10. Travel and evacuation

- Medicines, meter and fast sugar on the person, out of heat and cold (→ 01-07, → 22-06).
- Long walks and missed meals: check sugar more often if the medicine can cause lows; carry fast
  sugar in a pocket.
- Good socks, dry feet, check them every evening.
