# CL-20. Adrenal insufficiency

**What this page is for.** In adrenal insufficiency the body cannot make enough **cortisol**, the
hormone that keeps blood pressure and blood sugar up and that the body makes more of during
illness, injury and stress. A daily steroid tablet replaces it. The danger is **adrenal crisis**:
during illness, serious injury or other physical stress, the body needs much more cortisol than
usual, and if a crisis is not treated right away it can cause death (NIDDK, Adrenal
Insufficiency & Addison's Disease). **How much extra, and when, is
life-critical.** The prescriber's written plan comes first; this module prints it as a worksheet,
explains why it matters, covers the emergency injection, and prints the published amounts, as
published, for when there is no plan and no prescriber (part 7).

> **SAFETY**
> - **Adrenal crisis is a life-threatening emergency.** If the person is vomiting, collapsing or
>   confused, **give the emergency steroid injection** the prescriber supplied, exactly as they
>   taught you, and get care (NIDDK, Adrenal Insufficiency & Addison's Disease; MedlinePlus,
>   Acute Adrenal Crisis).
> - **Never stop the daily steroid suddenly**, and never skip it to make it last (MedlinePlus,
>   Prednisone).
> - **Extra doses for illness ("stress dosing") follow the prescriber's written plan.** When there
>   is no prescriber and no plan, part 7 prints the published sick-day rules and injection amounts
>   as each publisher wrote them, and the one the binder follows where they differ. This binder
>   never works out a dose for a person; children's amounts are the publishers' age bands.
> - **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products.** (floor,
>   → 30-01).

## Do this now

**Signs of adrenal crisis:** NIDDK lists sudden, severe pain in the lower back, belly or legs;
vomiting and diarrhoea; weakness; confusion; loss of consciousness (NIDDK, Adrenal Insufficiency
& Addison's Disease). The Endocrine Society adds dehydration, low blood pressure and fainting
(Endocrine Society, Adrenal Insufficiency); MedlinePlus adds dizziness, high fever, a fast
heartbeat and low blood sugar (MedlinePlus, Acute Adrenal Crisis).

1. **Give the emergency steroid injection** from the kit, into the muscle of the outer thigh,
   in the amount on the kit's pharmacy label or the prescriber's plan (part 7). **With neither:**
   adults and children 6 years and over, 100 mg; children 1 to 5 years, 50 mg; under 1 year,
   25 mg (BSPED 2023 for children; Society for Endocrinology 2016 for adults). Other publishers'
   children's bands differ (part 7).
   The Endocrine Society says a person in adrenal crisis needs a steroid injection right away,
   then the hospital (Endocrine Society, Adrenal Insufficiency). Do not wait to see if it passes.
   **Not sure it is a crisis? Inject.** ADSHG: "if in doubt, you should inject/be injected";
   NICE: "there is no risk of overdose from hydrocortisone in an emergency situation".
   **If the person is vomiting, tablets will not stay down.**
2. **Call for help** if any exists (911). Say "adrenal crisis" and show the medical ID.
3. **Lay them down**, on their side if vomiting or drowsy. Keep them warm.
4. **Fluids by small sips** if awake and not vomiting; oral rehydration solution is best (part 6).
5. **Not breathing normally:** CPR (→ 12-18).
6. **Write the time** of the injection and give the paper to whoever takes over.

**Fever, injury or vomiting without crisis signs yet:** follow the stress plan in part 7 now,
not later.

## 1. What it is

The adrenal glands, on top of the kidneys, make cortisol and, in some people, a salt-keeping
hormone. In **primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease)** the glands are damaged; in
**secondary adrenal insufficiency** the brain stops telling them to work. NIDDK says the most
common cause overall is suddenly stopping steroid medicines (corticosteroids, such as
prednisone) after taking them for a long time (NIDDK, Adrenal Insufficiency & Addison's
Disease). It is treated with a daily steroid, most often hydrocortisone taken two or three times
a day, sometimes with a second tablet (fludrocortisone) that balances salt and fluid in the body,
and higher doses are needed during physical stress (NIDDK, Treatment).

## 2. What runs out, and how fast

| What | Clock | When it is gone |
|---|---|---|
| Daily steroid tablets | Days on hand from the bottle (part 7). **The extra needed in illness uses the supply faster.** | Weakness, nausea, dizziness; then crisis (part 4). |
| Salt-keeping tablet (fludrocortisone), if prescribed | Days on hand. | Dizziness on standing, salt craving, dehydration. |
| Emergency injection kit(s) | Expiry date; one kit per crisis. | No way to treat a crisis in someone who is vomiting. |
| Needles, syringes, alcohol pads, sharps container | Count them. | The injection cannot be given. |

## 3. Storing the medication without power

- **Tablets:** original bottles, tightly closed, cool and dry, out of sun.
- **Emergency injection kit:** the label gives the temperature range and how to protect it from
  light. Read it and write it here: ________ (→ 29-14). The Solu-Cortef label, as published:
  "Store unreconstituted product at controlled room temperature 20°C to 25°C (68°F to 77°F)";
  once mixed, "Use the reconstituted and/or further diluted solution within 12 hours of
  preparation" and protect it from light. NIDDK says to keep a steroid injection
  with the person at all times and make sure friends and family know how and when to give it
  (NIDDK). Keep it where every adult can reach it in a minute, and a second kit in the go-bag.
- **Used needles** go into a sharps container or a hard plastic bottle with a lid (FDA, Best Way
  to Get Rid of Used Needles and Other Sharps).
- **Expired kit or tablets** when nothing else exists: {{choice DC-04}} (→ 30-05).

## 4. Day by day without it

- **First:** the symptoms of adrenal insufficiency return: tiredness, muscle weakness, poor
  appetite, nausea, belly pain, dizziness or fainting on standing, craving salt, low blood sugar
  (NIDDK, Symptoms & Causes).
- **Then:** vomiting, diarrhoea, confusion and adrenal crisis. Physical stress (illness,
  infection, injury) can make it much worse suddenly (Endocrine Society, Adrenal Insufficiency).
  How fast it happens differs from person to person; do not wait for it.
- **Long-term steroid users stopping suddenly:** MedlinePlus says the body may not have enough
  natural steroids to work normally (MedlinePlus, Prednisone), and MedlinePlus lists suddenly
  stopping long-term steroid medicine as a cause of adrenal crisis (MedlinePlus, Acute Adrenal
  Crisis).

**What helps, and is in your control:** never running out (part 7), regular meals, enough salt
and water as the prescriber advises (more in heat and heavy work), rest after hard work, staying
warm, and starting the stress plan early.

## 5. Warning signs that mean an emergency

- **Adrenal crisis:** see "Do this now". Ask the prescriber now what to do when you are not
  sure whether it is a crisis, and write the answer in part 7, so nobody hesitates on the day.
  With no answer written, the published one is to inject (ADSHG; NICE).
- **Any vomiting** that keeps tablets from staying down.
- **Serious injury, a broken bone, a burn, a seizure, or surgery-level wound** (→ 12-16).
- **High fever or signs of a serious infection** (→ 12-04).
- **Fainting, or dizziness that does not pass when lying down.**

## 6. Sick-day basics

The body needs more cortisol during illness. People with adrenal insufficiency are taught
**sick-day rules** by their prescriber: when to take extra, how much, and for how long. The
prescriber's are written in part 7; the published ones, for when there are none, are in part 7
too.

- **Fever, flu, diarrhoea, an infection, an injury:** start the stress plan in part 7.
- **Vomiting:** tablets may not stay down. The plan says when to use the injection.
- **Fluids and salt:** oral rehydration solution (the WHO home recipe: 1 litre safe water, 6
  level teaspoons sugar, half a level teaspoon salt; WHO, Cholera Q&A; safety notes on → 12-05).
- **Heat and heavy sweating** lose salt; ask the prescriber how this person should replace it
  (→ 17-04). With no prescriber: oral rehydration solution, above, replaces water and salt.

## 7. What to stock, and how much

**Ask your prescriber now, while you can, and write it here.**

**Why it matters.** The body needs much more cortisol than usual during illness, serious injury
or surgery (NIDDK). This person's body cannot make it. The prescriber's stress plan does that job
by hand: it says how much extra steroid to take, at which signs, and for how long. NIDDK says
the dose must go up with a high fever, and that someone who is vomiting and cannot keep the
tablets down needs medical care at once (NIDDK, Treatment). **Without the plan, a household
facing a fever or an injury has to guess, and a guess too small or too late can end in adrenal
crisis.** With it, the household knows what to give and when to use the injection. The plan
differs from person to person, and the prescriber is the only one who can write it.

**A. The emergency injection: how much**

| Who | Published amount (as published) | Publisher, year |
|---|---|---|
| Adults | "immediate bolus injection of 100 mg hydrocortisone i.v. or i.m." | Society for Endocrinology, 2016 |
| Adults who cannot take tablets | "Adults, im or sc hydrocortisone 100 mg" | Endocrine Society, 2016 (Table 3) |
| People 16 and over who cannot absorb tablets | "Give 100 mg intramuscular or intravenous hydrocortisone." | NICE (England), 2024 |
| Children | "less than 1 year: 25 mg"; "1–5 years: 50 mg"; "6 years and over: 100 mg", given "intramuscularly or intravenously" | BSPED (British Society for Paediatric Endocrinology and Diabetes), 2023 |
| Children who cannot take tablets | "children, im hydrocortisone 50 mg/m2 or estimate; infants, 25 mg; school-age children, 50 mg; adolescents, 100 mg" | Endocrine Society, 2016 (Table 3) |
| Children, teens and adults | "25 mg for children under 3 years of age"; "50mg for children 3-10 years of age"; "100mg for children older than 10 years of age or weighing more than 40 kg"; "100mg for teens and adults". Written as emergency-room instructions: the first dose "can be administered IM if IV access an issue". | CARES Foundation (US), brochure |

(i.m. or IM = into a muscle; i.v. or IV = into a vein, which needs a clinician; sc = under the
skin.) **For adults the sources agree: 100 mg. For children they differ** at ages 1 to 2 (BSPED
50 mg; CARES 25 mg) and 6 to 10 (BSPED 100 mg; CARES 50 mg), and the Endocrine Society's bands
("infants", "school-age", "adolescents") have no exact ages. **The binder follows BSPED 2023**
when the child's own kit label and plan are blank: it is the only one written for the first
injection given at home by the family, with exact ages, and it is the newest. Where it differs it
is the larger amount; NICE says there is "no risk of overdose from hydrocortisone in an emergency
situation" (written for people 16 and over) and ADSHG says "There is no known toxic dose of
hydrocortisone in an emergency".

**The kit.** The Solu-Cortef label, as published, for the 100 mg Act-O-Vial: "Each 2 mL contains
(when mixed): Hydrocortisone sodium succinate equiv. to 100 mg Hydrocortisone", and each is a
"Single-Dose Vial". The mixing steps, as published: "Press down on plastic activator to force
diluent into the lower compartment. Gently agitate to effect solution. Remove plastic tab covering
center of stopper. Sterilize top of stopper with a suitable germicide. Insert needle squarely
through center of stopper until tip is just visible. Invert vial and withdraw dose." Where to
inject: CARES says "the outer portion in the middle of the thigh"; ADSHG says "usually of the
outer thigh or upper arm". **For a child who needs less than the whole vial, ask a pharmacist or
nurse now how much of the mixed vial that is, and write it in the worksheet above.**

**B. When to inject (as published)**
- Society for Endocrinology, 2016: "Sick Day Rule 2: the need to administer glucocorticoids per
  i.v. or i.m. injection during prolonged vomiting or diarrhoea, during preparation for
  colonoscopy or in case of acute trauma or surgery".
- NICE, 2024: "If the person vomits within 30 minutes of taking an oral dose, advise them to take
  a further dose once vomiting subsides, at double the original dose. If vomiting recurs within
  30 minutes, give intramuscular hydrocortisone".
- BSPED card, 2022 (children): "If they show no sign of improvement, have persistent vomiting or
  diarrhoea, become drowsy or unresponsive, or has had a major accident or injury give
  intramuscular (IM) injection of hydrocortisone immediately".
- CARES Foundation: "do NOT wait to give the injectable hydrocortisone. It should be given BEFORE
  a trip to the emergency room or activating 911".
- ADSHG: "if in doubt, you should inject/be injected."

**C. Extra tablets for illness: the sick-day rules (as published)**

| Publisher, year | Rule as published |
|---|---|
| Endocrine Society, 2016 (Table 3, illness with fever) | "Hydrocortisone replacement doses doubled (>38°C) or tripled (>39°C) until recovery (usually 2 to 3 d); increased consumption of electrolyte-containing fluids as tolerated". (38°C is 100.4°F; 39°C is 102.2°F.) |
| Society for Endocrinology, 2016 (adults) | "Sick Day Rule 1: the need to double daily oral glucocorticoid dose during illness with fever that requires bed rest and/or antibiotics". Keep "an additional supply of hydrocortisone tablets so that they can double their dose for at least 7 days". |
| NICE, 2024 (16 and over) | "During periods of significant physiological stress, offer at least 40 mg oral hydrocortisone daily in 2 to 4 divided doses or at least 10 mg oral prednisolone daily in 1 to 2 divided doses until the acute illness or physical trauma has resolved." People already taking 10 mg prednisolone a day or more "do not need additional sick-day dosing, but they can split their total daily dose into 2 equal doses". |
| ADSHG (UK patient charity; adults) | Usual hydrocortisone "10-25mg": "Take 40mg in 24 hours, in 2-4 doses. Start by taking 20mg immediately." Usual "more than 25 mg": "Double your usual in 24 hours, in 2-4 doses." Usual prednisolone "3-10 mg": "10mg in 24 hours, in 1-2 doses." "Updose for 48 hours". At "39 degrees or more": "Take 20mg of hydrocortisone every 6 hours (or 5mg of prednisolone every 6 hours) and seek immediate medical advice." |
| CARES Foundation (children and adults with congenital adrenal hyperplasia) | "FEVER GREATER THAN 100.5: DOUBLE the hydrocortisone dose for the entire day"; "FEVER GREATER THAN 102: TRIPLE the hydrocortisone dose for the entire day"; "VOMITING: Triple dose with vomiting whether or not there is a fever." Vomited within 30 minutes: "Wait 10-15 minutes ... and repeat triple stress dose of hydrocortisone. If you/r child vomit(s) again, give the injectable hydrocortisone". "Try small amounts of clear liquids that contain SUGAR (not artificial sweetener) frequently, at least 1 ounce every 15 minutes." |
| BSPED, 2023 (children) | "a total daily hydrocortisone dose of around 30 mg/m2/day given as four equally divided doses"; the child's team writes the amount on the BSPED card. Mild cold with no fever: "No change". |

All agree the salt-keeping tablet (fludrocortisone) stays the same (BSPED; ADSHG; CARES: "The
Florinef dose does not change"). **They differ** on the fever that starts the rule (ADSHG "over
37.5"; Endocrine Society 38°C; CARES 100.5°F) and on how much: double or triple the person's own
dose (Endocrine Society, CARES, Society for Endocrinology) or a fixed amount (NICE, ADSHG).
**The binder follows the Endocrine Society 2016** for anyone on hydrocortisone with no written
plan: it is the US national society's guideline, it works from the person's own prescribed dose,
and it needs only a thermometer. For vomiting it follows NICE (one repeat dose at double, then the
injection), which ADSHG and CARES match. For people 16 and over on prednisone or prednisolone it
follows NICE's prednisolone line; the Solu-Cortef label's table of equal strengths lists
"Prednisolone, 5" and "Prednisone, 5".

**D. After the injection, when no hospital can be reached.** The published amounts after the
first injection are written for hospital staff, most of them into a vein:
- Society for Endocrinology, 2016 (adults): "followed by continuous intravenous infusion of 200
  mg hydrocortisone per 24 h (alternatively 50 mg hydrocortisone per i.v. or i.m. Injection every
  6 h)", with "rapid intravenous infusion of 1000 mL of isotonic saline infusion within the first
  hour".
- NICE, 2024 (16 and over): "give 200 mg intravenous hydrocortisone over 24 hours or 50 mg
  intramuscular or intravenous hydrocortisone 4 times a day"; then "Offer at least 40 mg oral
  hydrocortisone daily in 2 to 4 divided doses ... until any underlying cause has resolved and the
  person is clinically stable."
- Endocrine Society, 2016 (adults): "hydrocortisone 200 mg/d as a continuous infusion for 24 h,
  reduced to hydrocortisone 100 mg/d the following day"; children "hydrocortisone 50–100 mg/m2
  bolus followed by hydrocortisone 50–100 mg/m2/d divided q 6 h" (q 6 h = every 6 hours).
- BSPED, 2023 (children, hospital): "2 mg/kg (max 100 mg) intravenous bolus initially then bolus
  dose 6 hourly"; once "Stable and improving", "1 mg/kg (max 50 mg) intravenously 6 hourly".
- CARES Foundation (emergency-room instructions): "Hydrocortisone as a continuous drip (if
  necessary) or in 4 divided doses IV bolus": "50mg/day for 0-3 years of age"; "75 mg/day for
  3-10 years of age"; "100 mg/day for children greater than 10 years of age or weighing more than
  40 kg"; "100 mg/day for teens and adults".

**What the sources do not give:** a schedule for further injections given at home to a child, a
way to replace the drip of fluids at home, or a way to make the tablets last longer. The binder
will not invent one.

**What this binder recommends when no clinician exists:**
1. **Crisis signs ("Do this now"), or vomiting that the repeat dose did not stop: inject now** in
   the part A amount. Do not wait to be sure.
2. **Then move toward any care that can give fluids by drip.** When to leave is a card
   (part 11, → 30-20). Keep
   the person lying down, warm, and sipping oral rehydration solution if awake and not vomiting.
3. **Adults, no care reachable, still unable to keep tablets down:** the binder follows the line
   the Society for Endocrinology and NICE both print for the muscle: 50 mg into the muscle every
   6 hours. Each Act-O-Vial is single-dose. Once tablets stay down: at least 40 mg hydrocortisone
   a day in 2 to 4 doses (NICE) until well.
4. **Children, no care reachable, still unable to keep tablets down 6 hours after the
   injection:** no source covers this. The only published amounts in age bands are CARES's per
   day, in 4 divided doses (part D), written for staff giving them into a vein. The binder follows
   them, given into the muscle as the first injection was, because the alternative is no cortisol
   at all.
5. **Fever or illness without crisis signs:** the Endocrine Society rule (part C): double the
   usual hydrocortisone above 38°C, triple above 39°C, until recovery. Watch hour by hour for
   crisis signs.
6. **Back to the usual amount:** ADSHG: "If you are feeling better, go back to your usual dose";
   after "a more severe or long-term illness ... you shouldn't suddenly reduce to your normal dose
   in one go".
7. **Supply running short:** never skip the daily tablets to save them; a gap is how a crisis
   starts. If long-term steroid tablets will certainly run out, the prednisone label says "it is
   recommended that it be withdrawn gradually rather than abruptly" (prednisone tablets label).

**Practise the injection now** with the prescriber or nurse, on a trainer if they have one. Every
adult in the house should have done it once.

## 8. Sourcing when the pharmacy is closed

- **Pharmacies, clinics and disaster medical points** while they run, with the printed
  prescription and the emergency card. Hydrocortisone and prednisone are common; hospitals keep
  injectable steroids.
- **Someone else's prescription** is a card: {{choice DC-15}} (→ 30-16). Steroids come in very
  different strengths; tablets of one kind are not equal to the same number of another kind. Ask
  a pharmacist or prescriber before any switch.
- **There is no over-the-counter or herbal substitute.** Products sold for "adrenal fatigue" are
  not treatment.

## 9. Power and devices

None. The medicines need no power or fridge (check the label).

## 10. Travel and evacuation

- Tablets, the stress plan, and **an emergency kit on the person**, not in a bag in the trunk
  (→ 01-07, → 22-06).
- Travel brings strain, poor food, heat or cold: start the stress plan at the first sign of
  illness.
- Medical ID worn, emergency card in a pocket (→ 25-09).
