# CL-23. Hearing and vision aids

**What this page is for.** Glasses, contact lenses, hearing aids and cochlear implants are easy
to forget in a preparedness plan, until they are lost in an evacuation or the batteries run out.
Without them, a person may not hear a warning shout, read a label, see a hazard, drive, shoot, or
do half the jobs in this binder. Everything on this page is cheap to fix now and hard to fix
later: spares, batteries, a written prescription, and a plan for warnings that does not depend on
hearing or sight.

{{#if has.condition.CL-23}}**Someone in your household has this condition.** This module is at the front of the medical section for you.{{else}}**Nobody in your household is marked with this condition.** The module stays in the binder because most adults wear glasses at some point; check part 7 anyway.{{/if}}

> **SAFETY**
> - **Never rinse or store contact lenses in tap water or untreated water.** FDA warns that water
>   can carry germs that cause serious eye infections (FDA, Focusing on Contact Lens Safety).
>   In an emergency, switch to glasses.
> - **Someone who cannot hear must still get warnings:** a smoke alarm with a strobe or bed
>   shaker, and a household signal (part 4; USFA, Smoke Alarms).
> - **Hearing-aid and coin batteries are dangerous if swallowed.** Keep them away from small
>   children and from anyone confused.

## Do this now

1. **Count spare glasses, contact lens supplies and hearing-aid batteries**, and write the days
   on hand in part 7.
2. **A red, painful eye with blurred vision** in a contact lens wearer: take the lens out, wear
   glasses, and get care (part 5).
3. **Agree the household's warning signals** for someone who cannot hear or see well (part 4).
4. **Put the written glasses prescription in this binder** (photo slot below).

## 1. What it is

**Vision aids** are glasses, contact lenses, magnifiers and, for low vision, larger print and
better light. **Hearing aids** are small amplifiers worn in or behind the ear; since 2022 some are
sold over the counter to adults with mild to moderate hearing loss (FDA, OTC Hearing Aids: What
You Should Know). A **cochlear implant** is a surgically placed device with an outer sound
processor that runs on batteries. Some household members use none of these and still have
reduced hearing or sight; noise from generators, tools and firearms damages hearing for good
(CDC/NIOSH, About Noise and Hearing Loss).

## 2. What runs out, and how fast

| What | Clock | When it is gone |
|---|---|---|
| Disposable hearing-aid batteries | Days per battery (the person knows); count spares. | The aid is silent. |
| Rechargeable hearing aids and implant processors | A day or so per charge; need power. | Silent until charged. |

| Daily or monthly contact lenses | Days of lenses and solution. | Switch to glasses. |
| Contact lens solution | Bottles on hand. | **Do not substitute water** (SAFETY). |
| Reading glasses, magnifiers | Until lost. | Cannot read labels or doses. |

## 3. Storing the devices without power

- **Hearing aids:** dry and cool; out of the bathroom, the car and direct sun. A drying jar or
  a sealed box with a desiccant pack overnight. Open the battery door at night to save the battery.
- **Hearing-aid batteries:** in their original card at room temperature; pull the tab only when
  using one. Write the size here: ________ (→ 29-14).
- **Glasses:** a hard case, always. Spares in the go-bag and the car.
- **Contact lenses and solution:** sealed, out of heat and freezing; check expiry.
- **Cochlear implant processors and chargers:** dry, protected from impact; a spare processor if
  the clinic provides one.

## 4. Day by day without it

**Without hearing aids:** missed warnings, missed instructions, isolation and frustration.
**Without glasses:** falls, injuries with tools and fire, misread labels and medicine doses,
inability to drive or keep watch.

**Warnings that do not depend on hearing** (Ready.gov, Individuals with Disabilities; USFA, Smoke
Alarms):
- **Smoke and CO alarms** with strobe lights or a bed shaker for a person who is deaf or hard of
  hearing.
- **A household signal:** a flashlight flicked on and off, a tap on the shoulder, a written card
  ("FIRE: OUT NOW", "STAY INSIDE", "WE ARE LEAVING"). Agree the signals with everyone (→ 15-07).
- **Weather and hazard alerts** by text, a light or vibration as well as sound (→ 15-01).
- **A notepad and pencil** in every room and every go-bag; a whiteboard by the door.
- **At night,** the person sleeps where someone can reach them quickly.

**For someone who is blind or has low vision:** keep things in fixed places, clear walkways,
good light, tactile marks (a rubber band, a dot of glue) on medicine bottles and supply bins,
and a sighted guide for evacuation (→ 10-01).

**Protect the hearing you have:** earplugs or ear muffs near generators, chainsaws and firearms
(CDC/NIOSH, About Noise and Hearing Loss).

## 5. Warning signs that mean an emergency

- **Contact lens eye infection:** a red, painful eye, blurry vision, light hurting, discharge.
  Remove the lens, do not put another in, wear glasses, get care (FDA, Focusing on Contact Lens
  Safety; → 12-04).
- **Sudden loss of vision, flashes and floaters, or a curtain across vision:** urgent eye care.
- **Eye injuries and chemicals in the eye:** MedlinePlus first aid (MedlinePlus, Eye
  Emergencies); do not rub; flush chemicals with lots of clean water.
- **Sudden hearing loss in one ear,** or an ear that is painful, swollen or draining after an
  infection: get care.
- **A swallowed button or hearing-aid battery,** especially in a child: get care at once.

## 6. Sick-day basics

- **Colds and ear infections** can block hearing aids and make them painful; leave them out while
  the ear is sore and draining.
- **When ill, switch from contacts to glasses.**
- **Clean hands before touching eyes, lenses or aids** (→ 13-02).

## 8. Sourcing when the pharmacy is closed

- **Reading glasses** (magnifying, for close work) are sold everywhere and are easy to scavenge;
  they do not correct distance vision or astigmatism.
- **Prescription glasses** belonging to someone else rarely match; a near match can be better
  than nothing for a short time, but it may cause headaches and is unsafe for driving.
- **Hearing-aid batteries** are sold in pharmacies and big stores; the size is on the pack.
- **Contact lenses:** do not use someone else's. Switch to glasses.
- **Medicines** for eye or ear infections: someone else's prescription is a card:
  {{choice DC-15}} (→ 30-16). Expired eye drops: {{choice DC-04}} (→ 30-05); once opened, eye
  drops can grow germs.

## 9. Power and devices

- **Rechargeable hearing aids and implant processors** need charging every day. Charging cases
  usually charge from USB, so a power station or small solar panel can run them (→ 09-06,
  → 09-10, → 29-02).
- **Disposable zinc-air batteries need no charging** and are the most resilient choice in a long
  outage; keep a stock even if the person uses rechargeables.
- **EMP and storms:** a spare charger, cable and processor in a metal box (→ 27-03, → 27-02).

## 10. Travel and evacuation

- **Glasses on the face, spares in the go-bag** (→ 22-03, → 22-06), hearing aids in and a
  battery card in a pocket.
- **Tell rescuers and shelter staff** that the person is deaf, hard of hearing, blind or low
  vision; a card saying so helps (→ 25-09).
- **The communication plan** (→ 15-03, → 00-04) should include how this person will be reached
  if separated: text, written notes, a meeting place.
