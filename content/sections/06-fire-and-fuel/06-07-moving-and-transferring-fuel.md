# Moving and transferring fuel (siphoning)

**What this page is for.** Pouring, pumping and siphoning fuel is when it hurts people. This page
covers the tools that make it safe, the static-electricity rules for filling cans, why the
hose-down-the-filler-neck trick rarely works on modern cars, how to judge old fuel you find, and
what to do about a spill.

**New to fuels?** → 29-01 explains gasoline, diesel and kerosene and how to tell them apart.

> **SAFETY.**
> - **Fuel is never stored or transferred near an open flame or a running appliance.** Vapour
>   ignition. Transfer outdoors, engines off, nobody smoking.
> - **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products.** Fixed
>   in every source.
> - **Never siphon by mouth.** Gasoline irritates the lungs when breathed in and the stomach
>   lining when swallowed, and high exposures can harm the nervous system (CDC/ATSDR, ToxFAQs
>   for Automotive Gasoline). If anyone swallows fuel: **do not make them vomit** (vomiting is
>   how it gets into the lungs); **call Poison Control, 1-800-222-1222**; with no phone, → 12-17.
> - **Static can light it.** **Put the can on the ground to fill it**: never inside a vehicle,
>   a trunk, a pickup bed or on a trailer floor. **Fill slowly** (CPSC, Fuel Container Safety).
> - **Taking fuel that is not yours is theft** (→ 22-02).

## Do this now

1. **Buy a shaker (jiggle) siphon and a hand transfer pump.** They cost little and **remove any
   reason to put a hose in your mouth.** Put one in each vehicle kit (→ 22-05).
2. **Find out what your own vehicles allow.** Open the filler and look in. If you cannot see down
   into the tank, a hose will not go down it (below). Find out before an emergency.
3. **Buy two or three approved fuel containers** and label each with its fuel and the fill date
   (→ 06-06).
4. **Never keep fuel in the living space, and never in glass or a food or drink container.**

## Why "a hose down the filler neck" usually fails now

On most vehicles built in roughly the last 25 years, **it does not work**:

- **Anti-siphon screens and rollover valves** sit in the filler neck to stop fuel escaping in a
  crash. A hose hits them and stops.
- **The filler neck bends**, so even without a screen a hose will not reach the fuel.
- **Forcing a stiff hose past them can damage the neck** and leave a leaking tank.

Hybrid fuel systems are often sealed even more tightly; some run pressurised tanks. **Assume a
modern car's tank cannot be siphoned until you have checked.** For a household with modern cars,
**the fuel you can reach is the fuel in your cans**, which is why the cans stay full and rotated
(→ 06-06). The car's tank gives its fuel back by driving.

Siphoning still matters for equipment with open, reachable tanks: older vehicles, mowers, boats,
ATVs, generators.

## The four ways to move fuel, best first

**1. Shaker (jiggle) siphon: the one to own.** A hose with a one-way valve on the end. Drop the
valve end in the fuel, **jiggle it up and down a few times**, and gravity does the rest. **No
mouth suction at any point.**
- **The receiving can must sit lower than the fuel you are drawing from.** Gravity does the work.
- Cheap, nothing to break, works on any tank it can reach.

**2. Hand or battery transfer pump.** A pump with an inlet hose and an outlet hose. Works uphill,
faster, and does not care which container is higher. **The right tool for moving fuel between
containers**, which is most of what you will do: topping up a generator from a can, emptying a
can into a car.

**3. The fuel-rail valve on fuel-injected engines.** Many fuel-injected vehicles have a valve on
the engine's fuel rail that looks like a tyre valve (a **Schrader valve**). The car's own fuel
pump can push fuel out through it.
- **Real hazards:** a pressurised fuel system, a hot engine bay, electrical connections near fuel
  vapour.
- **It differs by vehicle.** Not every vehicle has a reachable valve, and how the pump is
  switched on differs by model.
- **Learn it on your own vehicle, from its own service manual, in daylight, before you need it.**
  Do not improvise it from a general description, including this one.

**4. A drain plug, or dropping the tank.** Some tanks have a drain plug; most modern ones do not.
Dropping a tank is a real job with the vehicle properly supported. Last resort.

## The static rules: the part people skip

CPSC's guidance is specific (CPSC, Fuel Container Safety):

1. **Put the container on the ground to fill it.**
2. **Never fill a container inside a vehicle, a trunk, a pickup bed or on a trailer floor**,
   including on a plastic bed liner.
3. **Fill slowly.** Fast flow builds static and splashes.
4. Keep the nozzle or hose **touching the container's opening** while filling.
5. No smoking, no flames, no running engines, nothing in your other hand.
6. **Do not get back into the vehicle in the middle of filling** and then return to the nozzle:
   that is how people build a charge on themselves.
7. Fuel rushing through a metal screen can itself make a spark.

{{figure fig-fill-on-ground}}

**Why it matters.** CPSC: if fuel vapour escapes from a container and meets a spark or flame, it
can ignite and **flash back into the container**, causing an explosion that can severely burn
people nearby. Each year thousands of people go to emergency departments with burns from
flammable liquids (CPSC).

**Flame jetting** is the failure worth knowing by name. CPSC describes it as an outside flame
suddenly igniting fuel vapour inside a container and forcefully throwing burning vapour and
liquid out of its mouth, like a blowtorch.

**That is why the container matters as much as the method.** Since **July 2023**, CPSC has
required portable fuel containers to carry **flame mitigation devices** (flame arresters) that
prevent flame jetting and container rupture. **Cans older than that almost certainly lack one.**
Replacing old gas cans is a cheap, concrete safety upgrade: put it on the shopping list (→ 26-09).

**Child-resistant closures:** gasoline cans have had them since 2009; kerosene and diesel
containers since December 2022 (CPSC). {{#if has.child}}With children in this household, buy
containers that have them (→ 20-02).{{else}}Buy containers that have them.{{/if}}

**Never use gasoline to start or restart a fire**, including one you think has gone out (CPSC;
→ 06-01).

## Containers

- **Approved fuel containers only**: designed, vented and sealed for the job (OSHA, Flammable
  Liquids, 29 CFR 1910.106; CPSC).
- **Never glass. Never a food or drink container.** Ever.
- **Label every container** with its fuel and fill date. The usual US colours are **red for
  gasoline, yellow for diesel, blue for kerosene** (→ 29-01), but **the label, not the colour, is
  what you trust**. Read it aloud, smell, then pour (→ 06-06).
- **Leave room at the top.** Fuel expands as it warms.
- **Store outside the living space**, away from anything that sparks and out of children's reach
  (→ 06-06).
- **Fuel inside a passenger vehicle** (in the cabin or an SUV's cargo area) is a trade-off the
  household decides: {{choice DC-06}} → 30-07. Road rules: → 06-08.

## Judging old fuel you find

Old gasoline is the most likely thing you will find, and it can wreck an engine you depend on.

- **Smell it.** Fresh gasoline smells sharp. Stale gasoline smells sour or varnishy, like old
  paint.
- **Look at it** in a clean, clear glass jar against something white. Fresh gasoline is nearly
  clear to pale straw. Dark, orange or cloudy is bad.
- **Look for a water layer** at the bottom. Gasoline with ethanol takes up water and separates. A
  visible layer means it cannot be used as it is.
- **Filter it** through a fine-screen funnel before use.
- **Diesel** keeps longer than gasoline, but microbes grow in it where water collects, and it
  thickens ("gels") in hard cold without treatment.{{#if is.cold_winters}} Your winters are cold
  enough for this.{{/if}}

**Whether to burn old fuel at all is a household decision**, and the jar test above is part of
it: {{choice DC-17}} → 30-18. If you do use questionable fuel, try it first in an engine you do not
depend on, never first in the generator that runs medical equipment or the vehicle you would leave
in.

## Spills

1. **Stop the source, then air the place out.** Vapour is heavier than air and pools low.
2. **Soak it up** with sand, cat litter or absorbent pads. **Do not hose it into a drain.**
3. **Nothing that sparks** until the vapour has cleared: no light switches, no starting a vehicle.
4. **Fuel-soaked clothing comes off outside** and never goes in a dryer.
5. **Skin:** wash with soap and water. **Eyes:** flush with clean water for 15 to 20 minutes.
6. **Swallowed or breathed in:** **Poison Control, 1-800-222-1222**. No phone: → 12-17. Do not make
   the person vomit; get them to fresh air; watch their breathing for a day.

## The legal and ethical line

**Taking fuel from a vehicle that is not yours is theft**, and any working authority will treat it
so. **Your own vehicles and equipment, and fuel you are given or trade for, are not in question**
(→ 22-02, → 18-02).

## When it runs out

- **Gasoline goes stale on the shelf** (→ 06-06). Count it in **hours of generator run time**, not
  gallons (→ 09-04, → 23-02).
- **Batch** every generator run (→ 09-04); keep vehicle fuel for the vehicle.
- **Resupply:** your own equipment first, then propane exchange while it exists, then trade
  (→ 18-02). **There is no renewable source of gasoline**, which is why long-term power plans rest
  on solar (→ 09-02).
- **Propane keeps for years and does not go stale.** A dual-fuel generator run on propane avoids
  every stale-gasoline problem on this page (→ 06-05, → 09-04).{{#if has.generator}} Your
  generator's fuel: {{ gear.generator_fuel }}. Use only what its manual names, and switch fuels by
  its procedure.{{/if}}

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** own the tools, know your vehicles, keep containers labelled and
  rotated.
- **B (on the move):** fuel is range, and range is the plan (→ 01-03). A siphon and a funnel belong
  in the vehicle kit (→ 22-05). Carrying fuel on the road: → 06-08.
- **C (settled somewhere new):** fuel becomes a finite resource. Keep it for setting up (tools,
  pumps, hauling) and run daily life on wood and solar.
