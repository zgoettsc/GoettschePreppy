# Storing fuel safely (gasoline, propane, kerosene)

**What this page is for.** Stored fuel is the most dangerous thing in a prepared house. This
page covers where each fuel is kept, how to label it so nobody pours the wrong one, how to keep
it from going bad, what must never be stored next to it, and how to keep children away from it.

**New to fuels?** Read → 29-01 first: it explains what gasoline, diesel, kerosene and propane
are and how to tell them apart.

> **SAFETY.**
> - **Fuel is never stored or transferred near an open flame or a running appliance.** Vapour
>   ignition.
> - **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products.** Fixed
>   in every source.
> - **Never run a generator, charcoal, or any engine indoors, in a garage, or near an open
>   window.** Carbon monoxide kills in minutes with no warning; alarms fail; there is no
>   mitigation that works every time.
> - **Bleach or pool shock is never mixed with ammonia, acids or other cleaners.** Chlorine gas.
>
> **Gasoline vapour is heavier than air. It flows along the floor and can be lit by a water
> heater's pilot light across the room** (CDC/ATSDR, ToxFAQs for Automotive Gasoline). If you
> smell strong fuel vapour indoors or in a garage: no light switches, no engines, no flames;
> get everyone out; open doors from outside if you can.

## Do this now

1. **Label every can today**, in large letters you can read by headlamp: "GASOLINE — NOT FOR
   HEATERS", "KEROSENE — HEATER ONLY", "DIESEL". **Before any pour: read the label aloud, smell,
   then pour.** Kerosene smells oily, like lamp fuel; gasoline is sharp and stings the eyes.
2. **Keep gasoline and kerosene apart**: not on the same shelf, not touching, a gap you can see.
3. **Propane cylinders outdoors**, upright, valves closed and capped, away from doors, windows
   and vents, and **at least 5 feet from anything that can light them** (NFPA 58).
4. **Never pour or transfer fuel indoors or in a garage.** Carry the can outside, pour, cap it,
   carry it back (→ 06-07).
5. **Never store gasoline in the house or basement. Never store propane cylinders in the house
   or basement**, not even "just for the winter".
6. **Approved fuel containers only**, labelled. Never milk jugs, drink bottles or unlabelled cans.
7. **Away from anything that sparks or burns**: water heater, furnace, pilot lights, electrical
   panel, woodpile.
8. **Pool shock (calcium hypochlorite) far away from all fuels.** It is a strong oxidiser and
   can react dangerously with petroleum (→ 29-05, → 03-08).
9. **A fire extinguisher outside the storage area**, so you never reach through a fire to get it
   (→ 29-12).

## Can colours, and why the label wins

In the US, fuel cans are usually coloured by an industry convention: **red for gasoline, yellow
for diesel, blue for kerosene** (→ 29-01). Many heater makers say the same: one common kerosene
heater manual says store kerosene only in a blue container clearly marked "kerosene", never a red
one (GHP Group/Dyna-Glo, kerosene heater manual).

**Use the convention, but trust the label.** Cans get reused, faded and borrowed, and some
households have cans in other colours. So:

- **Every can is labelled with its fuel**, whatever its colour.
- **A can with a missing or unreadable label is not poured into anything** until you know what is
  in it.
- **Read aloud, smell, then pour.** Gasoline in a kerosene heater causes a flare-up, fire or
  explosion (heater maker's manual).

## The attached garage

The garage is where most families keep fuel, and it is a hard place for it: pilot lights,
igniters, a car engine, tools that spark, and a shared wall and door with the house. Vapour
flowing along the floor finds them. Keeping fuel there is a trade-off the household decides,
with rules for how: {{choice DC-05}} → 30-06.

{{#if has.garage}}Your home has an attached garage. Whatever the choice, the transfer rule holds:
**never pour or transfer fuel inside the garage, power on or off.** A fuel-fired appliance that
cannot light during an outage lights again the moment power returns, and a vapour cloud lasts
longer than the pour.{{/if}}

> **Before the collapse:** model fire codes and your local fire code limit how much gasoline may
> be kept in an attached garage. Ask your fire department while it can answer. The physics is
> the same either way.

## Gasoline: the dangerous one

It is not the liquid that explodes, it is the **vapour**. Gasoline gives off vapour even in the
cold. The vapour is **heavier than air**, so it sinks, flows along the floor and pools in low
spots, and it lights at very low concentrations. A can leaking in a basement corner can be set off
by a furnace igniter on the other side of the room.

- **Detached storage is the standard**: a shed or an outdoor locker, ventilated, shaded, locked.
- **Approved, sealed containers**; newer ones have a flame arrester (a mesh at the spout that
  stops a flame entering the can) (CPSC, Fuel Container Safety). Keep cans out of direct sun.
- **Never fill a can sitting inside a vehicle or on a plastic truck-bed liner**: a static spark
  can light the vapour. Set it on the ground, nozzle touching the can (→ 06-07).
- **Never siphon by mouth.** Use a pump (→ 06-07).

### Gasoline goes stale: months, not years

Gasoline ages. Air and heat break it down, ethanol in pump gas draws water from the air, and
stale gas leaves gum in small engines (Honda Power Equipment, Fuel Recommendations). **Untreated
gasoline keeps for months, not years.** Stabiliser slows this if it is added when the fuel is
fresh; it cannot fix fuel that is already stale, and it has its own shelf life (Honda).

**A simple rotation that works:**
- Add stabiliser **when you fill the can**. Write the date and "STABILISED" on the can.
- **Keep cans full.** Less air in the can means less ageing and less water.
- On a set schedule, **pour each can into a vehicle's tank and refill it fresh**. Put the date on
  the rotation tracker (→ 26-08).
- If ethanol-free gasoline is sold near you, it keeps better in small engines.

**Old gasoline** (or kerosene, or diesel): whether to use it is a decision card with a jar test on
it. {{choice DC-17}} → 30-18.

> **Before the collapse:** gasoline too old to use goes to your county or city household
> hazardous waste collection. Never pour it on the ground, down a drain, or burn it.

**Generator fuel.** Use only the fuel your generator's manual names, and follow its steps for
switching fuels and for storage. Stale gasoline in a carburettor is the usual reason a stored
generator will not start (→ 09-04).{{#if has.generator}} Your generator's fuel: {{ gear.generator_fuel }}.{{/if}}

**Never gasoline in a heater or lamp.** No gasoline ever goes in a kerosene heater or any kerosene
appliance.

## Propane

- Cylinders are tough, but must be **upright, secured, and outdoors**. Heat raises the pressure;
  the relief valve then vents **flammable gas**.
- **At least 5 feet from anything that can light it**: an air-conditioner unit, a running
  generator, a grill in use (NFPA 58).
- Heater makers say the same in their own words: cylinders are stored outdoors, never in a
  building or garage (Enerco Group, portable radiant heater manual). NFPA 58 limits cylinders kept
  inside a home, attached garages included, to the 1-lb class (NFPA 58).
- **Never in a basement.** Propane is **heavier than air** and pools in low spaces.
- **Check for leaks with soapy water** brushed on the connection: growing bubbles mean a leak.
  **Never with a flame.**
- **Valves closed and capped** when not in use. Do not overfill.
- **Cold performance is good**: one of propane's real advantages (→ 06-05).
- Running a heater from a 20-lb cylinder: the cylinder stays outdoors (→ 08-09). A cylinder
  larger than 1 lb indoors is a decision card: {{choice DC-02}} → 30-03.

## Kerosene

Kerosene gives off much less vapour than gasoline, so it is easier to store. It still burns.

**Grades: the difference that matters.**
- **K-1 (also written 1-K): clear, low-sulfur.** **The only grade for any heater used in, or
  vented into, living space** (MU Extension, Unvented Portable Kerosene Heaters, G1999). Buy it
  clear and undyed where you can.
- **K-2 and red-dyed kerosene:** higher sulfur, sootier, more fumes. Outdoor use only; it fouls
  wicks and burners faster. Pump kerosene of unknown grade is treated as K-2.
- Never lamp oil in a heater, never gasoline in anything that burns kerosene.

**Storage.**
- **Labelled cans** (blue is the usual colour), away from anything that sparks, out of the living
  space, **cool and dark**: heat and sunlight age it.
- **Keep cans full.** A part-full can "breathes" damp air as temperatures swing, water condenses
  inside, and water grows sludge that clogs wicks and burners.
- **Date every can** and burn the oldest first.
- **Refuel heaters outdoors, with the heater cold**, never hot or running (→ 08-10).

**The jar test before burning stored kerosene.** Pour a sample into a clean, clear glass jar and
let it stand. Good fuel is **clear and bright**. Water sits as a layer or droplets at the bottom;
contamination shows as cloudiness, yellowing, sludge or anything floating. A common heater maker
says never use kerosene stored from one season to the next (GHP Group/Dyna-Glo manual). Whether
to follow that, or to jar-test and use older fuel, is a decision card: {{choice DC-17}} → 30-18.

**How long your kerosene lasts.** Hours of heat = gallons on hand ÷ the heater's burn rate in
gallons per hour (from its manual). This household: **{{ gear.fuel.kerosene_gal }} gal** ÷ ______
gal per hour = ______ hours. Wick heater manuals warn that turning the wick down to save fuel
makes odour, soot and carbon monoxide. To make the fuel last longer, **heat a smaller room
(→ 08-01), not a smaller flame.**

## Diesel

Diesel behaves like kerosene: little vapour, but water and sludge collect at the bottom of the
can or tank and plug filters, and it thickens in hard cold (→ 29-01). Label it, keep cans full and
sealed, date them, and follow your additive's label. Diesel goes **only** in diesel engines and
in heaters whose exhaust is ducted outdoors.{{#if has.diesel_vehicle}} You have a diesel
vehicle: keep its fuel clearly apart from gasoline.{{/if}}

## What must never be stored together

| Keep apart | Why |
|---|---|
| Fuels and **pool shock (calcium hypochlorite)** | A strong oxidiser plus petroleum is a fire and explosion risk (→ 29-05) |
| Fuels and anything that lights: water heater, furnace, garage heater, electrical panel, engines | Vapour travels to the flame or spark |
| **Kerosene and gasoline** | One wrong pour puts gasoline in a heater: an explosion (→ 08-10) |
| Fuels and living or sleeping space | Vapour, fire, carbon monoxide |
| Fuels and stored food and water | Contamination |
| Pool chemicals, acids and ammonia products, from each other | Toxic gases |

**The food-and-water row matters more than people think.** CDC says water contaminated with fuel
or toxic chemicals **cannot be made safe by boiling or disinfecting** (CDC, How to Make Water Safe
in an Emergency). A fuel spill into stored water ruins the water for good (→ 03-04).

**Pool and cleaning chemicals** cause their own accidents. Store them dry, off the floor, in their
original containers, never where something above them gets watered, liquids never with dry
chemicals, and each type in its own place, with fuel somewhere else again. Check them every few
months for caking, staining, warmth or damaged packaging. If chemicals are ever found reacting
(fuming, hot, bubbling): **do not add water, do not try to neutralise them, do not seal the
container**; keep everyone upwind and away. Poison Control: **1-800-222-1222**; with no phone,
→ 12-17. More on chlorine products: → 29-05.

## Children

- **Locked storage.** A curious small child and an unlocked fuel can is a catastrophe.
- **Never pour fuel into a drink bottle or food container.** This is how children are poisoned
  (CPSC). No exceptions, not even "just for a minute". If a child swallows any fuel or lamp oil:
  **do not make them vomit; call Poison Control, 1-800-222-1222** (→ 12-17).
- **Teach the rule** as an absolute line (→ 20-02).

## How much is sensible

More fuel is more capability and more risk.

- **Gasoline:** only what you will rotate through the **vehicles** and the generator, if it burns
  gasoline. Detached storage is the standard.
- **Propane:** several 20-lb cylinders is a large capability for a modest risk. Kept outdoors, it
  is the safest stored fuel for a home. Dividing it between jobs: → 06-05.
- **Kerosene:** only if you own an appliance that burns it.
- **Diesel:** only if you own a diesel engine or a ducted heater that burns it.
- **Wood is the safest bulk energy store** (→ 06-03): it does not explode, leak or go stale.

**If you do not have it:**
- Metal cabinets and outbuildings can serve as detached storage: ventilated, locked, shaded.
- Sand or cat litter for spills; a shovel to move soaked soil.
- **Do not improvise fuel containers.** This is the one place where "make do" is truly dangerous.

## When it runs out

- **Track quantity and age.** A full can of fuel two years old may not run the generator when you
  need it.
- **Save liquid fuels for the jobs only they can do**: generator runs for medical devices
  (→ 09-06), fast cooking in extreme weather.
- **In a long emergency, liquid fuels do not come back. Wood does.** Plan the change (→ 06-05).
- **Fuel in vehicle tanks:** many modern cars have screens that block siphoning, and fuel-tank work
  is dangerous. Treat getting fuel out of a tank as a real technical job (→ 06-07).

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** a detached shed or outdoor locker is best; nothing in the house or
  basement; propane outdoors. Never pour or transfer in the house or garage.
- **B (on the move):** carrying fuel in a vehicle with people in it is a decision card; the full
  road rules are on → 06-08.
- **C (settled somewhere new):** more room and more distance from neighbours, same rules. Keep
  fuel in a separate structure well away from where people sleep.
