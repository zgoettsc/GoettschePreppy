# Sustaining and banking a fire

**What this page is for.** A fire you keep going is worth far more than one you relight. It
saves matches, lighters, kindling and time. This page covers how to keep a fire steady, how to
"bank" it so it survives the night, where the wood's heat actually goes, and what to do with
the ashes.

> **CAUTION.** **Ashes stay hot for days.** Ash carried out in a paper bag or a plastic bin is
> a classic cause of house fires. **Metal bucket with a lid, on a surface that cannot burn,
> every time.** **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine
> products.** Fixed in every source. Keep an extinguisher or water within reach of every fire
> (→ 29-12).

## Do this now

1. **Bank the fire overnight** instead of letting it die: rake the coals together, cover the
   edges with ash, add a large log. In the morning the coals relight with kindling and no
   match.
2. **Burn dry, seasoned wood only.** Wet wood wastes its energy boiling off its own water and
   builds creosote in the chimney (EPA, Burn Wise; → 06-09).
3. **One fire, many jobs.** Cook, boil water, heat stones, dry clothes and melt snow in the
   same burn (→ 06-04).
4. **Ashes into a metal bucket with a lid.** Never paper, never plastic, never in a shed.
5. **Save the ash**: it has uses (below).

## Keeping it going

- **Coals do the work.** Flames look impressive; a deep bed of coals cooks, gives steady heat
  and relights easily.
- **Feed steadily, not in bursts.** One piece at a time keeps the coal bed stable. Three at
  once smother it and make smoke instead of heat.
- **Air control:** more air burns hotter and faster; less air burns slower and longer. In an
  open fireplace or fire pit you control air by how you place and space the wood.
- **Two logs burn better than one**, because they heat each other. Three is better still: the
  gaps between them are where the fire lives.

## Banking: the skill worth practising

Banking keeps live coals overnight so you wake to a fire, not a chore.

1. Let the fire burn down to a good bed of coals.
2. **Rake the coals into a compact pile.** A mound holds heat far better than a spread.
3. Put **one or two large, dense logs** on top (oak, hickory, ash and other dense woods,
   → 06-03).
4. **Cover the edges with ash.** Ash insulates and slows the fire to a smoulder.
5. In the morning, rake the ash off, expose the coals, add fine kindling and blow gently.

Done well, this holds coals for **8 to 12 hours**. In a long outage, a well-banked fire can mean
lighting one fire a week instead of one a day: a large saving in matches, lighters, tinder and
effort.

**In a wood stove or fireplace indoors:** a fire turned down to smoulder all night makes more
creosote than a small hot fire (EPA, Burn Wise). Bank with dry wood, and check the chimney often
in a season of heavy use (→ 06-09, → 08-08).

## Where the wood's heat actually goes

- **An open fireplace is a poor heater.** Most of its heat goes up the chimney, and some pull
  warm room air out of the house as they burn. Treat an open fireplace as a place to cook and
  boil water, with its heat as a bonus. A wood stove, with a door and an air control, heats far
  better (→ 08-08).
- **A contained fire beats an open one for cooking.** A rocket stove (→ 07-02) does the same
  cooking on **roughly half the wood**: 40 to 60 percent less in field and lab tests, and about
  two-thirds less with a lid, a windscreen and a haybox (an insulated box that finishes the
  cooking without fire).
- **Dry wood is the cheapest efficiency there is.** Below about 20 percent moisture, far more of
  the wood's energy reaches your pot instead of boiling off water (MU Extension, Wood Fuel for
  Heating, G5450; → 06-03).
- **Cook while the fire is already lit.** Every fire lit for one job is waste.

## Ashes: do not just throw them away

- **Hot ashes go in a metal bucket with a lid**, outside, away from anything that burns, for
  several days before you dump them.
- **Uses:** grip on icy paths, a light dressing on acid garden soil, soap-making (→ 13-09),
  scouring greasy pots, and covering each layer in a latrine (→ 13-01).
- **Do not** put large amounts in compost, put them on acid-loving plants, or keep them near
  stored drinking water.

## Fire safety with children

- **Never leave a fire unattended**, indoors or out, while children are awake.
- **A physical barrier** (a screen or fence) around any fire a child can reach.
- **Teach the boundary early**: a marked line nobody small crosses (→ 20-02).
- **Long-handled tools** so nobody reaches over the flame.
- Whether older children tend the fire is the household's call: {{choice DC-24}} → 30-25.

## Gear

**Have now:**

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Metal ash bucket with a tight lid** | Prevents the classic ash fire | And a metal shovel |
| Poker, shovel, tongs | Move coals without reaching in | Long handles |
| Heavy leather gloves | Wood and hot tools | Welding gloves are ideal |
| Bellows or a blow tube | Revives banked coals with no fuel | A length of metal pipe works |
| Grate or andirons | Air underneath burns better | Helps any fire |
| Moisture meter | Shows whether wood is really dry | Cheap; big efficiency gain (→ 06-03) |
| Fire extinguisher and water | Things go wrong | → 29-12 |
| Spark screen | Embers travel, especially in wind | Essential with children near |

**If you do not have it:**

- A metal trash can with a lid becomes an ash bucket.
- Any metal pipe becomes a blow tube; a rolled magazine works as a fan.
- Bricks, blocks or stones build a windbreak and reflect heat back at you.

## When it runs out

This page is itself a way to save: what it saves is ignition (→ 06-01) and wood (→ 06-04).

- **Bank** rather than relight; **batch** every heat job into one burn; use a **contained
  stove** for cooking.
- Nothing here needs restocking. It is a skill, and a skill does not run out. **Practise
  banking now**, while a failure just means lighting the fire again.
- Efficient burning, dry wood and banking together can roughly double how long a woodpile
  lasts.

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** {{#if has.outdoor_fire}}the outdoor fireplace or fire pit is for
  cooking and boiling.{{else}}an outdoor fire is for cooking and boiling.{{/if}} Bank it if you
  will use it again the same day; otherwise put it **fully** out. An unattended outdoor fire
  near fences and neighbours is a real risk.
- **B (on the move):** small, contained fires only, and fully out before you move on: drown,
  stir, drown again, until it is cold to the back of your hand.
- **C (settled somewhere new):** a banked stove overnight is the old rhythm, and how people
  lived through winters before matches were cheap.
