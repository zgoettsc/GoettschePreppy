# Alternative fuels (propane, charcoal, kerosene, biomass)

**What this page is for.** Wood is the long-term fuel, but it is slow and needs seasoning.
Stored fuels such as propane, kerosene and charcoal bridge the gap. This page compares them,
shows how far a tank goes, and explains how to divide a limited supply between cooking, heat and
power so the same tank is not promised three times.

**New to fuels?** Read → 29-01 first. It explains what gasoline, diesel, kerosene, propane,
butane, white gas, lamp oil and charcoal are, how to tell them apart, the usual can colours, and
which can ever be used indoors. This page assumes it.

> **SAFETY.**
> - **Never run a generator, charcoal, or any engine indoors, in a garage, or near an open
>   window.** Carbon monoxide kills in minutes with no warning; alarms fail; there is no
>   mitigation that works every time. Charcoal makes lethal carbon monoxide (CO). CDC: never
>   burn charcoal indoors, and a fireplace is indoors (CDC; → 29-03).
> - **Fuel is never stored or transferred near an open flame or a running appliance.** Vapour
>   ignition.
> - **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products.** Fixed
>   in every source.
> - **Never burn treated or painted wood, plywood, particleboard or trash**: toxic smoke and
>   residue (EPA, Best Wood-Burning Practices; MU Extension, Wood Fuel for Heating).
>
> Every fuel here can kill by CO, fire or explosion. Read → 08-02 and → 06-06 alongside this page.

## Do this now

1. **Propane is the best compact backup for cooking.** It keeps for years in a sealed cylinder,
   lights instantly and makes no smoke. It is limited and you cannot make more.
2. **Charcoal is outdoor-only. Always.**
3. **Stock some now.** Fuel limits water treatment, cooking and warmth all at once. A few 20-lb
   propane cylinders are among the most useful things a household can buy.
4. **Divide your propane on paper** between its jobs (worksheet below).
5. **Plan the move to wood** (→ 06-04). Stored fuels are a bridge; wood is the long game.
6. **Store fuel safely, outside the living space** (→ 06-06).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Propane** | Does not go stale in a sealed cylinder; the cylinder has its own date (→ 29-14) | Excellent | Good, in heaters rated for it | **Only appliances rated for indoor use**, with ventilation | Best compact backup. Cannot be made |
| **Wood** | Years if seasoned and dry | Good | Good | Installed, vented appliance only | Renewable, can be scavenged: the long game (→ 06-03) |

**Old fuel.** Whether to burn kerosene, diesel or gasoline that has been stored a long time is a
trade-off: fresh fuel only, or a jar test first. {{choice DC-17}} → 30-18.

## The shape of it

- **Propane, charcoal, kerosene and gasoline run out.** You will not make more. Spend them on
  the jobs that are hard any other way: a fast meal, boiling water in bad weather, running a
  generator to charge medical devices.
- **Wood and other plant fuel are renewable.** Slower, dirtier, more work, and the only thing
  that lasts.
- **So:** use stored fuels early while you get organised, then move to wood and efficiency
  (→ 07-02 rocket stove) as the emergency goes on. Do not burn through propane being
  comfortable in month one and reach month six with a woodpile you never started seasoning.

## How far a 20-lb propane cylinder goes

A BTU is a unit of heat. A full 20-lb cylinder holds about **430,000 BTU**. One heater maker's
figures show it: its manual gives **48 to 110 hours** per 20-lb cylinder, from high (9,000 BTU/h)
to low (4,000 BTU/h) (Enerco Group, portable radiant heater owner's manual), and 48 × 9,000 is
about 430,000. The US Department of Energy gives propane about 91,000 BTU per gallon (DOE
Alternative Fuels Data Center, Fuel Properties Comparison).

**Check the label first.** Most "20-lb" cylinders at exchange cages are filled to **15 lb**, not 20
(NIST, Liquefied Petroleum Gas 20 Pound Cylinders). A 15-lb fill holds three quarters as much:
about 320,000 BTU (→ 28-04).

**The method:** hours per cylinder = 430,000 (or 320,000 for a 15-lb fill) ÷ the appliance's BTU
per hour, from its label (→ 29-14). Real use is lower in hard cold.

- **Cooking:** read the burner's BTU rating on the stove's label. For example, a burner rated
  10,000 BTU/h would run about **43 hours** on a full 20-lb cylinder at full flame. Real cooking
  rarely runs a burner wide open the whole time. Stove technique: → 07-05.
- **Portable propane heater:** the manual figures above; use your own heater's rating (→ 08-09,
  → 28-04).
- **Generator:** run time per cylinder depends entirely on the model and the load. Use your
  generator's manual (→ 09-04, → 28-03).{{#if has.generator}} Your generator: {{ gear.generator_watts }} running watts, fuel: {{ gear.generator_fuel }}.{{/if}}

## The same tank, promised three times

The common trap: the same propane is "the generator supply", "the cooking backup" and "the
heater fuel" in three different plans. **Divide it on paper** before the outage, and recheck

**Things that help:**
- Decide which job each cylinder serves, and write what it may **not** be spent on.
- Know what can stand in for propane in each job. Gasoline may stand in for it in a dual-fuel
  generator; kerosene may stand in for it as heat. Often **nothing stands in for propane as
  quick, smoke-free cooking**, so keep enough back for cooking.
- Write a **refill trigger**: for example, the first empty cylinder is refilled that same week
  while refills still exist.
- Keep a **reserve** you never spend on comfort: for storms, illness and a quick meal for a sick
  child.

**Two propane decisions on other pages.** Refilling small 1-lb cylinders from a 20-lb tank:

Fire codes based on NFPA 58, the propane code, limit cylinders kept inside a home to the 1-lb
class (NFPA 58; Minnesota State Fire Marshal, LP Gas Inside Buildings).

## Charcoal: useful, and lethal indoors

Charcoal is tempting: cheap, keeps for years if dry, and gives steady, controllable cooking heat.
**It also makes a lot of CO**, and CDC says never burn it indoors (CDC, Carbon Monoxide Poisoning:
Basics). A fireplace is indoors. **Outdoors only, every time, whatever the weather.** If it is too
cold to cook outside, use a different method, not a different room.

**You can make charcoal** from wood by burning it with little air. In a long emergency it is a
useful skill: charcoal is lighter than the wood it came from, keeps better, and burns hotter and
cleaner.

## Homemade and scavenged fuels

- **Paper logs:** soak shredded paper and cardboard, press into logs, dry them **completely**.
  Free, but they burn quickly.
- **Compressed sawdust logs:** worth stockpiling now: dense, dry, no seasoning, compact.
- **Untreated pallets and scrap lumber:** plentiful in town (→ 06-04). Pull the nails and make
  sure it is untreated. Treated lumber is often green-tinged or stamped, and burning it is
  poisonous.
- **Never burn:** pressure-treated, painted or stained wood; plywood, OSB or particleboard;
  driftwood; railroad ties; plastics; foam; trash.

## The cold-weather footnote

**Butane canister stoves largely stop working below freezing**: butane boils at 31 °F, so below
that it will not turn to gas. **Propane keeps working** in far colder weather: it boils at −44 °F
(NIOSH Pocket Guide, n-Butane and Propane).{{#if is.cold_winters}} With your cold winters,
that alone makes propane the better backup stove fuel.{{/if}}

## Gear

**Have now:**

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 20-lb propane cylinders (several) and adapter hoses | Best compact cooking backup | Stored outdoors or detached (→ 06-06) |
| 1-lb propane cylinders | Portable; the size some indoor-rated heaters take | Refilling them is a decision (→ 30-04) |
| Charcoal, in dry bags | Steady outdoor cooking heat | **Outdoor use only** |
| Compressed sawdust logs | Dense, dry, no seasoning | Store dry |
| Kerosene (K-1) and a heater rated for indoor use | High heat output | Ventilation and a CO alarm (→ 08-10) |
| Fuel stabiliser | Slows gasoline ageing | Add when the fuel is fresh; date the can (→ 06-06) |
| Approved, labelled fuel cans | Safety; not mixing fuels up | → 06-06 |
| **CO alarm** | Any fuel, any time | → 08-02 |

**If you do not have it, scavenge:**
- **Untreated pallets, scrap lumber, storm debris**: the biggest fuel source in town (→ 06-04).
- **Paper and cardboard**, pressed into logs.
- **Part-used propane cylinders** from grills are common and often overlooked.
- Wood you turn into **charcoal** yourself keeps better than the wood did.

## When it runs out

- **Count cylinders in cooking days**, not pounds: how many days of meals one cylinder cooks for
  your household. Write it on → 23-02.
- **Save propane for what is hard with wood**: boiling water fast in a blizzard, cooking when it
  is dangerous to be outside, a quick meal for a sick child. Do ordinary cooking on wood
  (→ 07-01, → 07-02).
- **Wood can be found and grown; propane and kerosene cannot.** Every efficiency gain (rocket
  stove, banked fire, batch cooking) stretches both.
- **Decide in advance what part of the propane you will never spend on convenience.**

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** propane plus an outdoor wood fire (→ 06-04) covers nearly
  everything. Scrap wood in town is plentiful.
- **B (on the move):** a small propane or multi-fuel stove and a little fuel; gather wood at
  stops. Butane fails in winter.
- **C (settled somewhere new):** wood becomes the main fuel and propane becomes precious. Start
  making and storing charcoal, and cut and season wood a year ahead.
