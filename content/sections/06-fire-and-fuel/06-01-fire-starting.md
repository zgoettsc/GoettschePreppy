# Starting fire reliably (multiple methods)

**What this page is for.** Without power, fire cooks food, makes water safe and keeps people
warm. It is also the fastest way to lose a house. This page covers when not to light a fire,
how to build one that catches the first time, and which ways of lighting it still work when it
is cold and wet.

> **CAUTION.** **Never burn treated, painted or pressure-treated wood, plywood, particleboard,
> plastics or trash**: the smoke is toxic (EPA, Best Wood-Burning Practices). Inside the house, fire for heat or
> cooking belongs only in an installed, vented fireplace or wood stove, because every flame
> makes carbon monoxide (CO), a gas you cannot see or smell (CDC; → 29-03). **Never use
> gasoline to start or restart a fire** (CPSC; → 29-01). **Fuel is never stored or transferred
> near an open flame or a running appliance.** Vapour ignition. **Children do not have access
> to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products.** Fixed in every source. Lighters and
> matches count as fuel here.

## Do this now

1. **Keep three different ways to light a fire, in three places**: lighters, storm-proof
   matches, and a ferrocerium rod (below).
2. **Keep manufactured tinder** (fire-starter cubes or similar). It works wet, cold and
   one-handed, which is exactly when you need it.
3. **Practise once in bad weather now.** Lighting a fire with numb hands in freezing wind is a
   different skill from lighting one in the yard in summer.
4. **Keep dry kindling inside**, under cover, before every storm.
5. **Clear the spot**: nothing that burns within a few feet, and water or an extinguisher
   beside you (→ 29-12).

## The gate: when not to light an outdoor fire

Knowing how to light a fire includes knowing when the answer is no. Run these checks before
any open fire outdoors.

| Check | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Burn ban?** | Counties and states ban open burning in dry spells because the grass and brush really will carry fire. Under a ban: contained appliances only (a camp stove outdoors), no open flame. The reason holds whether or not anyone enforces it |
| **Red Flag Warning?** | The National Weather Service's warning for **critical fire weather: strong wind plus very low humidity over dry fuels** (the glossary's example criteria: sustained wind of 15 mph or more and humidity of 25 percent or less, after a dry spell). Criteria vary by state and office (NWS Glossary, Red Flag Warning). **On a Red Flag day, no open fire**, no ash dumping outside and no spark-making work outdoors (→ 17-05) |
| **Wind right now?** | Wind carries embers past any fire ring you control. Pick a wind limit before you need it, lower than the conditions that bring a Red Flag Warning, and write it here: no open fire above ______ mph |
| **Dry-fuel season?** | Any time the grass is brown and cured, and any drought. Treat every other check one step more cautiously |

## Why fires fail

A fire needs fuel, heat and air. Fires almost always fail for one of three ordinary reasons:
**wet fuel**, **too big too fast**, or **not enough air**. Build small, build dry, leave gaps.

## The fuel ladder: the part beginners skip

You need **four sizes**, all ready **before** you strike anything:

1. **Tinder** catches a spark: a cotton ball smeared with petroleum jelly, birch bark, dryer
   lint, fatwood shavings, char cloth, a commercial fire starter.
2. **Kindling**: from pencil-lead to pencil thickness. Dry twigs, thin split shavings.
3. **Small fuel**: finger to wrist thickness.
4. **Fuel wood**: the real logs (→ 06-03).

**Have three times more kindling than you think you need.** Running back for more while a weak
flame dies is the most common failure.

## Ways to light it, ranked for reliability

| Method | Reliability | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Butane lighter** | High, when warm | **Fails in the cold**: butane boils at 31 °F, so below freezing it barely turns to gas (NIOSH Pocket Guide, n-Butane). Keep one in an inside pocket against your body |
| **Storm-proof or waterproof matches** | High | Work cold and wet; there are only so many. Keep them in a waterproof case with the striker |
| **Ferrocerium ("ferro") rod** | Very high | A metal rod that throws hot sparks when scraped. Works wet and cold, for a great many strikes. **Needs good tinder** and practice |
| **Magnifying lens** | Depends on weather | Free and unlimited, but needs bright sun, which winter often does not give |
| **Battery and steel wool** | Good improvised method | A 9-volt battery touched to fine steel wool lights it at once. **Store them apart**: together in a drawer they are a fire hazard |
| **Bow drill or other friction** | Low | Hard, especially in damp cold. Learn it; do not rely on it |

**The cold-weather lesson:** a lighter kept in your pocket works; a lighter left in a cold shed
often will not. Keep ignition warm and dry, on your body.

## Building it: a method that works in the wet

1. **Platform.** Lay split wood or bark so the tinder is not on cold, wet ground or snow.
2. **Windbreak.** Your body, a wall of split wood, or the fireplace itself.
3. **Tinder bundle** on the platform.
4. **Kindling** over it in a teepee or lean-to, with air gaps.
5. **Light the tinder low, on the side the wind is coming from**, and shield it.
6. **Feed small first.** Add bigger pieces only once the flame is steady.
7. **Do not smother it.** Most dying fires need air and patience, not more wood.

**In snow or rain**, the platform matters more than anything else. A fire on snow melts itself
out. Build up, not down.

## Tinder you can make or find

- **Cotton balls and petroleum jelly** in a pill bottle: cheap, nearly waterproof, burn for
  minutes.
- **Fatwood**: resin-rich pine. Lights when damp and shaves easily.
- **Birch bark**: burns even when wet.
- **Dryer lint** from natural fibres, **char cloth**, waxed cardboard, egg-carton-and-wax pucks.
- **Cattail fluff, dry grass, milkweed down** where they grow.

## Gear

**Have now:**

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Lighters, a bag of them | Easiest method; cheap; good barter | Keep some on your body in winter |
| Storm-proof matches and a waterproof case | Work when lighters fail | Include the striker |
| Large ferrocerium rod and striker | A great many lights | Practise **before** you need it |
| Commercial tinder or fire-starter cubes | Work wet, cold, one-handed | Cheap; keep plenty |
| Fatwood | Natural, keeps indefinitely, lights damp | |
| Dry kindling stored inside | The real bottleneck | Restock before every storm |
| Hatchet and knife | Dry wood is **inside** wet wood: split it to reach it | → 16-01, → 16-02 |
| Fire extinguisher and a bucket of water | Things go wrong | Beside the fire, not behind it (→ 29-12) |
| Metal ash bucket with a lid | **Hot ashes start house fires.** USFA: metal container with a lid, outside, at least 10 feet from the house | Never a cardboard box or plastic bin (→ 06-02) |

**If you do not have it:**

- A 9-volt battery and steel wool is the best improvised way to light a fire in a house.
- Petroleum jelly, lip balm, candle wax and cooking oil all help weak tinder burn longer.
- Paper, cardboard and untreated wood scraps are everywhere in town (→ 06-04).
- **Never gasoline.** It flashes, explodes and kills people every year (CPSC; → 29-01).

## When it runs out

- **Count** lighters and matches. A ferro rod lasts for a great many strikes, which is why it
  belongs in every kit: it turns something you use up into something that lasts.
- **Use one lighter until it is empty** rather than starting several.
- **Keep a fire going rather than relighting it** (→ 06-02). Banked coals save lighters,
  matches and kindling all at once.
- Lighters and matches are common things to scavenge and good to barter (→ 18-02).

## Fire indoors

A fireplace or wood stove is the only indoor place for a wood fire. Before the first fire of
the season, check the chimney (→ 06-09). Using one that has not been inspected this year is a
trade-off: {{choice DC-21}} → 30-22. Candles and open-flame cooking indoors are a separate
trade-off: {{choice DC-20}} → 30-21. Keep CO alarms working in every room with a flame
(→ 08-02).
