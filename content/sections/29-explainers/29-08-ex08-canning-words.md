# Canning words, explained from the beginning

**What this page is for.** Home canning keeps food for years without power, and done wrong it
makes a poison you cannot see, smell or taste. Every canning rule comes from a few words. This
page explains them so the tested recipes in the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning make sense.
It does not give process times: those come only from a tested recipe (→ 04-04, → 04-05).

> **SAFETY — the floor that applies (→ 30-01).** **Low-acid foods are pressure-canned to the
> published time and pressure, or not canned.** Botulism has no field treatment and the food
> looks and smells normal.

## Botulism in one paragraph

*Clostridium botulinum* is a germ that lives in soil and on vegetables as tough **spores** that
survive boiling. In a sealed jar with no air, little acid and room temperature, the spores wake
up and make **botulinum toxin**, one of the most poisonous substances known. It causes paralysis
that starts with the eyes, face and swallowing and can stop breathing. **The jar can look, smell
and taste normal** (USDA; CDC). Acid stops the spores growing; very high heat kills them; heat
also destroys the toxin. That is the whole logic of canning.

## The words

- **pH** — a scale of how acidic something is, from 0 to 14. **Lower is more acidic**; 7 is
  neutral (plain water). Lemon juice and vinegar are low; meat and most vegetables are near
  neutral.
- **High-acid food** — pH **4.6 or lower**: most fruit, pickles, jams, and tomatoes **with added
  lemon juice or citric acid**. Botulism spores cannot grow, so a **boiling-water canner** is
  enough (USDA, Principles of Home Canning).
- **Low-acid food** — pH **above 4.6**: vegetables, beans, meat, poultry, fish, soups, and plain
  tomatoes with nothing added. These **must** be processed in a **pressure canner** (USDA).
- **Boiling-water canner** (water bath) — a big pot with a rack; jars covered by boiling water.
  Water cannot get hotter than boiling: **212°F at sea level**, and lower the higher you live.
- **Pressure canner** — a heavy pot with a locking lid, a vent, and a **gauge**. Holding steam
  under pressure raises the temperature to **about 240°F to 250°F**, which kills botulism spores
  in the tested time (NCHFP, Using Pressure Canners). USDA processes for low-acid vegetables have
  used pressure only since 1943.
- **Pressure cooker vs. pressure canner** — a pressure cooker cooks dinner. **Many pressure
  cookers and electric multi-cookers are not tested for canning** and may not hold the
  temperature for long enough. Use equipment the maker states is for canning.
- **Dial gauge vs. weighted gauge** — a **dial gauge** shows pressure on a needle and must be
  tested yearly; one that reads more than 2 psi off is replaced. A **weighted gauge** rocks or
  jiggles at its set pressure and needs no testing (NCHFP).
- **psi** — pounds per square inch, the unit of pressure on the gauge.
- **Altitude adjustment** — because water boils cooler up high, recipes add time (water bath) or
  pressure (pressure canner) above 1,000 ft. **The recipe's altitude table governs.**
  {{#if is.high_altitude}}Your home is above 5,000 ft: every recipe needs its altitude row.{{else}}Your home elevation is about {{ region.home.elevation_ft }} ft; find that row in every recipe.{{/if}}
- **Venting** — letting steam pour out of a pressure canner for the time the recipe says before
  closing the vent, to push out the air. Trapped air means a lower temperature.
- **Headspace** — the empty space between the food and the top of the jar, set by the recipe.
  Too little and food bubbles out and the lid fails; too much and air stays in.
- **Hot pack / raw pack** — filling jars with hot, cooked food or with raw food. The recipe's
  time is tested for one or the other, not both.
- **The seal** — as the jar cools, a vacuum pulls the lid down. A sealed lid is concave and does
  not flex or pop when pressed in the centre. **Unsealed after 24 hours:** refrigerate and eat, or
  reprocess with a new lid.
- **Tested recipe** — a recipe whose time, pressure, jar size and pack were measured in a
  laboratory. USDA and the National Center for Home Food Preservation publish them. **A family
  recipe card is not a tested recipe.**

## Before eating home-canned low-acid food

CDC's backstop: boil it hard in an open pan **10 minutes, plus 1 minute for every 1,000 ft of
elevation**, liquid included, before eating (CDC, Botulism Prevention). This destroys the toxin.
It does not rescue a jar that is **bulging, leaking, spurting, mouldy or foul-smelling**: those
are thrown out unopened, where children and animals cannot reach them.
