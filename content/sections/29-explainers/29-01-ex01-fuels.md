# Fuels, explained from the beginning

**What this page is for.** Most fuel accidents happen because someone did not know which liquid
was in the can, or did not know that a fuel is only safe in the appliance built for it. This
page says what each common fuel is, what burns it, how long it keeps, whether it can ever be
used indoors and why, and how to tell them apart.

> **SAFETY — the floors that apply (→ 30-01).**
> - **Never run a generator, charcoal, or any engine indoors, in a garage, or near an open
>   window.** Carbon monoxide kills in minutes with no warning; alarms fail; there is no
>   mitigation that works every time.
> - **Fuel is never stored or transferred near an open flame or a running appliance.** Vapour
>   ignition.
> - **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products.** Fixed in
>   every source.

## Two ideas that explain every fuel rule

1. **Everything that burns makes carbon monoxide (CO)**, a gas you cannot see or smell (CDC,
   Carbon Monoxide Poisoning: Basics). Only an appliance built and rated for indoor use, run the
   way its manual says, belongs inside. → 29-03
2. **The vapour burns, not the liquid.** Gasoline gives off vapour even when cold. The vapour is
   heavier than air, flows along the floor and can be lit by a pilot light or a spark across the
   room. Kerosene and diesel give off far less vapour, which is why they are easier to store, but
   they still burn.

## What each fuel is, and how to tell them apart

| Fuel | What it is | What burns it | How to recognise it | Usual can colour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Gasoline** | Liquid fuel refined from crude oil. Most US pump gasoline is about 10% ethanol (EIA) | Cars, lawn and garden engines, most portable generators | Sharp smell that stings the eyes; very thin; evaporates fast | Red |
| **Diesel** | A heavier "distillate" fuel oil for diesel engines; road diesel is ultra-low sulfur, 15 ppm or less (EIA) | Diesel cars and trucks, tractors, diesel generators, ducted diesel heaters that vent outdoors | Oily, heavy smell; feels greasy; off-road diesel and **heating oil** are dyed red by IRS rule (EIA) | Yellow |
| **Kerosene** | A lighter distillate than diesel. **1-K (K-1)** is the clear, low-sulfur grade (University of Missouri Extension, G1999) | Wick kerosene heaters, kerosene lamps, some ducted heaters | Oily smell like lamp fuel; K-1 is water-clear. Red-dyed or yellowed kerosene is not K-1 | Blue |
| **Propane** | A gas at normal pressure that turns liquid under pressure, so it is sold in steel cylinders (EIA) | Grills, camp stoves, portable propane heaters, some generators, house furnaces on tank service | Comes only in pressure cylinders or tanks. A smell is added so leaks are noticed: if you smell gas, get everyone out (FEMA, Utility Shut-off and Safety) | White, grey or steel cylinder |
| **Butane** | A close relative of propane, also stored as a liquid under pressure (EIA) | Small canister stoves, lighters | Small thin metal canisters | Printed canister |
| **White gas** ("camp fuel") | A very volatile refined petroleum liquid, as dangerous to store as gasoline | Liquid-fuel camp stoves and pressurised lanterns | Clear, solvent smell; sold in metal cans labelled camp fuel | Printed metal can |
| **Lamp oil** | Refined liquid paraffin for oil lamps. "Tiki" or citronella oil is a different product, outdoor only | Oil lamps made for it; K-1 kerosene also burns in flat-wick kerosene lamps | Clear or coloured; often sold in bottles that look like drinks | Plastic bottle |
| **Wood** | Logs, split and dried ("seasoned") | Fireplaces, wood stoves, outdoor fires | Seasoned wood is lighter, cracked at the ends, and sounds hollow when two pieces are knocked together | — |
| **Charcoal** | Wood already partly burned, sold as lumps or briquettes | Outdoor grills only | Black, light, dusty | Paper bag |

**Colours are a habit, not a guarantee.** Red for gasoline, yellow for diesel and blue for
kerosene are the usual US convention; your heater's manual may name a colour too. But cans get
reused and households use their own colours. **The label is
what you trust:** read it aloud, smell it, then pour. A can with no readable label is not poured
into anything until you know what is in it. → 29-14

## How long each keeps, and whether it can ever be used indoors

| Fuel | How long it keeps | Indoors? | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gasoline** | Months, not years. Air and heat age it; ethanol pulls water from the air; stabiliser slows ageing but cannot fix stale fuel and has its own shelf life (Honda Power Equipment, Fuel Recommendations) | **Never.** Not in a heater, a lamp or a stove | Too volatile. In a kerosene heater it is an explosion. Engines that burn it make lethal CO (floor above) |
| **Diesel** | Ages too: water collects at the bottom of a tank and it thickens in hard cold. Follow your additive's label | **Only** in a heater whose exhaust is ducted outdoors, installed as its manual says | Burning it in the room puts the exhaust in the room |
| **Kerosene** | Keeps longer than gasoline when full, sealed and dark, but water creeps in. Before use, a sample in a clear jar must be clear, with no water layer and no cloudiness. How old is too old is a choice → 30-18 | **Only** K-1, only in a heater or lamp made for indoor use, with the ventilation its manual requires and a CO alarm | Unvented heaters put all their exhaust into the room. Overnight use is a choice → 30-02 |
| **Propane** | Does not go stale in a sealed cylinder; the cylinder itself has a date (→ 29-14) | **Only** appliances rated for indoor use. The propane code (NFPA 58) allows only 1-lb cylinders to be kept inside a home, two at most (Flame King filling training, citing NFPA 58) | A leaking valve lays heavy gas along the floor where a pilot light can light it. Larger cylinders inside → 30-03 |
| **Butane** | Keeps sealed; check the canister | **No.** CDC: never use a portable camp stove indoors | Fails in freezing weather because it stops turning to gas |
| **White gas** | Keeps sealed | **No** | Same reasons as gasoline. Never in a wick lamp or kerosene heater |
| **Lamp oil** | Keeps sealed | In a lamp made for it, in a ventilated room | A swallowed mouthful can enter the lungs. **Do not make the person vomit; call Poison Control, 1-800-222-1222** (CDC MMWR; Poison Control) → 12-17 |
| **Wood** | Years if dry; split, covered and aged at least 6 months before it burns well (EPA, Burn Wise tips) | **Only** in an installed, vented fireplace or wood stove | The chimney carries the smoke and CO out. Never burn trash or treated wood (EPA) |
| **Charcoal** | Years if dry | **Never.** Not in a fireplace, not "for a while" | Very high CO output (CDC). This is a floor |

## If the wrong fuel goes in

- **Gasoline or white gas in a kerosene heater or lamp:** flare-up, uncontrolled flame,
  explosion. Do not light it. Outdoors, cold, drain it, new wick (→ 08-10).
- **Gasoline in a diesel engine, or diesel in a gasoline engine:** do not start it. Drain the
  tank. The vehicle or generator manual says which fuel it takes.
- **Any fuel in a drink bottle:** this is how children are poisoned. Never decant fuel into a
  food or drink container (CPSC, Fuel Container Safety).
- **Gasoline to start or restart a fire:** never (CPSC).
