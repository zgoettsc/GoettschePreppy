# Fire extinguishers, fire blankets and alarms, explained from the beginning

**What this page is for.** In an outage, a household burns more things indoors (heaters, lamps,
candles, stoves) and the fire department may not come. This page explains the alarm that wakes
you, the extinguisher that stops a small fire, the blanket that smothers one, and the moment to
stop fighting and leave.

> **SAFETY.** **People first, then the fire.** A home fire can fill a house with smoke very
> fast. If the fire is bigger than a wastebasket, spreading, or between you and the exit, **get
> everyone out, stay out, and call 911 from outside** (USFA, Choosing and Using Fire
> Extinguishers). Young children and older adults should not use extinguishers (USFA).

## Smoke alarms

- **Where:** inside each bedroom, outside each sleeping area, and on every level including the
  basement (USFA, Smoke Alarms).
- **Test monthly** with the test button. Replace 9-volt batteries yearly; hard-wired alarms need
  their backup battery replaced yearly. **Replace the whole alarm 10 years from the manufacture
  date** printed on it (USFA).
- **Two kinds of sensor:** ionization (quicker to flaming fires) and photoelectric (quicker to
  smouldering fires). Because you cannot predict the fire, have both kinds or dual-sensor alarms
  (USFA).
- **Smoke alarms do not detect carbon monoxide.** A combination alarm does both; otherwise you
  need separate CO alarms. → 29-03

## Fire classes

An extinguisher's label shows the letters of the fires it is for (USFA):

| Class | Fire in | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Ordinary materials | Wood, paper, cloth, bedding |
| **B** | Flammable liquids | Gasoline, oil, grease, oil-based paint |
| **C** | Electrical equipment that is plugged in | Appliances, tools, power strips |
| **D** | Burning metals | Workshop and industrial metals |
| **K** | Cooking oils and fats in cooking appliances | Deep fryers |

A household **"ABC"** extinguisher covers the first three. The numbers before the letters rate
its size: on "1A:10B:C", the 1A matches 1.25 gallons of water on a class A fire and the 10B
covers a liquid fire of about 10 square feet (USFA). A bigger number is a bigger fire.

## Using an extinguisher: PASS

Use it **only if** everyone else is out or leaving, 911 has been called, the fire is small and
not spreading, you are not breathing smoke, and **your back is to a clear exit** (USFA). Then:

1. **Pull** the pin, nozzle pointing away from you.
2. **Aim** low, at the base of the fire, not the flames.
3. **Squeeze** the lever slowly and evenly.
4. **Sweep** side to side across the base until it is out.

Small extinguishers empty in seconds. If it is not out when the extinguisher is empty, leave.
Watch the spot afterwards: fires can re-light. Check that the gauge needle is in the green; some types need shaking monthly and pressure testing every few years (USFA).

## Fire blankets

A fire blanket is a sheet of fire-resistant fabric in a quick-pull pouch. It smothers a small
fire by cutting off air: laid over a burning pan (heat off, leave it covered until cold), or
wrapped around a person whose clothes are burning after they **stop, drop and roll**. Read its
pouch now, before you need it. **Never throw water on burning fat or oil.** Put a lid or the
blanket on it and turn off the heat. A wool blanket can stand in; synthetic blankets melt. → 12-03

## When to leave instead

- The fire is spreading, or bigger than you can cover with one extinguisher.
- Smoke is filling the room, or you cannot see the exit.
- It is a **lithium battery** fire (power station, e-bike, tool pack): it burns fiercely, makes
  toxic smoke and can re-light. Get out and call for help (NFPA, Lithium-Ion Batteries). → 29-02
- Fuel is involved and the fire is near more fuel. → 29-01

**Plan two ways out of every room** and a meeting place outside, and practise it. Close doors
behind you: a closed door slows fire and smoke.
