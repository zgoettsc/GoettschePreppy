# Measuring without instruments, explained from the beginning

**What this page is for.** Many survival jobs depend on a measurement: how much water is left,
how much bleach goes in, whether the freezer is still cold, how far the next town is. When the
scale's battery is dead and the phone is off, you can still measure, by **calibrating** ordinary
things against something whose size you know. Do the calibrating now, while you still have
instruments, and write the results on this page. Unit conversions are on → 25-02.

> **Where a number keeps someone safe** (a bleach dose, a cooking temperature, a canning time),
> measure it properly or use the published method that needs no instrument: a rolling boil
> (→ 03-04), drops from a dropper (→ 29-05), a tested recipe (→ 29-08). Do not estimate those.

## The idea: one known thing

Every method below works the same way. You find **one thing whose size is known**, compare the
unknown thing to it, and count. Then you write the answer down so nobody has to do it twice.

## Volume

- **Mark a bucket.** Pour in a known measure (a 1-gallon jug, a 1-litre bottle) one at a time
  and draw a line at each level with a marker. That bucket now measures water, feed and fuel.
- **Labels carry volumes:** water jugs, milk jugs, soda bottles, paint cans, the water heater's
  data plate ({{ dwelling.water_heater_gallons }} gal for yours).
- **A box of known size:** volume = length × width × height. One cubic foot holds about 7.5
  gallons (→ 25-02).
- **Firewood:** a cord is a stack 4 ft high, 4 ft deep and 8 ft long, 128 cubic feet
  (University of Missouri Extension, Wood Fuel for Heating). A tape measure is the scale.
- **Small amounts:** a medicine dropper or syringe from a first-aid kit is your most accurate
  small measure. Kitchen spoons vary; check one against the dropper's marks.

## Weight

- **Water is the reference weight:** a gallon of water weighs about 8.34 lb (→ 25-02). Two
  full gallon jugs are close to 17 lb.
- **A balance:** a board over a round log or pipe, the unknown on one end, jugs of water on the
  other, slid until it balances. Count the jugs.
- **A bathroom scale for heavy things:** weigh yourself holding it, then without it, and
  subtract.
- **Count what is packed:** cans and bags carry their weight on the label; a pantry can be
  weighed by counting labels.

## Temperature

- **Check a thermometer** in a glass packed with ice and a little water, stirred: it should read
  32°F. In boiling water it reads 212°F at sea level, less the higher you live.
- **The fridge and freezer:** a refrigerator should be at 40°F or below and a freezer at 0°F or
  below. With the door shut, a fridge keeps food safe about 4 hours without power and a full
  freezer about 48 hours (FoodSafety.gov, Food Safety During a Power Outage). Ice crystals still
  in frozen food are a useful sign; a cheap appliance thermometer is the real answer.
- **Hand feel is not a thermometer.** Cooked meat is safe at the temperature on the published
  chart, not by colour (FoodSafety.gov, Safe Minimum Internal Temperatures). Keep one probe
  thermometer with spare batteries, and a min/max thermometer for medicine (→ 29-07).

## Distance

- **Your pace.** Walk a measured 100 feet (along a tape, or a known length such as a lot line
  from your deed) and count your steps. Do it on rough ground and with a pack too. Write it down:
  _____ steps per 100 ft.
- **Time it.** Walk for 15 minutes on a road whose length you know and work out your own walking
  speed, then your speed carrying a load and with children. Write both down. → 24-04
- **Maps** have a scale bar: mark its length on a strip of paper and step it along the route.
- **Vehicles:** the odometer, while the car still runs.

## Time

- **A wind-up or battery wall clock** outlasts phones. Keep one, with batteries.
- **Count seconds** ("one-one-thousand, two-one-thousand") against a clock now, until your count
  matches; then you can time a minute without one.
- **Measure what burns:** light a candle or an oil lamp with a full tank and note how many hours
  it lasts. That tells you hours of light per candle or per fill.
- **The sun** is highest at local noon, when shadows are shortest. A stick in the ground makes a
  rough clock.
