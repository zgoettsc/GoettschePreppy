# Cash, value and a degraded economy

**What this page is for.** Having money you can use when card machines, ATMs and phone payments do
not work, and understanding what holds value as a disruption gets longer. **The single most useful
fact here: in the first hours and days of almost any disruption, cash works and cards do not.** Card
readers, ATMs and payment networks all need power and connections (→ 17-08). Shops that stay open go
cash-only, and ATMs empty fast. Most households have no cash at all.

> **CAUTION. Cash is also a security item** (→ 14-03). Keep it discreet, split up, and do not talk
> about it. **Never type account numbers, PINs or passwords into this app**; anything like that is
> written by hand on paper kept separately (→ 19-07).

## Do this now

1. **Get cash in small bills:** fives, tens and twenties, **not hundreds**, because nobody will make
   change, and trying shows you have more.
2. **Split it:** a main reserve in your most secure place at home, and smaller amounts in the grab
   folder (→ 19-01), each go-bag (→ 22-03) and {{#if has.vehicle}}each vehicle (→ 22-05){{else}}any
   vehicle you use{{/if}}. Never all in one place, and never all behind a lock that takes minutes.
3. **Write on paper** the names and phone numbers of your banks, credit unions and card issuers, and
   keep it in the grab folder (→ 19-01).
4. **Have more than one way to reach money:** two institutions, a credit card with room on it, and
   cash. One point of failure is a real risk (→ 19-06).

> **Before the collapse:** bank deposits are insured by the FDIC, and credit union deposits by the
> NCUA, up to $250,000 per depositor, per institution, per ownership category (FDIC,
> Understanding Deposit Insurance; NCUA, Share Insurance Coverage). Know which insurer covers each
> of your accounts.

## How a broken economy goes

| Stage | What works | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | Cards, phone payments, cash | **Prepare now** |
| **Hours to days** (outage, storm) | **Cash only.** ATMs down or empty; card readers dead | This is what the cash is for |
| Weeks (regional disruption) | Cash, but prices rise sharply and some goods are gone | Cash still works; scarcity is the problem |
| Months (severe) | Cash matters less as supply fails; goods and skills start to matter (→ 18-02, → 18-03) | Barter grows beside cash |
| Long-term collapse | Barter, reciprocity, local exchange | Skills and relationships are the currency (→ 18-01) |

**Most real events stop at the second or third stage.** Cash covers the great majority of realistic
scenarios, which makes it the most valuable financial preparation by far.

## How much cash, and how

- **Small bills.**
- **Enough for what?** A useful measure: a full tank of fuel, a few days of food and supplies, and a
  night's lodging, per adult. Adjust to what you can afford.
- **Split it** (step 2 above).
- **Keep some in the vehicle**: evacuation is when you will want it (→ 01-03).
- **Dry and discreet:** a waterproof bag, not an obvious place.
- **A few coins** for vending machines and parking.
- **Do not talk about it** (→ 14-03).

## Banks and access

From the CFPB's disaster guidance (CFPB, Disasters and Emergencies) and the FDIC:
- **Two institutions are better than one**: a bank and a credit union, ideally.
- **Know how to reach each without the internet**: a phone number and a branch address, on paper.
- **Know your available credit.** A card with room on it is a resource while payment systems work.
- **Know what is on automatic payment** and what happens if it fails. Creditors are often
  accommodating after a disaster if you contact them early (CFPB).
- **Disaster financial help exists** (USA.gov, Find Disaster Financial Help). Write down where to
  find it (→ 25-03).

## What holds value: the honest version

- **Cash is the most useful store of value for realistic scenarios.** It works in the stages where
  nearly everything actually lands.
- **Precious metals** are a long-term store of value, not a practical way to pay in a crisis. When
  cash has failed, nobody makes change for a gold coin, and most people cannot tell real from fake.
  They also mark you as worth robbing (→ 14-03). **This binder recommends no investment**; buying
  metals is a financial decision, not a preparedness one.
- **The best stores of value in a long disruption are goods and skills** (→ 18-02, → 18-03), and
  unlike metals, they are useful even if nothing bad happens: fasteners, matches, soap, seeds, salt,
  and the ability to fix things.
- **Fuel, food and medicine hold great value in scarcity**, and are exactly what you should be
  slowest to trade.

## Insurance and assistance

- **Insurance is the main financial protection** against realistic disasters: fire, wind, hail,
  flood (→ 19-04). **Standard homeowner's policies usually do not cover flood** (→ 17-03).
- **Disaster assistance programs exist** (USA.gov). Keep the papers that let you use them (→ 19-01,
  → 19-04).
- **The photo inventory** (→ 19-01) is what makes a claim work.

## Medicine: the expense that cannot wait

{{#if has.refrigerated_meds}}**Your household has refrigerated or life-critical medicines**
({{#each refrigerated_meds as r}}{{r.name}}; {{/each}}).{{/if}} For any household member whose
medicine cannot be skipped, a money problem quickly becomes a medical emergency (→ 12-11).
- **Know your insurance, your pharmacy, and the manufacturer assistance programs** for each such
  medicine. Look them up now and write them down (→ 12-07).
- **A cash reserve set aside for medicine** is a legitimate part of the plan.
- **For such a household, financial capacity is medical capacity.**

## The ordinary resilience that matters most

Not exciting, and it does more than any stockpile for realistic scenarios:
- **An emergency fund**, the most useful preparedness asset most households can build.
- **Low debt**: less fragility, more room to move.
- **More than one income path** where possible (→ 19-08).
- **Insurance in force and understood** (→ 19-04).
- **Records organized** (→ 19-01, → 19-03).
- **Knowing your fixed bills** and what happens if one is missed.

**Substitutes:** skills (→ 18-03), goods everyone needs (→ 18-02), labor, and relationships
(→ 18-01). **What you cannot substitute is cash in the first days.**

## When it runs out

- **Cash runs out fast in a long event**, and buys less as goods get scarce. Plan for days to weeks,
  not months.
- **What replaces it, in order:** cash, then goods, then skills, then relationships. Each lasts longer
  and is harder to spend.
- **Spend cash early on what will become unavailable** (fuel, medicine, specific supplies), not on
  what will still be there.
- **In a developing situation, acting early with cash is usually right**; the window closes fast
  (→ 17-09). Panic-buying in front of your neighbors has real costs (→ 18-01). Being ready beforehand
  resolves both.

## By phase

- **A, at home:** cash covers the realistic scenarios. Keep the fund, insurance and records in order,
  and be able to pay for medicine without notice.
- **B, on the move:** cash and the grab folder (→ 19-01) are the financial plan. Split the cash between
  adults and vehicles. Fuel will be the main expense and the first thing to vanish.
- **C, settled:** local exchange matters more (→ 18-02). Cash keeps working a long time in rural
  communities; over years, skills and produce become the practical currency.
