# Identity protection and fraud after a disaster

**What this page is for.** Protecting your household from the scams and identity theft that follow
every disaster: contractor scams, fake charities, fake officials and adjusters, and theft using
papers and mail lost in the chaos. One of the most consistent harms after a disaster, and largely
preventable. It works two ways: **papers and mail are exposed** (a damaged house, a displaced family,
mail going to an empty address), and **people under stress decide fast**, which is exactly what scams
are built on (→ 21-01).

> **CAUTION. Urgency, plus contact you did not ask for, plus a request for money or personal
> information, is a scam, essentially always.** Hang up, and call back on a number you find yourself:
> the one on your policy, card or statement.
>
> **The FTC is the federal agency for reporting and recovering from identity theft**; its identity
> theft line is 1-877-438-4338 (FTC, Identity Theft: A Recovery Plan). Write it on your contact
> list now (→ 25-03).

## Do this now

1. **Consider a credit freeze at all three national credit bureaus, for every adult.** A freeze stops
   anyone opening new credit in your name. It is free, you can lift it when you need credit, and it is
   the strongest protection against new-account fraud. It does not affect your credit score (FTC,
   What To Know About Credit Freezes and Fraud Alerts).
2. **Consider freezes for the children too.** Child identity theft is real and goes unnoticed for
   years, usually until the child first applies for credit as an adult. A parent can request a free
   freeze for a child under 16; each bureau has its own steps (FTC, What To Know About Credit Freezes
   and Fraud Alerts).
3. **Write down** the FTC line above and the three bureaus' phone numbers (→ 25-03).
4. **Secure your papers** (→ 19-01). The same box that protects against loss protects against
   exposure.
5. **Turn on account alerts** with your bank and card issuers while systems work.

## The post-disaster scams

| Scam | How it works | Defense |
|---|---|---|
| Contractor fraud | Door-to-door "we're working in the area", wants a large deposit, disappears | **Never pay much up front.** Check licensing. Get it in writing. Use established local firms |
| Fake adjusters or inspectors | Claim to be from your insurer or the government; ask for information or fees | **Call your insurer yourself** on the number on your policy (→ 19-04). Real officials do not demand payment |
| Charity fraud | Fake relief groups, urgent appeals | Give to organizations you already know |
| Advance-fee "assistance" | "Pay a fee to get your grant" | **Federal agencies never charge you for disaster assistance, and FEMA never charges for an inspection** (FEMA, Disaster Fraud) |
| Identity theft | Using papers, mail or information exposed in the chaos | Freezes, alerts, secured papers |
| Price gouging | Often illegal | Report to the state attorney general (→ 25-03) |
| Phishing | Fake texts and emails about claims, aid or accounts | **Never tap the link.** Contact the organization yourself |

## Why a disaster exposes you

- **Papers scattered or reachable** in a damaged house.
- **Mail going to an empty or damaged address.** If you are displaced, ask the Postal Service to hold
  or forward it.
- **Personal details shared with many parties** during recovery (insurers, contractors, agencies), any
  of whom can be impersonated.
- **Stress and distraction** (→ 21-01), the reason scams work.
- **Debris and discarded paper.** Shred or burn anything with identifying information; do not put it
  on the debris pile (→ 13-03).

## Freezes and alerts

- **A freeze blocks new credit accounts** being opened in your name. Free to place and to lift at all
  three bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion), and you can lift it temporarily when you apply for
  credit (FTC).
- **A fraud alert** is lighter: it asks lenders to check it is really you. A free alert placed with
  one bureau is passed to the other two and lasts one year (FTC, Identity Theft: A Recovery Plan). A
  freeze is stronger.
- **Do it for every member, children included.**

## If it happens: the recovery path (FTC)

The FTC's first three steps, in its order (FTC, Identity Theft: A Recovery Plan):
1. **Call the companies where you know fraud happened.** Ask them to close or freeze those
   accounts, and change the logins, passwords and PINs.
2. **Place a fraud alert and get your credit reports.** Go through them and note anything you do
   not recognize.
3. **Report it to the FTC** (line above). The online report produces an official identity theft
   report and a recovery plan.

Then:
4. **Consider a freeze** with each bureau, if you have not already.
5. **File a police report** if you know who did it or a company asks for one.
6. **Keep a record of everything:** dates, names, what was said (→ 25-05).
7. **Follow the plan through.** Recovery is a process, not one call.

## Angles that matter for some households

- **Medical identity theft:** someone using your health insurance. Read the "explanation of benefits"
  statements your insurer sends. {{#if has.refrigerated_meds}}For a household with expensive ongoing
  prescriptions, this could interfere with getting the medicine you actually need (→ 12-07).{{/if}}
- **Children's identities:** freeze them, and check periodically that no credit file exists.
- **Papers in a go-bag are papers outside a safe** (→ 22-03). Worth knowing; the trade-off still
  favors having copies with you.
- **A displaced household is an exposed household:** mail, address and papers all in flux.

## Information hygiene (→ 14-03)

- **Do not discuss money or accounts in public**, including on radio, which anyone can hear (→ 15-02).
- **Do not post about being away from home.**
- **Check before sharing anything**: call the organization yourself, on a number you looked up.
- **Shred or burn** papers you discard.
- **Distrust urgency.** Legitimate organizations will let you call them back.

## The everyday version, which matters more

Most identity theft has nothing to do with disasters:
- **Freezes on every member.** Free, effective, lasting.
- **Account alerts** on bank accounts and cards.
- **Check credit reports.** A look every few months is plenty.
- **Strong, unique passwords and a password manager** (→ 19-03).
- **Two-step sign-in, with the backup codes printed on paper** (→ 19-03), so a dead phone does not
  lock you out (→ 09-06).
- **Papers secured at home** (→ 19-01).

> **Before the collapse:** federal law lets you get a free credit report from each of the three
> bureaus every 12 months, and the bureaus have permanently extended free weekly reports
> (FTC, Free Credit Reports). Placing and lifting a freeze is free (FTC).

**Substitutes:** skepticism is free and is most of the defense. **Calling back on a number you found
yourself** defeats nearly every impersonation scam. **Neighbors**: word travels fast about a scammer
working a street (→ 18-01). **What you cannot substitute is the freeze, done in advance.**

## When it runs out

- **Nothing here runs out.** Freezes stay; skepticism does not wear out.
- **Attention lapses.** Review every year (→ 25-10): freezes still on, alerts still set, children's
  files checked, passwords current.
- **Risk rises during and after any disruption and stays high for months.** Stay skeptical longer than
  feels necessary.
- **If something does happen, recovery depends on proving what is yours** (→ 19-03).
- **Fraud after a disaster is far more likely than most scenarios in this binder.** A ten-minute,
  free credit freeze is one of the best returns on any page here.

## By phase

- **A, at home:** freezes and alerts now. After any local disaster, expect contractor and adjuster
  scams within days. Check everything, and warn the neighbors.
- **B, on the move:** keep the grab folder on a person (→ 19-01), hold your mail, and be especially
  wary of anyone offering help that costs money.
- **C, settled:** fewer door-to-door scams, but remote and displaced households are targeted for that
  reason. The freeze protects wherever you are.
