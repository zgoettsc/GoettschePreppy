# Stress, panic and judgment

**What this page is for.** Stress is not a soft topic. It wears down the thing every other page in
this binder depends on: judgment. People under sudden stress narrow their attention, fixate, lose
access to what they know, misjudge time, and take the first option instead of the best one. A
well-supplied household making bad decisions is not a prepared household. This page explains
what stress does, how to plan around it, and what to do in a panic attack.

> **Check the body first.** Low blood sugar, carbon monoxide, cold, dehydration, infection and
> lack of sleep all look like anxiety, confusion or poor judgment (table below). **If several
> people in one room are confused, headachy or sick at once, get everyone outside and check the
> carbon monoxide alarm** (→ 08-02).
>
> **If phones work:** call or text **988** (988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline), or SAMHSA's
> **Disaster Distress Helpline, 1-800-985-5990** (call or text). They may keep working when much
> else does not. Someone in danger: → 12-15.

## Do this now

1. **Learn one breathing technique and practise it until it is automatic:** box breathing or a
   long slow out-breath (below). Practised when calm, it works when you are not.
2. **Write down the decisions you do not want to make under stress:** the go/no-go triggers
   (→ 01-02), the medical triggers (→ 12-16 and your condition modules), and who does what
   (→ 00-07). **Deciding in advance is the single best defence against stress-damaged judgment.**
3. **Agree between the adults how to say "I'm not coping"** and what happens next (→ 21-05).
4. **Post 988 and the Disaster Distress Helpline** where you would find them (→ 25-03).

## What sudden stress does to you

It is physical, not a flaw of character:
- **Tunnel vision and tunnel hearing:** you stop noticing what is at the edges.
- **Loss of fine hand control:** hands shake; small tasks become hard.
- **Time distortion:** events feel much faster or slower than they were.
- **Working memory fails:** **you lose access to things you know**, which is why written checklists
  and drilled procedures work when recall does not (→ 24-02).
- **Fixation:** locking onto one problem or one answer and being unable to shift.
- **Falling back on habit:** you do what you have practised, not what you have read. That is the
  whole argument for drills.
- **Poor risk judgment**, in both directions.

None of this can be willed away. All of it can be planned around.

## What actually works

1. **Decide in advance.** Write thresholds and plans down (→ 01-02, → 12-16, → 00-07). A decision
   made calmly last month beats one made brilliantly under pressure.
2. **Checklists on paper.** This binder is one. Working memory fails; paper does not.
3. **Drill it** (→ 24-02). Practising the shelter drill once beats reading about it ten times.
4. **Breathe on purpose.** Box breathing (breathe in, hold, breathe out, hold, each for the same
   slow count), or simply a long, slow out-breath. Slow breathing calms the body's alarm response.
   Practise now.
5. **Say it out loud.** Explaining a plan to another adult exposes bad reasoning fast and slows you
   down usefully.
6. **Take thirty seconds.** Almost nothing is so urgent that thirty seconds of thought makes it
   worse, and panicked action often does (→ 12-16).
7. **Do the next small thing.** When the whole problem is overwhelming, shrink it: what is the one
   next action? Then the one after.
8. **Move.** Physical action burns off arousal and brings back a sense of control.

## Panic attacks

Common, frightening and manageable (→ 12-15):
- **What it looks like:** racing heart, short of breath, tight chest, sweating, shaking, a certainty
  of dying or losing control. It peaks and passes.
- **What to do:** stay with them, calm and close. Breathe slowly together. Ground them: "name five
  things you can see, four you can hear." Tell them it will pass. **Do not leave them alone, do
  not belittle it, do not shout.**
- **But chest pain and shortness of breath can also be a heart attack, an asthma attack or a
  severe allergic reaction.** If there is any doubt, treat it as medical (→ 12-16, → 02-09,
  → 02-07, → 02-03).

  sugar looks like a panic attack. **Check the glucose first** (→ 02-01).

  Running out, or stopping some of them suddenly, can cause severe symptoms; the module is → 02-16.

## The body first: things that look like stress

Thinking and behaviour change for physical reasons all the time, and in a crisis every one of
these is more likely (→ 12-15):

| Cause | Looks like | Page |
|---|---|---|
| **Low blood sugar** (anyone with diabetes) | Anxiety, confusion, irritability, aggression, clumsiness | → 02-01, → 02-05 |
| **Carbon monoxide** | Headache, confusion, nausea, sleepiness, **in everyone in the room at once** (CDC) | → 08-02 |
| **Dehydration** | Headache, irritability, poor concentration | → 12-05 |
| **Hypothermia** | Confusion, poor judgment, clumsiness, mumbling | → 08-05 |
| **Infection or fever** | Confusion; serious infection can show first as confusion | → 12-04 |
| **Lack of sleep** | All of the above, plus swings of mood | → 21-07 |
| **Hunger** | Irritability, poor decisions | |
| **Pain** | Short temper, tunnel vision | → 12-13 |
| **Missed or stopped medicines** | Varies; some cause withdrawal or rebound | → 02-16, → 02-17 |

**Rule out the body before concluding it is the mind.**

## Warning signs of distress that needs attention

SAMHSA's list for people affected by disaster (SAMHSA, Warning Signs and Risk Factors for Emotional
Distress), in short:
- Sleeping or eating too much or too little.
- Lack of energy, or always feeling tired.
- More alcohol or other substance use.
- Worrying much of the time; feeling guilty without knowing why.
- Physical complaints with no clear cause.
- Anger, feeling edgy, or lashing out at others.
- Pulling away from others.
- Feeling hopeless or overwhelmed.
- **Thoughts of self-harm: take these seriously at once** (→ 12-15).

## The adults' state is household infrastructure

- **Children's wellbeing tracks the adults' state** (→ 20-08). Looking after your own stress is
  child care.
- **A stressed adult makes worse decisions** about fire, tools, medicine, travel and people, all
  dangerous areas (→ 12-16, → 16-06, → 01-02).
- **Your household has {{ household.adults }} adult(s).** Every adult who stops functioning cuts
  the household's capacity; with two or fewer, that margin is thin.
- **So rest, food, water, warmth and sleep are not indulgences.** They are the inputs to good
  judgment, and rationing them below a floor is a false economy.

## What helps over hours and days

- **Sleep** (→ 21-07): the biggest single lever.
- **Eat and drink** regularly, above all anyone with diabetes (→ 02-01, → 02-05).
- **Warmth** (→ 08-01): cold wears down mood and judgment.
- **Physical work with a visible result.**
- **Routine** (→ 21-04): fewer decisions, time marked.
- **Talk to another adult** (→ 21-05), and take turns being the steady one.
- **Limit news and radio** to set times, not constant monitoring (→ 15-04).
- **Get outside into daylight** when it is safe.
- **Do something for someone else** (→ 18-01): agency and connection at once.

CDC lists strong social connections and positive coping skills among the things that protect mental
health (CDC, About Mental Health). Both are free, and both need deliberate upkeep.

## When it runs out

- **What runs out is capacity, slowly and invisibly.** Month one runs on adrenaline; months later
  is the hard part (→ 21-02).
- **Watch for the warning signs in yourself and each other**, and treat them as information, not
  failure.
- **Ration decisions.** A tired household should make fewer decisions, not more. That is what
  routine (→ 21-04) and written thresholds are for.
- **Substitute** each other (→ 21-05), other people (→ 18-01) and structure.
- **Judgment is the resource that makes every other resource usable.** Wood, water, food and
  medicine are worth less in the hands of someone exhausted and not thinking. Protecting the
  household's ability to think clearly is a survival measure, which is why this section sits beside
  food and water.

## By phase

- **A (shelter in place):** the first days have the highest arousal, the worst judgment and the
  biggest decisions (→ 01-02). That is exactly why the thresholds are written in advance. Slow down;
  use the checklists.
- **B (moving):** sustained stress plus exhaustion plus weather plus children (→ 01-03). Decisions on
  the move are the most stress-damaged of any. Decide the route, stops and turn-back points before
  leaving.
- **C (establish):** the sharp phase gives way to the grind (→ 21-02). Same answer: routine, sleep,
  purpose and connection. At a remote site, isolation becomes a real risk (CDC), which is an argument
  for community (→ 18-01).
