# Conflict between adults

**What this page is for.** A few adults, possibly children, one heated room, no relief, for months:
that is hard on any partnership or household, and **conflict under it is normal, not a sign that
something is wrong.** But it matters more than usual, because in this situation the adults **are**
the household's decision-making, its labour and its medical backup. Adults who cannot work together
are a household at half capacity in every way. This page covers preventing conflict, fighting well
when it comes, and the line where it is not a conflict at all.

> **SAFETY. One line that is not a communication problem:** if conflict involves **violence,
> threats, control or anyone being afraid**, that is a safety matter needing outside help, not
> something to work through. **While phones work: National Domestic Violence Hotline
> 1-800-799-7233 (text START to 88788)**; that is the line built for this. **988** is the
> mental-health crisis line and the second call (→ 12-15, → 25-03). When phones do not work, the
> outside help is a neighbour's house (→ 18-01): leave, with the children, and go there.

## Do this now

1. **Have the conversation now, while things are calm.** How do we make decisions? How do we say we
   are not coping? What do we do when we disagree and something must be decided?
2. **Write down the roles** (→ 00-07) and **the decision thresholds** (→ 01-02). Most household
   conflict is about unclear expectations and contested decisions; writing them down in advance
   removes most of it.
3. **Schedule the breaks.** Each adult gets time alone, on the routine (→ 21-04). A maintenance item,
   not a treat.
4. **Agree a pause phrase** that means "I need to stop this conversation and come back to it."
   Simple, agreed, honoured without argument.
5. **Fill in the agreement in pen** and re-date it at every yearly review:

```
AGREED: __________ (date)
PAUSE PHRASE: ______________________________________________
DECISION RULE (who breaks ties), by area:
   medical: __________  go/stay: __________  children: __________
   other: ____________________________________________________
BREAK SCHEDULE: each adult gets ______ hours alone a week,
   on the routine (21-04).
SIGNED: ______________________  ______________________
```

## Why conflict rises

It is structural, and naming the causes makes them easier to handle:
- **No relief:** no work, no separate friends, no space, no time apart.
- **Exhaustion and poor sleep** (→ 21-07): the biggest single amplifier.
- **Constant low-level fear**, above all about medical supplies.
- **High-stakes decisions** with no clear right answer (→ 01-02, → 12-16).
- **Unequal or unclear workload:** the commonest source of resentment.
- **Different coping styles.** One wants to plan and talk; another wants to work and not talk. Both
  are legitimate, and each can look like a failing to the other.
- **Different risk tolerance.** One wants to move; one wants to stay. **This is the big one**
  (→ 01-02).
- **Small irritations compound** in confinement.
- **Grief** (→ 21-03), processed differently and on different schedules.

## What prevents it

1. **Written roles** (→ 00-07). Who does the fire, the water, the children in the morning, the
   night waking, the medical routines.
2. **Rotate the unpleasant jobs.** Fairness over time matters more than one equal split.
3. **Written decision thresholds** (→ 01-02). Deciding in advance removes the highest-stakes argument
   from the moment it would be hardest.
4. **A decision rule.** When you truly disagree and something must be decided, who decides? Options:
   whoever's area it is; whoever has to carry it out; take turns; or **the more cautious option wins
   when it is about safety.** Pick one now.
5. **Scheduled time apart** (→ 21-04), even an hour.
6. **Scheduled time together that is not logistics.**
7. **Sleep** (→ 21-07). Much of what feels like a relationship problem late at night is a sleep
   problem.
8. **Say the small things early.** Resentment builds in silence and arrives all at once.
9. **Thank each other out loud for the work.** In a grind, invisible work feels unappreciated, and
   usually both people feel it at once.

## Fighting well, when you do

- **Not in front of the children.** They absorb far more than you think (→ 20-08). Step outside, or
  wait.
- **Use the pause phrase.**
- **One topic.** Not a list of grievances.
- **Check the body first** (→ 21-01): hungry, cold, exhausted, in pain, or low blood sugar in anyone
  with diabetes. A large share of household conflict in a crisis is physical, and checking is faster
  than arguing.
- **Separate the decision from the feeling.** "I'm frightened about the medicine running out" and "we
  should leave tomorrow" are different statements and need different answers.
- **Ask what they want:** to be heard, to be helped, or to decide something. Answering the wrong one
  is most of what goes wrong.
- **Come back and close it.** Unresolved conflict festers in a confined household.

## The decisions most likely to divide you

| Decision | Why it is hard |
|---|---|
| **Go or stay** (→ 01-02) | Highest stakes, real uncertainty, different risk tolerance |
| **How much to ration** | One person's prudence is another's deprivation |
| **Whether to help neighbours** (→ 18-01) | Generosity against the household's margin |
| **How much to tell the children** (→ 20-08) | Different instincts about honesty and protection |
| **Medical judgment calls** (→ 12-16) | The hardest, and there may be no right answer |
| **Whether to trust someone** (→ 18-05) | Different reads on people |
| **The decision cards** (→ 30-02 to → 30-25) | Different risk tolerance, written down; agree them now |
| **How much risk to accept**, for anything | Usually temperament |

**Decide in advance what you can** (→ 01-02, the decision cards), and for the rest, agree the decision
rule now.

## When one adult's health is the household's clock

If an adult depends on a medicine or device (insulin, oxygen, dialysis, a heart or seizure medicine),
that person's supply becomes the household's central clock, and it creates pressures worth naming out
loud:
- **The person with the condition may feel like a burden.** That feeling is corrosive; say that it is
  not true.
- **Another adult may carry fear** about something they cannot control.
- **At least two adults should be competent at the medical routine:** a practical necessity, and a
  way of sharing the weight (→ 12-07, the condition modules).
- **Supply status is household information, not private information.** Every big decision branches on
  it, so all adults see it by right. Agreed now, it is teamwork; asserted later, it feels like
  surveillance.

{{#if has.condition.CL-01}}### Diabetes distress

**It has a name.** CDC describes diabetes distress: the exhaustion of a disease that never takes a day
off (CDC, Diabetes and Mental Health). Scarcity makes it worse: rationed supplies, no specialist, no
relief.

**Warning signs, for the other adults to watch.** Burnout looks like quiet corner-cutting, not a
crisis:
- **Skipped checks:** "I know where I'm at" replacing the actual number.
- **"It's fine"** when asked, and the conversation closing.
- **Rationing insulin without saying so.** The most dangerous one, and the one pride hides longest.
- **Alarm fatigue:** sensor or meter alerts silenced or resented rather than acted on.

**Agree an opener now, so it is never an accusation later**, for example: "Supply check: talk me
through the last three days." It asks for the record, not a confession, and works because it was
agreed in calm. **The caregiver's side is real too:** dread of being the one who has to give the
emergency medicine, alone, at night. The answer is the drill (→ 24-02, → 02-01): practice until it is
routine, because a rehearsed hand shakes less.{{else}}### Chronic illness distress

Living with any condition that never takes a day off is exhausting, and scarcity makes it worse (CDC
describes this for diabetes as "diabetes distress"; CDC, Diabetes and Mental Health). Watch for quiet
corner-cutting: skipped checks, "it's fine", rationing a medicine without saying so. Agree a calm,
data-first opener now ("Supply check: talk me through the last three days"), and drill any emergency
medicine an adult may have to give (→ 24-02) so the dread turns into procedure.{{/if}}

## Merging into another household

{{#if has.destinations}}Your plan includes these destinations: {{#each destinations as d}}{{d.name}} ({{d.status_text}}). {{/each}}{{else}}Moving in with others, or taking others in, is common in a long event.{{/if}}
Moving in with another household (or taking one in) brings three frictions on day one. Name them now:
1. **Authority: whose land, whose call.** Settle it in writing before arrival where you can (→ 18-04),
   or in the first hours where you cannot: which decisions are the hosts', which are shared, which are
   yours alone (your children's medical care, always).
2. **Parenting styles.** A workable split: "our children, our rules; their house, their spaces." You
   hold the safety and allergy lines everywhere (→ 20-02, → 02-04); the hosts set the rules for their
   rooms, tools and things, and you back them in front of the children.
3. **Stores: pooled or separate.** Decide before the first shared meal (→ 18-04). Undecided food is
   the fastest source of resentment there is.

**Hold a weekly meeting of all adults:** 30 minutes, a standing agenda, and one rule that makes it
work: **grievances go to the meeting, not to the kitchen.** A complaint with a scheduled place to land
does not leak out sideways all week (→ 18-06).

**Know your posture.** In someone else's house you are a guest, even in a crisis: deference costs less
than friction. If you arrive unexpected, arrive self-sufficient, offer your labour and skills on day
one, and let the terms be drafted at the hosts' table, on their timetable. Knowing which posture you
are in prevents most collisions.

## When it is more than ordinary conflict

Get help if any exists; use the lines if phones work (→ 12-15):
- **Contempt, stonewalling, or being unable to talk at all.**
- **Anger that keeps escalating**, especially with alcohol (→ 12-15).
- **Anyone withdrawing completely** (SAMHSA warning sign; → 21-02).
- **Hopelessness in anyone:** a strong warning sign (CDC).
- **Any violence, threats, control or fear.** A safety matter, not a conflict: the National Domestic
  Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233, text START to 88788) while phones work; 988 second; a neighbour's
  door when they do not (→ 18-01).
- **Thoughts of self-harm:** take seriously, act at once (→ 12-15).

## The other side of it

Shared hardship, handled reasonably well, tends to strengthen partnerships rather than break them.
Working side by side on things that matter, seeing each other be competent, getting through something
hard together: that outcome is available, and it depends mostly on the upkeep on this page: sleep,
clear roles, scheduled relief, and saying things early.

**Scavenge or substitute:** everything here is free: conversations, written agreements, scheduled
breaks, ten minutes outside. **Other people** (→ 18-01): a neighbour who takes the children for an
hour, or an adult to talk to who is not your partner, is worth an enormous amount. **What you cannot
substitute: the conversation. Have it before you need it.**

## When it runs out

- **What runs out is patience**, on the same schedule as everything else in this section (→ 21-02).
- **The upkeep items are the answer**, and they are cheap: sleep, breaks, clear roles, thanks, and
  saying things early. All are easier to set up now than to repair later.
- **When capacity is low, lower the household's ambitions rather than raising demands on each other.**
  A household doing less, together, beats one doing more, resentfully.
- **Substitute:** other adults (→ 18-01), physical separation, time and sleep.
- **The adults working together are the household's decision-making, labour and medical backup.**
  Everything else in this binder assumes it. That makes this page infrastructure, and it deserves the
  same deliberate upkeep as the water system.

## By phase

- **A (shelter in place):** confinement and monotony are the stressors (→ 21-02). Neighbours are right
  there and the best relief available (→ 18-01). Set roles and breaks early.
- **B (moving):** high-stakes decisions, exhaustion, weather and frightened children, all at once
  (→ 01-03). This is when written thresholds and a pre-agreed decision rule earn their keep; the
  alternative is arguing at the roadside.
- **C (establish):** isolation replaces confinement, and merged-household frictions become daily life.
  Start the weekly meeting the first week, and build some community at the new site (→ 18-01).
