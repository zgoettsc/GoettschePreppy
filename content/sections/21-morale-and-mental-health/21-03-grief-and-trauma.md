# Grief, loss and trauma

**What this page is for.** Nobody wants to read this page, and it is better read in advance. In a
long, serious event, loss is a real possibility: of people, of pets, of the house, of a way of life,
of the future you expected. Thinking about it once, calmly, now, makes it less overwhelming later.
Grief is not a problem to solve and it keeps no schedule. This page is practical: what helps, what
to tell children, and what to watch for.

> **If phones work:** SAMHSA's **Disaster Distress Helpline, 1-800-985-5990** (call or text), and
> **988** (call or text) are both useful for exactly this. Write them on the wall (→ 25-03). Someone
> in danger: → 12-15.

## Do this now

1. **Have one conversation, now, about the unthinkable** (→ 12-16): what we would do, who decides,
   what each of us would want. It is enormously easier now than in the moment.
2. **Write down what matters:** documents (→ 19-01), wishes, contacts (→ 25-03). Practical, not
   morbid.
3. **Print photographs.** Digital photos vanish with a dead phone or a burned house. Physical photos
   are among the things people most regret not having.
4. **Post 988 and the Disaster Distress Helpline** (→ 25-03).

## The kinds of loss to expect

Not all grief is about death, and the smaller losses add up:
- **A person.** The hardest, and possible (→ 12-16).
- **A pet.** For a child it may be the first experience of death. It is real grief. {{#if has.pets}}If
  the household may have to give up its pets, that decision is on → 30-11.{{/if}}
- **The house**, to fire, storm or flood (→ 17-02, → 17-03, → 17-14).
- **The life you expected:** school, work, plans, normality. A real loss, and rarely acknowledged,
  which makes it harder. Teens feel this one sharply.
- **People you cannot reach.** Not knowing is its own grief.
- **Animals you have raised** (→ 05-04), heavier when a child has named them.
- **Ability:** an injury that permanently changes what someone can do (→ 12-09).

## What helps

There is no fixing it. What helps is fairly consistent:
1. **Not being alone with it.** Connection protects (CDC). Say it aloud to someone, even briefly.
2. **Ritual** (→ 21-06). Marking a loss matters more than people expect: a burial, a marker, a
   candle, words said, a moment together. Ritual gives grief somewhere to go.
3. **Routine** (→ 21-04). Keeping the structure is not disrespectful; it holds people up. The
   household still needs water and wood, and that is a mercy as much as a burden.
4. **Purpose and being useful**, including caring for others (→ 18-01).
5. **Time.** No shortcuts.
6. **Sleep, food, warmth** (→ 21-07). Grief is physically exhausting.
7. **Letting it be what it is.** Grief comes in waves, arrives unexpectedly and does not follow
   orderly stages. **People grieve differently and on different timelines**, a common source of
   friction between partners (→ 21-05). Name it rather than reading it as not caring.
8. **Talking about the person or thing lost.** Avoiding the subject is usually worse.

## Telling children

This most needs thinking about in advance (→ 20-08; CDC, Helping Children Cope During and After a
Disaster):
- **Plain, concrete words.** "Died", not "went to sleep", "passed away" or "we lost him". Soft words
  confuse and frighten small children; a child told someone "went to sleep" may become afraid to
  sleep.
- **Simple and honest:** "Grandma died. That means her body stopped working and she won't come back.
  We're all very sad."
- **Expect the same questions for weeks.** That is how children process; answer patiently each time.
- **Reassure them about their own safety and yours.** That is usually the question underneath, even
  if they do not ask it.
- **Let them see adults grieve**, in a manageable form. Children who never see adult feeling learn to
  hide their own.
- **Include them in the ritual** if they want: a drawing, a stone placed, saying goodbye. Do not force
  it.
- **Expect effects:** going backwards, clinginess, sleep problems, new fears, repetitive play about it
  (→ 20-08). All normal; all ease with time and steadiness.
- **Keep the routine** (→ 20-03).
- **Babies and toddlers** will not understand, but they feel the adults' state. Closeness and routine
  are the whole help for them.
- **Teens** may grieve privately, through anger, or with friends rather than family. Offer, keep the
  door open, and watch for the warning signs below.

## If a pet dies

**Do not minimise it.** For a child it may be the first death, and how it is handled shapes how they
understand death:
- **Tell the truth plainly.**
- **Let them have a ritual:** a burial, a marker, a drawing. It is a rehearsal for larger griefs.
- **Let them be sad for as long as they are sad.**
- **Do not rush to replace the pet.**
- **If the household ended the pet's life** (→ 30-11, → 20-09), say so simply and honestly at the
  child's level ("she was very sick / we could not feed him, and we helped him die without pain"), and
  never let a child watch. The adult who did it may grieve hard too.

## If a person dies and no services are available

Said plainly because it is better considered in advance (→ 12-16):
- **Contact any authority that exists.** Handling of human remains is governed by law and public
  health rules, and those duties do not vanish because services are degraded.
- **If truly no authority can be reached,** the practical concerns are: **a written record** (what
  happened, when and where, signed by the adults present; it matters later, legally and personally),
  **dignity**, **public health** (away from water and wells, away from where people live; → 13-07
  for siting), and **being able to find and identify the site afterwards** (marker, sketch map,
  description).
- **Do not skip the ritual.** Even a short one: words said, a marker, the family together. It
  matters to the living, and to children most of all.
- **Tell the children the truth** and include them as they can manage.

## Trauma: the longer tail

Beyond grief, people carry the events themselves:
- **Intrusive memories, nightmares, jumpiness, avoidance, numbness and irritability** are common
  after frightening events, and usually settle with time, safety, sleep, routine and connection.
- **Talking helps most people, but being made to talk does not.** Offer; do not push.
- **Children process through play and repetition** (→ 20-08). Let them.
- **When it does not settle** (persistent, severe, getting worse, or stopping someone functioning,
  over weeks) it needs professional help wherever any exists (→ 12-15).
- **Watch for hopelessness and talk of self-harm:** the strongest warning signs (CDC, Risk and
  Protective Factors for Suicide). Act at once (→ 12-15).
- **Watch for more alcohol use** as a way of coping (→ 12-15).

## A child who saw a frightening emergency

A child may see an adult collapse, a seizure, a bad injury, adults moving fast and scared. The event
may be over in minutes; **how it lives in the child's memory depends on what happens after.** CDC's
advice for children after a disaster is to talk about what happened simply, in words they understand,
to stay calm, and to reassure them that they are safe (CDC, Helping Children Cope During and After a
Disaster).

**Once the person is stable, say something like:**

> "[Name]'s body got sick very fast. We gave the help it needed. They're okay now. And you did your
> job perfectly."

- **Tie it to their drill** (→ 20-04). Whatever they did (shouted, blew the whistle, stayed with the
  little ones, or just stayed out of the way), **call it the job done right.** The memory to build is
  "I helped", not "I was helpless". That frame is most of the difference in how a young child carries
  a frightening event.
- **Keep it concrete and small:** what happened in the body, what fixed it, that grown-ups know what
  to do. Answer their questions; do not go past them (→ 20-08).
- **What not to say:**
  - **Do not swear them to secrecy.** Secrecy turns a manageable memory into a shameful one, and the
    household needs children who report things, not hide them.
  - **Do not bring in death they did not see.** "Could they have died?" can get "The help works, and
    that's why we practise." True, calm and enough for a young child.
  - **Do not rehearse the fear back at them** ("weren't you scared?"). Ask once what they saw and felt,
    and follow their lead.
- **Expect some going backwards afterwards:** clinginess, sleep trouble, acting it out with toys.
  Normal processing, not a warning sign by itself.
- **If it may happen again** (a chronic condition in the house), the second time is easier if the
  first was talked through: the child meets it with a script instead of raw fear.
- **Debrief the adults too**, the same night, briefly: what happened, what worked, what changes
  (→ 21-05, → 12-15).

## Guilt and second-guessing

Especially after a death you could not prevent (→ 12-16):
- **"Would a hospital have saved them?" has no answer**, and it can consume someone. Naming that in
  advance helps.
- **You did what could be done with what you had.** That is usually true and worth saying aloud to
  each other.
- **Guilt is a normal part of grief**, not evidence of fault.
- **Talk about it:** with each other, with anyone available, with 988 if phones work. Isolation makes
  it worse (CDC).

**Scavenge or substitute:** everything that helps here is free: words said aloud, presence, ritual,
routine, time and each other. Ritual from anything: a stone, a song, a tree planted (→ 05-13), a
moment of silence. Other people (→ 18-01): grief shared with a community is carried differently.
**What you cannot substitute: time, and not being alone.**

## When it runs out

- **Grief does not run out on a schedule, and the goal is not to finish it.** The goal is to carry it
  without the household coming apart.
- **What can run out is the household's capacity to function through it** (→ 21-02). The answers are
  the same as everywhere in this section: routine, sleep, food, warmth, purpose and connection.
- **Lower the household's ambitions while grieving.** Water, heat, food and medicine, and little more,
  for a while. That is correct triage, not failure.
- **Watch each other** (→ 21-05, → 12-15) and the children (→ 20-08).
- **Most people do come through grief**, and most households hold together through loss, especially
  those that keep talking, keep the routine, mark the loss properly and do not isolate. That is worth
  having read once before you need it.

## By phase

- **A (shelter in place):** losses are often of normal life and of people out of reach. Name them,
  and keep the routine.
- **B (moving):** leaving home is itself a loss, for children above all. Mark it with a small ritual if
  you can, and keep the bedtime routine on the road (→ 20-03).
- **C (establish):** new places carry old grief. Make a place for remembering at the new site: a
  marker, a planted tree, photos on a wall.
