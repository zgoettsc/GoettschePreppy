# Morale over the long haul

**What this page is for.** Almost every preparedness plan is built for month one, and almost every
real hardship is decided months later. Month one runs on adrenaline, novelty and the sense of doing
something. Months in, the adrenaline and novelty are gone, nothing has improved, the work is endless
and there is no visible end. **That is the phase that breaks households**: not through hunger but
through exhaustion, conflict (→ 21-05), giving up, taking risks and making bad decisions (→ 21-01).
This page is about getting through it.

**Social connection protects; isolation is a risk factor** (CDC, Risk and Protective Factors for
Suicide; CDC, About Mental Health). That is the single most useful finding on this page.

> **If phones work:** **988** (call or text) and SAMHSA's **Disaster Distress Helpline,
> 1-800-985-5990**. Someone in danger: → 12-15.

{{#if is.cold_winters}}**Your hardest stretch is likely late winter:** long dark nights, everyone
confined to one heated room (→ 08-01), little travel and little news, and everyone indoors for
months. Plan for that scenario specifically.{{else}}**Your hardest stretch** is whatever season keeps
everyone confined: heat, smoke, storms or winter. Plan for it specifically.{{/if}}

## Do this now

1. **Get to know your neighbours, actually:** names, children, skills, and enough of a relationship
   that you would knock on each other's doors (→ 18-01). The most valuable morale investment there
   is, and it costs only time now.
2. **Stock the morale supplies now**, because you will not think of them later: books, games, art
   supplies, seeds, projects, coffee, spices, small treats (→ 21-06).
3. **Build the daily and weekly routine** (→ 21-04) and write it down.
4. **Plan the hard season on purpose:** light for the evenings (→ 10-03), things to do, something
   on the calendar. Do not plan to endure it; plan to live through it.

## The arc of a long event

| Stage | What it feels like | The risk |
|---|---|---|
| **The first days: sharp** | Adrenaline, urgency, clear purpose | Bad decisions under stress (→ 21-01) |
| **The first weeks: adapting** | Building routines, solving problems, competence | Overwork; burning through supplies |
| **The next months: grind** | Normal now, and relentless | Fatigue building invisibly |
| **Months in: the wall** | No end in sight; nothing changes; everything is hard | **Conflict, despair, risk-taking, giving up** |
| **Later: new normal** | If you get through the wall, it becomes life | Complacency; dropping vigilance |

The stages are a common pattern, not a schedule, and nobody can say in advance how long each one
lasts. **Knowing the arc helps:** when the wall feels
uniquely awful, it is a predictable stage, not a verdict on the household.

## What keeps people going for months

1. **Purpose.** Work that visibly matters. The chores of a grid-down house are exactly this, if they
   are framed as building something rather than just surviving.
2. **Connection.** Neighbours, family, community (→ 18-01). The strongest lever, and the one most
   often neglected.
3. **Routine and structure** (→ 21-04). Fewer decisions; time marked.
4. **Progress you can see:** a stack of wood, a filled jar, a fixed thing, a garden bed. Visible
   accumulation beats "nothing ever changes."
5. **Marking time forward** (→ 21-06): birthdays, holidays, a different Sunday, the solstice.
6. **Small pleasures:** coffee, something sweet, music, a story, a warm wash. Not frivolous;
   load-bearing over months.
7. **Some choice** about something, however small.
8. **Hope with a foundation:** not "it'll be fine", but a plan, a next step, a season coming.
9. **Sleep** (→ 21-07).
10. **Daylight and the outdoors**, even in winter (→ 11-02).

## The hard season

- **Light in the evening** (→ 10-03). A lit, warm, gathered evening is worth scarce light and fuel.
  It is what makes the hardest months survivable.
- **Get outside every day it is safe** (→ 11-01; limits → 12-10). Even a short time outside changes a
  day.
- **Vary the days.** A different meal, a different project, a "Saturday" that is different from a
  Tuesday.
- **Projects with visible progress:** mending (→ 16-04), building (→ 16-08), planning the garden
  (→ 05-01), teaching a child something (→ 20-03).
- **Indoor things, stocked now:** books, games, cards, instruments, art supplies, puzzles. They weigh
  little and cost little, and in the hard months they matter enormously.
- **Make something:** bread, candles (→ 10-04), soap (→ 13-09), a repair. Making things is
  disproportionately good for morale.
- **Accept bad days.** Some days everyone is miserable. It passes.

## Few adults, no relief

{{#if has.child}}Adults with children and no relief, for months, is hard on everyone, and naming it
helps.{{else}}A small household with no relief, for months, is hard, and naming it helps.{{/if}}
- **You may be each other's only adult company.** That is a heavy load on a few relationships
  (→ 21-05).
- **Take turns.** Schedule breaks: one adult takes the children or the chores for two hours while
  another does something alone. Put it on the routine (→ 21-04).
- **Protect time together that is not logistics.**
- **Say the quiet things out loud.** Resentment builds in silence.
- **Other adults matter enormously** (→ 18-01). Even brief contact with someone outside the
  household changes everyone's capacity.

## One working adult

Sometimes there is only one functioning adult: illness, injury, a bad medical day, or the other
adult away. **Solo mode is a defined way of running the house, not a heroic improvisation.**

**What drops when solo:**
- Laundry and non-urgent cleaning.
- The learning block (→ 20-03): books and free play cover it.
- Non-critical projects, preserving, and any high-risk work with no second adult (ladders, saws,
  chainsaws).

**What never drops:**
- **Medicines:** everyone's routine medicines on time; emergency medicine (epinephrine, glucagon,
  rescue inhalers) within reach (→ 12-07, your condition modules).
- **Safe water**, made and available (→ 03-01).
- **The children's baseline routine:** meals, the bedtime sequence (→ 20-03).
- **The solo adult's sleep.** Within the first day, switch to "nap when they nap". Sleep is working
  capacity; a solo adult running on none is the next casualty (→ 21-07).

**Ask the neighbours. This is what the mutual-aid list is for** (→ 18-01). The ask, printed so it
gets said:

> "I'm on my own with the kids for a few days. Could you take them for two hours tomorrow, or bring
> water by? I'll do the same for you."

Specific, limited, returned: the kind of ask neighbours say yes to. **The commonest failure is not
asking.**

**Plan solo mode in days, not weeks.** Once it runs past a few days, lower the standards **on
purpose**, not by collapse: simpler meals, more free play, a shorter routine, more help asked for. If
solo mode has no end in sight, that is a real input to the go/no-go decision (→ 01-02).

## Watching for the slide

The warning signs (SAMHSA; → 21-01, → 12-15). Over months, watch for:
- **Withdrawal:** stopping talking, stopping doing things.
- **Hopelessness:** a strong warning sign (CDC).
- **More alcohol use:** a common route that makes everything worse (→ 12-15).
- **Losing routine:** stopping washing, dressing, the small things. Often the first visible sign.
- **Snapping at the children** more than usual.
- **Not sleeping, or sleeping all the time** (→ 21-07).
- **Talk of self-harm: take it seriously** (→ 12-15, 988).

**And check the body first** (→ 21-01): blood sugar, dehydration, infection, carbon monoxide and
sleep debt all show up as changes in mood and thinking.

## The honest part

A long grid-down event would be hard, for a long time, and no preparation makes it pleasant. **What
preparation does is stop hardship becoming catastrophe**: it keeps people fed, warm, healthy, busy and
connected enough that the hard months are endurable. And there is another side: households that come
through long hardship often describe it as hard and meaningful, with closer relationships, real
competence and a clearer sense of what matters. That outcome is worth aiming at, and it depends more
on how people treat each other than on the pantry.

**Scavenge or substitute:** everything that matters most is free: routine, connection, purpose,
daylight, sleep, conversation and making things. Stories, songs and games need nothing. Book and game
swaps with neighbours (→ 18-07). Projects from salvage (→ 22-02). **What you cannot substitute:
other people.**

## When it runs out

- **What runs out is capacity, on a rough schedule:** the arc above. Expect the wall; plan for it.
- **Do not ration morale supplies to nothing early.** Spend some light, some coffee, some sweetness,
  on purpose and on a schedule (→ 21-06). A household that saves every pleasure for a rainy day finds
  every day is the rainy day.
- **Substitute** connection for entertainment, making for buying, routine for novelty, purpose for
  optimism. All renewable.
- **The reinvestment that pays most is community** (→ 18-01). Every one of the hardest scenarios in
  this binder is better with neighbours in it.
- **The honest floor:** if the household's morale is failing and there is no end in sight, that is
  information for the go/no-go decision (→ 01-02). A household that cannot hold its position should
  consider changing it. That is a legitimate input, not weakness.

## By phase

- **A (shelter in place):** neighbours are right there; use that. Build the routine, stock the
  indoor supplies, protect the evening block, get outside every day you can.
- **B (moving):** morale on the move is about **the next stop**, not the destination: short horizons,
  visible progress, warm food, and telling the children the plan (→ 20-08, → 01-03). Exhaustion
  dominates; manage it.
- **C (establish):** **isolation becomes the main morale risk** (CDC), replacing confinement. The work
  is more varied and visibly productive, which helps, but building community at a rural site is an
  early priority, not an afterthought (→ 18-01).
