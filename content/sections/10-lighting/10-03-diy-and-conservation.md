# DIY lamps and conserving light

**What this page is for.** The cheapest light is the light you never have to make. This page is
mostly about conserving it: using daylight, bouncing the light you have, and rearranging the day.
Homemade lamps come at the end. Anything here that involves a flame follows every rule on → 10-02.

> **CAUTION.** Flame indoors is a household decision ({{choice DC-20}} → 30-21); if you use it,
> every rule on → 10-02 applies. **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or
> chlorine products.** Fixed in every source. **Dark stairs are one of the most likely serious
> injuries in a long outage**, and there may be no emergency room: light them (below).

## Do this now

1. **Put something white where you will spend winter evenings.** A white ceiling or a white sheet
   on the wall behind a lamp can roughly double the useful light in a room, free, for good, with no
   batteries. This is the best lighting improvement in the binder.
2. **Move the household's evenings into one room** (→ 08-01). One lamp lighting one room where
   everyone is, instead of four lamps in four rooms, cuts lighting use by most of it, and the room
   is warmer and better for morale (→ 21-04).
3. **Rearrange for daylight now.** Put the table, the work surface and the children's play area by
   the best windows. In winter, useful daylight is short; **being in the right place to use it is
   free light.**
4. **Mark the stairs** with glow tape and put a night light at the top and bottom (below).
5. **Practise the homemade lamp once, outdoors, in daylight.** Building an oil lamp for the first
   time at night in a crisis is how accidents happen.

## Conservation first: the gains are huge

**1. Set the day by the sun.** Write down sunrise and sunset in late December where you live.
Moving the household's waking hours earlier (up at first light, main work in daylight, dinner at
dusk, bed early) can cut the hours you need artificial light by half or more. **This is the
biggest saving available, and it costs nothing.** It is how everyone lived until recently. Long
nights also mean long sleep, which is not a bad thing (→ 21-07).

**2. Gather the household.** One heated, lit room in the evening (→ 08-01). Light, heat, keeping
an eye on children, conversation and fuel all improve at once.

**3. Bounce, don't beam.** A bare light sends most of its output where nobody is looking, and a
point of light makes harsh shadows that tire the eyes.
- **Aim a lantern at a white ceiling** instead of at the room. The whole ceiling becomes the light.
- **Foil or a mirror behind a lamp** sends the backward half forward.
- **White sheets, white paper or pale walls** make the same light go further.
- **A see-through diffuser** (a milk jug, a bottle of water, a jar, tracing paper) spreads a
  headlamp into soft room light. **A headlamp strapped around a jug of water is an excellent
  improvised lantern.**

**4. Light the task, not the room.** You rarely need a lit room. You need light **on the book, the
cutting board, the wound, the pump**. Headlamps and clip lights use a tiny fraction of the energy
of lighting a whole room.

**5. Use the lowest setting that works.** Your eyes adapt. In a dark house a "moonlight" mode is
often plenty, and low runs many times as long as high (your light's packaging gives both run
times). Make low the default; keep high for outside.

**6. Red at night.** Keeps your night vision, disturbs sleeping children less, and is far harder to
see from outside (→ 10-05).

**7. Let daylight in.** Light-coloured curtains open on the sunny side by day (and closed at night
for warmth, → 08-03); snow outside a sunny window is a huge natural reflector; a mirror placed to
bounce daylight into a dark corner is an old trick that works.

**8. Do the light-needing work together.** Reading, mending, cleaning and paperwork in one lit
block, not five.

## The stairs: where light is a safety system

**A fall on dark stairs is a serious injury**, and in a long outage there may be no emergency
room. Engineer the stairs; do not improvise them.

- **Glow-in-the-dark (photoluminescent) safety tape on every stair edge.** It charges from daylight
  or lamplight and glows for hours, right where feet go: **no power, no batteries, no upkeep.** A
  strip along the edge of each step marks every tread in a fully dark house. Buy real
  photoluminescent safety tape, not novelty stickers.
- **A small night light at the top and bottom of the stairs**: plug-in while the grid is up,
  battery or USB LED after. A typical LED night light draws about half a watt, so two of them
  through a 12-hour winter night use about 12 watt-hours (a planning figure; check your own), and
  they are a **fixed line in the light budget** (→ 10-06).
- **Wrap the ends of the handrail** with the same glow tape. Make one hand on the rail the habit for
  every adult, every trip, especially when carrying anything.
- **Never carry a small child on dark stairs.** Light first: the night lights on, or your headlamp
  lit **before** you pick the child up. An adult with a child in both arms cannot catch the rail.
  If the light is not there, make it there first.{{#if has.infant}} There is an infant in this
  household: this rule applies every day.{{/if}}{{#if has.toddler}} There is a toddler in this
  household: gate the stairs and light them.{{/if}}{{#if has.elder}} An older member lives here:
  light and mark the stairs before anything else.{{/if}}{{#if has.mobility_aid}} A member uses a
  walker or wheelchair: light every route they use at night.{{/if}}

## Homemade light, safest first

**Headlamp and jug = lantern.** Strap a headlamp around a see-through jug of water, beam pointing
in. A soft lantern at once: no flame, no extra battery. **The best improvised light on this page;
make it your default.**

{{figure fig-jug-lantern}}

**Solar path lights, brought indoors.** Put them outside in the morning, bring them in at dusk. Dim,
but free and completely safe. **Good children's night lights.**

**12-volt LED strip from a battery.** Cut to length, stuck to a shelf edge, aimed at the ceiling.
The most efficient permanent light you can build (→ 09-08).

**Salvaged vehicle lights.** Dome lights, map lights and LED work lights run straight off 12 volts
and are everywhere.

**A water bottle in front of a light.** A clear bottle of water in front of a small light spreads
and softens it. Free and safe.

**Improvised oil lamp (a flame: all of → 10-02 applies).** A jar, a lid or float that cannot burn,
a cotton wick, and any liquid cooking oil or rendered fat:
- Wick: cotton string, a cotton fabric strip, jute twine. **Never synthetic: it melts and drips
  flame.**
- Keep the wick short: just enough showing for a small, steady flame. A long wick smokes.
- The oil does not catch fire easily; **the wick burns and draws the oil up.** That makes an oil
  lamp safer than a puddle of fuel, but a tipped lamp still spreads burning oil.
- **Contained, low, stable, on a surface that cannot burn, watched; never in a bedroom, never
  overnight, never where a child or pet can reach.**

**Candle from scraps.** Melt old candle stubs in a double boiler (**never** directly on the heat:
wax fires are serious) and pour into a jar around a cotton wick (→ 10-04).

**Fire glow.** A wood stove or fireplace that is already burning gives real background light for
free. Not enough for tasks, but enough to move around by.

## What not to improvise

- **No gasoline, camp fuel, white gas, alcohol or solvent in a homemade wick lamp.** This is how
  people are killed and burned. Heavy oils and fats only. (A purpose-built, sealed alcohol lamp is a
  different device, → 10-02, and denatured alcohol is a poison as well as a fuel.)
- **No open dish of burning liquid**, ever, especially alcohol: its flame can be nearly invisible.
- **No flame in a bedroom, left alone, or where children sleep.** Repeated because it is the rule
  people break.
- **No opening lithium batteries** to take out cells for light projects (→ 09-07; NFPA, Lithium-Ion
  Batteries).
- **No flame in a tent, a vehicle or any small closed space**: carbon monoxide, fabric and no room
  to escape (→ 08-02).

## Gear

**Have now:**

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| White paint, white sheets or white poster board | Roughly doubles the light in a room, free, for good | The best-value lighting purchase |
| See-through jugs and bottles (keep a few) | Headlamp diffusers: instant lanterns | Free |
| Aluminium foil, small mirrors | Reflectors behind lamps; bounce daylight | Cheap |
| Clip-on lights and small clamps | Task light exactly where needed | Low draw |
| 12-volt LED strip, connectors, fuses | Efficient permanent room light | → 09-08 |
| Cotton string or cotton fabric for wicks | The consumable in every homemade lamp | **Cotton only** |
| Wide-mouth canning jars and lids | Lamp bodies, candle moulds | Endlessly useful |
| Trays and tiles that cannot burn | Under every lamp | Cheap insurance |
| Solar path lights (several) | Free, safe background light and children's night lights | Out each morning |
| Photoluminescent safety tape (a roll) | Stair edges and handrail ends: no-power fall prevention | Real safety-grade tape |
| Two small night lights for the stairs | The stairs are never dark | A fixed line on → 10-06 |

**If you do not have it:**
- **Reflectors:** foil, chip bags, mirrors, white-painted plywood, aluminium flashing, a polished pot
  lid.
- **Diffusers:** milk jugs, soda bottles, plastic food tubs, frosted glass, a white T-shirt over an
  LED lamp (**never over a hot lamp or a flame**).
- **Lamp bodies:** cans, jars, ceramic bowls, a hollowed potato or turnip (a real old-fashioned
  lamp), a shell.
- **Wicks:** cotton shoelaces, mop strands, twisted cotton balls, torn cotton sheet, jute twine.
- **Fuel:** cooking oil, bacon grease, rendered tallow (→ 05-16), old candle stubs.
- **Light sources:** vehicle bulbs, bike lights, solar garden lights, a phone screen on its lowest
  brightness for close work, emergency exit-sign LEDs.

## When it runs out

- **Conservation never runs out.** Shifting the day, gathering in one room and reflectors keep
  working when every battery is dead.
- **Wick material** is the quiet consumable of every homemade lamp. Cotton string, rag and jute are
  cheap now; an old cotton sheet is a large supply.
- **Oil and fat compete with food.** **Burning fat for light is burning calories.** In a real
  shortage that is a real decision, and it usually favours eating the fat and going to bed early.
  Track it on → 23-02.
- **The fallback ladder:** LED on solar, then LED on stored cells, then the glow of a fire you were
  burning anyway, then a contained oil lamp, then **sleep**. Sleeping through the dark is not a
  failure; it is how people have always lived, and it costs nothing.
- **Spend light on morale, on purpose** (→ 21-02, → 21-04). Children need light for bedtime
  routines, stories and school work (→ 20-03); adults need enough to read and to feel the evening
  exists. Budget a fixed lit block each evening and protect it.

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** white ceiling, one gathered room, headlamp-and-jug lanterns, solar
  path lights in at dusk, stairs marked and lit. Day shifted early in winter.
- **B (on the move):** headlamps on low, red mode, a jug diffuser at rest stops. **No flame.** Light
  discipline is a safety matter (→ 10-05).
- **C (settled somewhere new):** build permanent 12-volt lighting and pale, reflective interior
  surfaces; use flame more freely in a building you control; start making your own fuel and wicks
  (→ 10-04) so lighting becomes renewable instead of stocked.
