# Making candles and oil lamps

**What this page is for.** Batteries and bought fuel run out; fat and wax can be renewed. This page
shows how to render fat into tallow, make jar candles, and make simple oil lamps from cooking oil,
safely. Making them is more dangerous than burning them.

> **SAFETY.**
> 1. **Melting wax and rendering fat is a hot-fat fire hazard.** Wax or fat heated too far
>    **catches fire**, and **water on a wax or grease fire flashes the hot fat into a fireball.**
>    Melt **low and slow, in a double boiler (a pot inside a pot of water), never on direct heat,
>    never left alone.** A metal lid and a fire blanket or extinguisher within reach (→ 29-12). If
>    it smokes, it is too hot: turn off the heat. If it catches fire: lid on, heat off, do not move
>    the pot, **never water**.
> 2. **Everything you make is still an open flame.** Every rule on → 10-02 applies: contained,
>    stable, watched, out of reach of children and pets, never in a bedroom, never overnight.
>    **USFA still says use flashlights, not candles** (USFA, Candle Fire Safety). Flame indoors is a
>    household decision: {{choice DC-20}} → 30-21.
> 3. **Bought lamp oil or kerosene in any of these lamps is a poison in the room.** Cooking oil and
>    tallow are food; lamp oil and kerosene are thin fuels a child can breathe into the lungs with
>    one swallow. **Do not make the child vomit**: that causes the lung injury. **Call Poison
>    Control, 1-800-222-1222**; with no phone, → 12-17 (CDC MMWR; Poison Control).
> 4. **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products.** Fixed in
>    every source. Children stay out of the kitchen while fat is hot.

## Do this now

1. **Practise once, now, outdoors or with a window open, with an extinguisher beside you.** Hot fat
   is unforgiving; do not meet it for the first time by lamplight.
2. **Start saving wax and fat.** Candle stubs, old jar candles, broken crayons, bacon grease, beef
   fat trimmings, cooking fat past food use. It costs nothing and it is a real light reserve.
3. **Stock cotton wick material**: cotton butcher's twine, cotton string, an old cotton sheet.
   **Cotton only.** Synthetic melts and drips flame.
4. **Buy wide-mouth canning jars in quantity.** They hold candles, lamps, food and half the
   improvisations in this binder.

## The raw materials

- **Tallow** (rendered beef, lamb or deer fat): the traditional candle fat. Hard at room
  temperature, so a tallow candle stands up. Modest smell. **The best candle material you can
  produce yourself.**
- **Lard** (rendered pork fat): softer. Better in a jar lamp than as a standing candle.
- **Any cooking oil** (vegetable, olive, canola, bacon grease): liquid, so it can only be a **lamp**,
  never a candle. The easiest lamp fuel there is.
- **Beeswax and paraffin:** the good stuff. Beeswax is renewable if you keep bees; paraffin comes
  from old candles. **Remelted candle stubs are free candles** and the easiest win here.

## Rendering fat (making tallow)

1. **Trim the fat** and cut it small, or grind it. More surface means faster, cleaner rendering.
2. **Melt it low and slow**: a heavy pot on the lowest heat, or a double boiler, with a little water
   in the bottom at the start to stop scorching. **Low. Slow. Watched.** If it smokes, the heat is
   too high.
3. **When the solid bits (cracklings) turn golden and sink, strain** through cloth into a clean
   jar. Strain twice: impurities make a candle smoke.
4. **Cool.** It sets white and firm, and keeps a long time cool and covered. Rancid fat still burns;
   it just smells worse.
5. **Cracklings are food** if the fat came from food-safe handling: treat them like any cooked meat
   (→ 04-12; FoodSafety.gov, Safe Minimum Internal Temperatures).

**Throughout:** hot fat, a flame or hot stove, and a distracted adult is the recipe for a kitchen
fire. **Lid within reach. Extinguisher within reach. Never water on a fat fire. Children out of the
kitchen.**

## Making candles

1. **Melt wax or tallow in a double boiler**: a can or jar standing in a pot of simmering water.
   **Never direct heat.** The water bath cannot get hotter than boiling, 212 °F (100 °C), and that
   is what makes it safe. **Typical figures:** paraffin and tallow melt at about 120 to 150 °F (50
   to 65 °C); **pour at about 175 °F (80 °C)**; never let the melt pass about 200 °F (95 °C).
   Paraffin can catch fire at roughly 390 to 465 °F (200 to 240 °C), and tallow smokes at about
   390 °F (200 °C): temperatures a pot on direct heat reaches in minutes and a water bath never
   can. These are typical material values; your wax may differ, so stay low. **Smoke means you are
   already too hot: turn off the heat.**
2. **Prepare the wick.** Cotton string, ideally dipped in melted wax and pulled straight first (a
   "primed" wick burns better). **Wick size matters most:** too thin and the candle drowns in its
   own melted wax; too thick and it smokes and burns fast. **Expect to experiment**: start with
   three strands of cotton butcher's twine braided, for a jar candle.
3. **Anchor the wick** at the bottom (a washer, a nut, a blob of hardened wax) and hold it straight
   and centred, tied to a pencil laid across the jar's mouth.
4. **Pour slowly** into the jar or mould. Too hot and it shrinks and cracks; too cool and it does
   not fill.
5. **Cool slowly, undisturbed.** A dip usually forms around the wick; top it up with a second pour.
6. **Trim the wick to about 1/4 inch** before lighting. A long wick is the main cause of smoking,
   sooty candles.

{{figure fig-double-boiler}}

**Jar (container) candles are safer than standing candles**: the flame is enclosed, the melted wax
stays put, and the jar does not tip easily. **Make jar candles.**

**Dipped candles** are made by dipping a wick into melted tallow again and again, cooling between
dips, over dozens of dips. Traditional and slow, and the candles stand free and can tip. A useful
skill, a less safe product.

## Making an oil lamp (easier and better than candles)

Faster, cheaper and safer, and it uses liquid oil or fat directly.

**Float-wick jar lamp:**
1. A wide-mouth jar, half to two-thirds full of oil or melted fat.
2. A **float** to hold the wick upright: a bottle cap, or a disc of cork or metal with a hole
   punched through, floating on the oil, the wick threaded through it.
3. **Only about 1/8 inch of wick above the float.** More smokes badly.
4. Light it. The float keeps the flame at the surface as the oil drops; it burns until the oil is
   gone and then goes out.

{{figure fig-float-wick-lamp}}

**Lid-wick jar lamp:** punch a hole in a metal jar lid, run the wick through with its tail in the
oil, screw the lid on. Even simpler. Keep the exposed wick short.

**Why oil lamps are fairly forgiving:** the oil in the jar is far below the temperature at which it
catches fire. **The wick burns, drawing oil up.** A knocked-over lamp still spreads burning oil, so
**containment and stability matter absolutely**, but it is not a pool of fuel waiting for a spark.

**Never in a wick lamp: gasoline, camp fuel, white gas, naphtha, alcohol or any solvent.** They turn
to vapour and the lamp becomes a bomb. **Heavy oils and fats only.** (Alcohol belongs only in a
purpose-built, sealed alcohol lamp or stove, → 10-02, never an improvised jar.) If you put **bought
lamp oil or K-1 kerosene** in a jar lamp instead of cooking oil, it is a poison too: see the safety
box above and → 12-17.

## Getting usable light out of it

Homemade light is dim: a candle or fat lamp gives a small fraction of an LED lantern's light. Use
→ 10-03: bounce it off a white ceiling, put foil behind it, put two or three small lamps where you
are working rather than one in the middle of the room, and keep it close to the task.

## Gear

**Have now:**

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Wide-mouth canning jars (many) | Candle jars, lamp bodies, everything else | Buy in quantity |
| **Cotton** wick material: butcher's twine, string, rag | The consumable in every candle and lamp | **Never synthetic** |
| A melting pot or large can, and a pot for the water bath | The double boiler is the safety mechanism | Kept apart from food cookware |
| Candy or fat thermometer | Keeps you well below the smoke point | Pour at about 175 °F; never over about 200 °F |
| Fire extinguisher, metal lid, fire blanket | Hot-fat fire: **never water** | Within reach before you start (→ 29-12) |
| Fine strainer and cloth | Clean tallow burns cleaner | Strain twice |
| Washers, nuts or bottle caps | Wick anchors and floats | Easy to improvise |
| Beeswax or paraffin | Better light than fat | Also waterproofs cloth and thread (→ 16-04) |
| Hot pads, long tongs, apron | Burns are the common injury | → 12-03 |

**If you do not have it:**
- **Wax:** old candles and stubs, tea lights, broken crayons, wax toilet-bowl rings (really used for
  this), cheese wax, canning wax, beeswax from a hive.
- **Fat:** trimmings from any butchered animal (→ 05-09), bacon grease, drippings saved in a jar,
  fat from poultry and rabbits (→ 05-16), suet.
- **Wicks:** cotton shoelaces, mop strands, twisted cotton balls, torn cotton sheet, jute twine.
- **Containers:** cans, jars, ceramic dishes, shells, a hollowed potato or turnip.
- **Moulds:** cans, cardboard tubes lined with paper, ice trays, small jars.
- **What you cannot improvise:** the double-boiler discipline. Improvising **that** is how kitchens
  burn.

## When it runs out

- **The limit is fat, and fat is food** (→ 04-12). Burning tallow for light is burning calories. In
  a real shortage that trade usually loses: **eat the fat, go to bed early, and light the evening
  with the fire you are already burning.** List fat on the master consumables table (→ 23-02) as
  one resource shared between food, cooking, soap and light.
- **How long it lasts:** a small jar lamp burns for many hours on a modest amount of oil; a jar
  candle runs for tens of hours. Still a countdown unless you are producing fat.
- **Resupply:** animals you butcher (→ 05-09, → 05-16), cooking fat saved instead of thrown out, and
  beeswax if you keep bees. **Beekeeping is one of the few truly renewable light sources**, and it
  gives food and pollination too (→ 05-12).
- **Move back up the ladder to LED on solar** (→ 09-02) whenever you can. Homemade flame is the floor
  of the lighting plan, not the goal.
- **The skill is the asset.** Rendering, wicking and pouring are learnable in an afternoon and
  impossible to learn from a book at midnight. Practise now (→ 24-01).

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** practise and stockpile. Save every candle stub and every jar of
  grease. Make a few jar candles so you know your wick size works.
- **B (on the move):** do not make candles on the move. Carry a couple of finished jar candles at
  most and rely on headlamps (→ 10-01).
- **C (settled somewhere new):** this becomes part of the household economy: tallow from every
  animal butchered, beeswax if you keep hives, a shelf of jar candles, lamp oil from cooking fat.
  Light stops being a countdown and becomes a crop.
