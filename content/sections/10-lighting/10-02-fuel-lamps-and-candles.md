# Fuel lamps and candles (fire risk)

**What this page is for.** This is a page of last resort. Batteries eventually run out, so this
page covers candles, oil and kerosene lamps, lanterns and homemade fat lamps: which are least bad,
the rules that keep them from burning the house down, and how to keep children from drinking the
fuel.

> **SAFETY.**
> - **In a power outage, use flashlights, not candles** (US Fire Administration, Candle Fire
>   Safety). About 20 home candle fires are reported in the US every day (USFA, citing NFPA).
>   USFA says **do not use lit candles in bedrooms, bathrooms or sleeping areas.**
> - **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products.** Fixed
>   in every source. Lamp oil, kerosene and lighters count.
> - **Fuel is never stored or transferred near an open flame or a running appliance.** Vapour
>   ignition. Fill lamps away from any flame, never while lit or hot.
> - **Swallowed lamp oil or kerosene:** these thin fuels slip into the lungs and cause a chemical
>   pneumonia, and **vomiting can carry more of it into the lungs.** **Do not make them vomit;
>   wipe out the mouth; call Poison Control, 1-800-222-1222** (CDC MMWR; MedlinePlus, Poisoning
>   first aid). Coughing, choking or fast breathing means it reached the lungs. How long to keep
>   watching, and the full protocol: → 12-17.
>
> **Four hazards:** (1) **fire**; (2) **carbon monoxide (CO) and fumes**: any flame in a closed
> room uses oxygen and makes CO, which you cannot smell (→ 29-03); (3) **burns and spilled fuel**:
> a knocked-over oil lamp spreads burning liquid; (4) **swallowing the fuel**.

**Whether to use candles and oil lamps indoors at all** is a household decision:
{{choice DC-20}} → 30-21. This page gives the rules for doing it as safely as it can be done.

## Do this now

1. **Make LED the default; this page is the backup** (→ 10-01).
2. **Smoke alarms and CO alarms on every level, battery-powered, tested**, before any lamp is ever
   lit indoors. Test them monthly (USFA, Smoke Alarms). This is the prerequisite, not an extra
   (→ 29-12).
3. **Set the house rules now, out loud, with every adult, and teach the children:** no flame in
   bedrooms; no flame left alone, ever, even for a minute; no flame under anything that can burn;
   no child carries a flame; nothing burning when everyone goes to sleep.
4. **Buy proper lamps and the fuel they were made for, together.** The homemade versions on
   → 10-04 are for when you have no choice; a real lamp with a glass chimney and its own fuel is
   far safer.
5. **An extinguisher in each room where flame is used**, plus a pot lid or fire blanket in the
   kitchen (→ 29-12, → 12-03).
6. **Store lamp fuel as a poison:** original child-resistant container, capped, up high or locked;
   **never poured into a drink bottle, jar or cup**; filled lamps out of children's reach even when
   cold; fill lamps where the children are not.

## The rules, in order of how often they are broken

1. **Never leave a flame alone.** Blow it out when you leave the room and when you go to bed. This
   one rule prevents most candle fires (USFA).
2. **Never in a bedroom, a bathroom or where anyone sleeps** (USFA). A candle left burning "just
   while we fall asleep" is the classic fatal fire.
3. **At least 12 inches clear of anything that can burn** (USFA): curtains, bedding, paper, books,
   decorations, clothes drying near the stove, firewood.
4. **On a sturdy, uncluttered surface that cannot burn and cannot tip.** Never on a shelf, never on
   a table a child can pull, never where it can be bumped in the dark.
5. **Out of reach of children and pets. Physically** out of reach, not "probably high enough".
6. **Ventilate.** A sealed, winterised room (→ 08-03) with a burning lamp builds CO and uses up
   oxygen. **Crack a window.** This is the tension that makes flame light dangerous in winter: the
   house is sealed for warmth and the lamp needs air.
7. **Keep hair, sleeves and loose clothing away.** A long sleeve over a candle is a common burn.
8. **Extinguisher, lid and water within arm's reach before you light.**
9. **Trim wicks and use proper holders.** A long wick makes a big, smoky flame; a proper holder
   catches drips and does not tip.
10. **Never move a burning oil lamp, and never refill one that is lit or hot.**

## The devices, worst to best

### Candles: the worst option, and the most tempting

Cheap, everywhere, need nothing. Also a leading cause of fires, very little usable light, and
easily knocked over. If you use them:

- **Enclosed jar or hurricane candles only**: the flame inside glass that does not tip easily and
  cannot easily touch anything.
- **No tapers or pillar candles on an open surface.**
- **Never carried by anyone.** Carry a headlamp; leave the candle where it stands.
- **Never in a child's room, never overnight.**
- **Battery "flameless candles" are a good substitute** where you want the feel without the risk;
  USFA recommends them. Put them in the children's rooms.

### Kerosene and lamp-oil lamps: the traditional answer, with real caveats

A proper wick lamp with a glass chimney gives good, steady, warm light for hours on a little fuel,
and a little heat.

- **Use only the fuel the lamp is made for**: lamp oil, or **K-1 clear kerosene** in a kerosene
  lamp (→ 29-01). **Never gasoline, camp fuel (white gas), alcohol or diesel in a wick lamp**: it
  causes fires and explosions and is a documented way people die. The lamp maker's instructions
  decide what fuel it takes.
- **"Lamp oil" is a category, not one thing.** Clear, highly refined paraffin lamp oil is fine in a
  flat-wick kerosene lamp. **Citronella or "tiki torch" oil and coloured or scented decorative oils
  are outdoor-only**: soot and irritating fumes. They are the most common thing in a garage
  labelled "lamp oil". Read the label before it goes in a lamp.
- **Kerosene grades:** only clear K-1 belongs indoors; red-dyed or unknown pump kerosene is treated
  as the sootier K-2 and kept outdoors (MU Extension, G1999; GHP Group/Dyna-Glo kerosene heater
  manual: "never use any fuel other than water-clear 1-K"; → 06-06).
- **Labels:** kerosene is usually kept in a **blue** can, gasoline in **red**. Trust the label,
  not the colour: **read it aloud, smell, then pour** (→ 06-06).
- **Old kerosene:** water gets into part-full cans, and wet or dirty fuel smokes, smells and makes
  more CO. Date every can, burn the oldest first, and jar-test before filling (→ 06-06). Whether to
  burn kerosene that has been stored a long time is a household decision: {{choice DC-17}} → 30-18.
- **Fill outdoors or over a tray**, the lamp cold and out.
- **Ventilate.** Kerosene lamps make CO, soot and fumes, and they smell.
- **Trim the wick** so it burns clean. A smoking lamp puts soot in your lungs and on the ceiling.
- **Storage:** fuel cans stay outside the living space (→ 06-06). Only the day's fill comes inside,
  in its labelled container.{{#if has.kerosene_heater}} Your kerosene heater shares the same
  fuel stock: count both jobs against it (→ 08-10, → 10-06).{{/if}}

### Pressurised liquid-fuel and propane lanterns: bright, hot, and for outdoors

A pressurised camping lantern is very bright and makes **serious** amounts of CO and heat.
**Outdoors only.** A garage with the door open is not ventilated (→ 08-02). The mantles (the small
fabric bags that glow) are fragile and get used up: stock spares, or the lantern is useless.

### Alcohol lamps: clean-burning, in a purpose-built lamp only

Denatured alcohol burns clean, with little soot or smell. But an **alcohol flame can be nearly
invisible in daylight**, and spilled burning alcohol spreads and is hard to see.

- **Only a purpose-built, sealed alcohol lamp or stove** (a closed reservoir with its own wick or
  burner).
- **Alcohol never goes in an open or improvised wick lamp** (→ 10-03, → 10-04): it turns to vapour
  and the jar becomes a fireball. An open dish of alcohol is not a lamp.
- **Denatured alcohol contains methanol.** Swallowed methanol can cause blindness and death, and
  breathing a lot of its vapour is as harmful as swallowing it (CDC/NIOSH, Methanol). Same storage
  rules as lamp oil, same Poison Control number (→ 12-17).

### Fat, tallow and oil lamps: the ones you can make

Any fat or vegetable oil with a wick will burn (→ 10-04). Dim, smoky and smelly, but **renewable**,
which is why it is in this binder. In a jar, low, stable, and watched.

## Children and pets, specifically

- **Nothing burning at floor level or anywhere a pet can reach.** Cats jump onto every surface;
  assume any flat surface is reachable. A cat's tail crosses a flame without the cat noticing.
- **Nothing burning where a crawling or toddling child is awake.**
- **Nothing a child can drink.** Lamp oil and kerosene look like water or juice in a clear
  container. Original containers, capped, up high or locked.
- **Teach young children the rule in words they will repeat:** "Fire stays where a grown-up puts
  it. We don't touch, we don't carry, we tell a grown-up." Then give each child **a headlamp of
  their own**: a light they are allowed to use makes the rule about flame much easier to keep
  (→ 20-04).
- **Flameless lights in the children's rooms, always.**
- **When everyone sleeps, everything is out.** No exceptions.

| Trays or tiles that cannot burn, under each lamp | Catch drips, stop spread | Cheap |
| A headlamp for every person, children included | Removes the **reason** to carry a flame | The real fix |

**If you do not have it:**
- **Lamps:** a jar, a lid punched for a wick, and oil (→ 10-04). Contained, low, stable.
- **Wicks:** cotton string, cotton fabric strips, mop cotton, cotton shoelaces, jute twine. **Never
  synthetic fabric**: it melts and drips flame instead of wicking.
- **Fuel:** cooking oil, rendered fat, tallow, old candle wax, paraffin. **Never gasoline, camp fuel
  or solvents in a lamp.**
- **Holders:** canning jars, cans, ceramic dishes, hurricane glass.
- **Reflectors:** foil behind a lamp sends its backward light forward, for free (→ 10-03).
- **What you cannot improvise:** a working smoke alarm and a working CO alarm. Buy several now, with
  batteries.

## When it runs out

- **Lamp oil and candles go fast** in daily use: a lamp may run a handful of hours per fill, and
  winter nights are long. Count fuel in **hours of light** and write it on → 23-02. Most households
  have days, not months.
- **One lamp for the household**, in the gathered room (→ 08-01), not one each, and only during the
  evening. **Shift the day earlier**: daylight is free.
- **Resupply:** → 10-04: tallow and fat candles, oil lamps from cooking fat, old candle wax
  remelted. Sustainable at a low level, and worth practising **before** the store-bought candles
  are gone.
- **Move back toward LED as flame fuel runs low**, not deeper into flame. Rechargeable cells and a
  solar panel are a renewable light supply (→ 09-02); candles are not.
- **The habit that outlasts every fuel:** sleeping when it is dark. It costs nothing.

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** flame is backup only, in the one heated gathered room, watched, with
  alarms and an extinguisher. Children's rooms get flameless lights. A flickering window is visible
  a long way (→ 10-05).
- **B (on the move):** **no open flame while moving with children.** Headlamps only. A lamp in a
  vehicle or tent is a fire and CO hazard in a small space, and it ruins everyone's night vision.
- **C (settled somewhere new):** more room to use flame safely in a building you control, and the
  phase where making candles and lamp oil (→ 10-04) becomes a real, renewable part of the
  household: tallow from butchered animals (→ 05-16), fat from cooking, wax from bees if you keep
  them.
