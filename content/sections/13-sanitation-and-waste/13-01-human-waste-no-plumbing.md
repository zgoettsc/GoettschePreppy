# Human waste without plumbing

**What this page is for.** What to do when the toilet stops working: how to tell what is
broken, how to build and run a bucket toilet, and where the waste goes. Getting this right is
one of the highest-value things in this binder, and it costs almost nothing.

> **SAFETY.** **Sanitation failure is one of the classic ways a survivable situation becomes a
> deadly one.** Poor sanitation spreads diarrheal diseases such as cholera and dysentery, and
> typhoid and intestinal worms; WHO counts hundreds of thousands of deaths a year from unsafe
> sanitation, mostly from diarrhea (WHO, Sanitation fact sheet). **Human waste that gets into
> land or water spreads disease to everyone downstream** (CDC, About Global Sanitation).
>
> Diarrhea dehydrates infants and young children dangerously fast (→ 12-05), and it is a
> serious sick-day event for anyone with diabetes{{#if has.condition.CL-01}} (→ 02-01){{/if}}.
>
> **Always: gloves for handling waste, hands washed with soap and water straight after, and
> waste kept far from anything anyone drinks, eats or touches** (CDC, Protecting Workers
> Handling Human Waste or Sewage).

## Do this now

1. **Buy the bucket-toilet kit today:** two 5-gallon buckets, a snap-on toilet seat lid,
   heavy-duty bags, and a large bag of cover material (sawdust, wood shavings, peat moss or
   dry leaves). Small cost; enormous problem solved.
2. **Stock heavy-duty contractor bags**, several boxes.
3. **Buy far more toilet paper than seems reasonable, and a bidet bottle or peri bottle** (a
   squeeze bottle for rinsing with water). It uses a fraction of the paper and works better.
4. **Decide now where waste goes:** the container, the storage spot and the final disposal
   (→ 13-07). Deciding during the crisis is how mistakes happen.
5. **Know your sewer situation.** If the sewer works but there is no water pressure, you can
   usually still use the toilet by **pouring a bucket of water into the bowl** to flush it.
   That is the easiest answer; check it first.
   {{#if has.septic}}Your house is on a septic system: it needs no power to work (→ 13-12).{{/if}}

## First: work out what is actually broken

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| **No water pressure, sewer working** | **Pour-flush with a bucket of water.** Water you would not drink is fine: rain, snowmelt, pool water. **Best option; check it first** |
| **Sewer backed up or broken** | Stop using the toilet at once. Use the bucket system below |
| **Pipes or the drain line frozen** | → 13-05. Do not pour water into a frozen line |
| **Flooding, sewer backing up into the street** | Do not use the toilet; sewage will come back up into the house |
| **Septic system** | **Keep using it carefully: → 13-12.** A gravity septic system needs no power, only water and a working drain field (EPA) |

**A pour-flush uses about as much water as the toilet's own flush:** EPA says newer
high-efficiency toilets use 1.6 gallons or less, and many older ones 3.5 to 5 gallons (US EPA,
How to Care for Your Septic System). That is a lot when water is rationed (→ 03-01). **Pour it
fast, straight into the BOWL, not the tank.** Try it once now with a marked bucket to learn
the least that clears your toilet. **Flush for solids only and let urine collect between flushes.** "If it's yellow, let
it mellow" is correct water-saving practice in a crisis.{{#if has.pool}} Your pool can supply
flush water (→ 03-03).{{/if}}

### Protecting the house from sewer backup

When town sewers fail, sewage can rise back up through the lowest drains in the house: basement
floor drains, showers and toilets. Heavy rain, more than power loss, is the usual cause, and
storms often bring both.

- **Know your lowest drains.** {{#if has.basement}}In your basement, find the floor drain, any
  shower, and any toilet.{{else}}Find the lowest floor drain, shower and toilet in the house.{{/if}}
- **Drain plugs** (rubber expanding or mechanical gripper plugs sized to the drain) can sit in
  floor drains and showers that you are not using. Pour water in a floor drain first: if it
  runs to a sump pit rather than the sewer, it cannot back up from the sewer.
- **A backwater valve** (a one-way flap in the main drain, fitted by a plumber) is the
  permanent fix. Before the collapse it is worth asking a plumber about.
- **An inflatable drain plug through the main cleanout** (the capped access pipe on the main
  drain) seals the whole house drain at one point. While it is in, **nothing drains in either
  direction.** Tie its safety cord so it cannot be lost in the pipe.
- **Stage towels and a wet-dry vacuum** by the lowest fixture. Write where your main cleanout
  is: __________ (→ 16-12).

## The bucket toilet: the standard answer

Simple, cheap and effective, and it works indoors in winter, which very few alternatives do.

{{figure fig-bucket-toilet}}

**Build it:**

1. **Two 5-gallon buckets:** one in use, one being emptied and cleaned.
2. **A snap-on toilet seat lid.** Comfort matters more than you would think over months, and
   the lid seals in smell.
3. **A heavy-duty bag** lining the bucket, or **no bag** if the waste is going to a compost
   system (→ 13-06).
4. **Cover material** in a second container beside it: sawdust, wood shavings, peat moss,
   shredded dry leaves, dry grass, sand or wood ash.
5. **A scoop.**

**Use it:**

- **Put a layer of cover material in the bottom before first use.**
- **Cover every deposit completely** with a scoop of material. **This is the whole trick.**
  Covering controls smell almost entirely and keeps flies off, and flies carry disease.
- **Keep the lid closed.**
- **Collect urine separately if you can.** Urine is most of the volume, and it is what makes
  the bucket heavy, smelly and quick to fill. A separate jug or bucket for urine makes the
  solids bucket last much longer and cuts smell sharply. **This is the biggest single improvement you can
  make.**
- **Wash hands with soap and water straight afterwards** (→ 13-02). Put a handwashing station
  right next to the toilet. This is not optional.

{{photo toilet-setup}}

**Empty it:**

- **Gloves and, ideally, rubber boots** (CDC).
- **Where it goes** depends on your situation: bagged and stored, a compost system (→ 13-06),
  or a properly placed latrine (→ 13-07). **Never into a storm drain, a creek, a ditch, or
  anywhere water flows.**
- **Wash hands. Every time. Properly.**

### When the bucket is full

1. **Tie the bag off** with the air pressed out, gloves on.
2. **Double-bag it.** Use a contractor-weight outer bag.
3. **Into a lidded, animal-proof storage container**, outdoors, away from living space, water
   storage and food. In winter, the cold side of the house: **frozen waste is inert and does
   not smell.**
4. **When trash collection and sewers are gone for good:** bury it by the distances on
   → 13-07 (at least 100 feet from any well, spring or surface water, downhill, with at least
   5 feet of dry soil between the bottom of the pit and the water table), or add it to a
   running compost or latrine system (→ 13-06). The 200-foot figure on → 13-07 is for shallow
   catholes while traveling, not for pits; the two do not conflict.
5. **Wash hands with soap. Every time.**

## The hard part: where does it actually go?

This is the part people skip, and the part that matters.

{{#if is.urban}}**In a town or city this is genuinely difficult.** A town lot has no room for a
latrine at safe distances from neighbors and water (→ 13-07), neighbors close on all sides,
local rules about waste, and sometimes a high water table or a river nearby.{{else}}**Even on
a rural property,** check the distances to wells, streams and the house before choosing a spot
(→ 13-07).{{/if}}

**Realistic options at home, best first:**

1. **Pour-flush while the sewer works.** By far the best. Check it first, and keep checking.
2. **Bag and store**, sealed in heavy bags in a sealed container in a cold place, until
   disposal or until service comes back. **Winter helps a lot: waste freezes and stops
   smelling.** This is the realistic short-to-medium-term answer in town.
3. **A composting system** (→ 13-06), if you have yard space, discipline and time. Composting
   human waste properly takes a long time and real management; done badly it is a disease
   hazard.
4. **A properly placed pit** (→ 13-07): usually not possible on a town lot.

> **Before the collapse:** local codes govern waste disposal, pits and composting toilets. They
> never decide whether you keep waste away from water to stay alive.

**Be honest that option 2 piles up.** A household produces a steady, large volume. **Work out
where a month's worth goes before you are a month in.**

## Rules that prevent a disease outbreak

1. **Waste never goes near water, food or food preparation** (CDC, WHO): not the water store,
   not the edible parts of the garden, not the kitchen.
2. **Handwashing with soap and water after every use.** The single most effective control
   (→ 13-02).
3. **Cover everything:** smell, flies and exposure, all at once.
4. **Gloves for handling and emptying**, and wash hands afterwards anyway.
5. **Tools and containers kept only for this**, never used for anything else. Label them.
6. **Keep children away from the waste system entirely** (→ 20-02), and manage diapers
   separately (→ 13-08, → 20-06).
7. **Anyone sick uses their own container if possible** (→ 13-11); treat their waste as more
   dangerous.
8. **Flies carry disease; they are not just a nuisance.** Cover, seal and screen.

## Toilet paper and the water-versus-paper trade

- **Stock deeply.** Bulky but cheap, and good for morale.
- **A bidet bottle or peri bottle** uses a little water, cuts paper use sharply and is more
  hygienic. A very good preparedness purchase almost nobody makes.
- **Cloth wipes**, washed (→ 13-04), are the long-term answer, at a cost in water and soap.
  Wash them completely apart from all other laundry.
- **Nothing but human waste and toilet paper goes into a system you will compost**: no wipes,
  no plastic, no hygiene products.

**If you don't have it:**

- **Buckets:** restaurants, bakeries, building sites, hardware stores.
- **Cover material:** sawdust from firewood cutting (→ 06-04), shredded dry leaves, dry grass,
  peat, sand, dry soil, wood ash, shredded paper.
- **Bags:** any heavy plastic; double up thin ones.
- **Seat:** a wooden frame over the bucket, a foam pool noodle split along its length and
  pressed over the rim (comfortable), or the seat from your own toilet.
- **Handwashing:** a jug with a spigot, a bottle with a pinhole, or a **tippy-tap** (a hanging
  jug tipped by a foot lever: a standard, very effective low-water design).
- **Toilet paper:** washable cloth wipes, newspaper (crumple it to soften it), or water and the
  left hand as much of the world uses. **All of these raise the need for handwashing**, which is
  fine as long as you actually do it.

## When it runs out

- **Bags and cover material are the consumables.** Bags are the harder one: stock boxes now.
  **A bucket system without bags becomes a compost system** (→ 13-06), which works but needs
  land and management.
- **Runway:** use the bucket maths above and keep the consumables table (→ 23-02) up to date
  with real use.
- **Ration:** **separate urine**, the biggest extender. Get cover material from your own yard
  and firewood. Pour-flush whenever the sewer works.
- **Substitute:** cover material is almost unlimited (sawdust, leaves, ash, soil). **What you
  cannot substitute is somewhere for it to go**, which is why → 13-07 matters, and why this is
  harder in town than at a rural site.
- **Long term, a proper system:** composting (→ 13-06) or a correctly placed latrine (→ 13-07)
  on land. **In town, over a long event, waste disposal is a real reason to consider leaving**
  (→ 01-02), alongside water and food.
- **What never runs out is discipline:** cover it, wash your hands, keep it away from water.
  Those three habits prevent most of what this page warns about.

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** check the sewer first and pour-flush if it works. Otherwise a
  bucket toilet indoors, urine separated, waste bagged and stored in a sealed container in a
  cold spot. Plan the disposal path before storage fills.
- **B (moving):** bury waste properly and away from water (→ 13-07), pack out what you must,
  and **carry soap and hand sanitizer**. Hygiene slips exactly when illness would be most
  dangerous (→ 12-05).
- **C (rural or at a destination):** finally solvable properly. **Place the latrine or compost
  system correctly on the first day** (→ 13-07): well away from and downhill of any water
  source, away from the garden and the kitchen. On a septic system, → 13-12 is the first move.
  Getting this right at the start prevents the illness that would otherwise arrive in month
  two.
