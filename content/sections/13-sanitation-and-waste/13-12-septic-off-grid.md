# Operating a septic system off-grid

**What this page is for.** A septic system is a buried sewage plant for one house. Most of them
need no electricity. This page explains how to keep one working when there is no pumping truck,
what must never go down the drain, and what to do when it struggles.
{{#if has.septic}}Your home is on septic: this page is for you now.{{else}}Your home is on
{{ dwelling.sewer }}. Keep this page for a destination or relative's house on septic.{{/if}}

> **SAFETY.**
> - **Where a place has a working septic system, the first sanitation move is to operate it,
>   not to dig a latrine.** A working septic system is a complete, buried, already-placed
>   sanitation plant. Wrecking it through ignorance, then digging holes next to a well, is the
>   failure this page prevents.
> - **When the grid is down, there are no pumping trucks. The tank cannot be emptied. So
>   saving water is the maintenance:** every gallon you keep out, and every wrong thing you keep
>   out of the tank, is the only service it will get.
> - **A drain field with sewage coming to the surface is raw sewage, full of germs.** Do not dig
>   into it, keep children and pets away (→ 20-02), and do not try to fix it with a shovel.

## Do this now

1. **Gather the records** for your system, and for any destination on septic: where the tank is,
   when it was last pumped, and the installer or pumper's paperwork. Fill in the survey below.
2. **Find out whether it is a gravity system or a pumped one** (below). It changes everything.
3. **Read the never-flush list below once now.** Most septic damage comes from habits brought
   over from city sewers.
4. **Keep a bucket-toilet kit** (→ 13-01), including in any load you take to a destination on
   septic. It is the relief valve that lets a stressed septic system rest.

## How a tank and drain field work (EPA)

{{figure fig-septic-system}}

Household waste water runs down one main drain pipe into a buried, watertight **septic tank**.
There, **solids sink to the bottom as sludge; fats and grease float to the top as scum**; the
cleared liquid in the middle (**effluent**) flows out to the **drain field**, a shallow covered
trench system in dry soil. **The soil does the treatment:** as the liquid soaks down, the soil
filters it and its organisms eat the bacteria, viruses and nutrients before the water reaches
groundwater (US EPA, How Your Septic System Works).

Two facts drive everything on this page:

1. **The tank is alive.** Microbes digest the waste. Poison them (chemicals, heavy loads of
   bleach) and the tank becomes a dead box that passes solids out to the field.
2. **The field can only soak up so much.** Flood it with water and sewage comes up on the lawn or
   backs up into the house (EPA).

## What changes when the grid is down

- **No pumping trucks.** Tanks are normally pumped every 3 to 5 years (US EPA, How to Care for
  Your Septic System). Without pumping, sludge and scum build up until they escape to the field,
  which is the one failure you cannot repair. **So slow the clock: less water, fewer solids,
  nothing that kills the microbes.** A tank pumped recently starts with years of room.
- **Pumped and mound systems** (with electric float switches and an effluent pump) stop working
  without power and back up quickly. A gravity system is the good answer. Find out which yours is
  (survey below).
- **Well pressure may be gone too** (→ 03-10). Pour-flushing from a bucket (→ 13-01) still feeds a
  septic system fine, and naturally rations water.

## The never-flush list: tape it next to every toilet (EPA)

**Only human waste and toilet paper go in. Never flush:**

- **Wipes, even "flushable" ones** (baby wipes, wet wipes)
- **Cooking grease or oil**
- **Household chemicals:** gasoline, oil, pesticides, antifreeze, paint, paint thinner, photo
  chemicals, drain cleaner
- **Large volumes of toxic cleaners, including bleach** (EPA: never pour large volumes of toxic
  cleaners down the drain). Disinfecting batches (→ 13-11), sanitizing tubs and
  well-disinfection water (→ 03-10) poured down the drain can kill the tank's microbes. Pour
  those outside, away from water and the field
- **Pads, tampons, condoms, dental floss, diapers, paper towels, cigarette butts, coffee grounds,
  cat litter** (litter: → 13-03)
- **Medicines**

## Managing the water load: the real maintenance

- **Spread the load.** Do not do all the laundry in one day (EPA). Space laundry (→ 13-04) and
  bathing across the week so the field soaks up each dose before the next.
- **Send greywater elsewhere where it is lawful.** Wash-basin and laundry water poured onto soil
  (→ 13-03) is water the field never sees: the biggest single saving. **Never divert toilet
  waste.**

  > **Before the collapse:** greywater rules differ by state. Check before re-plumbing anything.

- **Fix every leak and drip.** A running toilet floods a field.
- **Flush on purpose:** "if it's yellow, let it mellow" (→ 13-01); solids get one honest flush.

## Finding the tank and the field

- **Paperwork first:** the county health or environmental office's permit drawings, the
  inspection from when the house was sold, the pumper's receipts (which also date the last
  pump-out).
- **Look:** follow the drain pipe where it leaves the house; EPA says the tank is usually about
  10 feet from the building (US EPA, Frequent Questions on Septic Systems). Look for tank lids
  or risers (often a black or green disc), a rectangle of grass that grows differently, snow that melts
  first.
- **Probe gently** with a thin rod along that line to find the tank's edges. **Do not drive
  machinery around guessing:** crushing a lid or a pipe causes the failure you fear.

## Signs of failure, and what to do

**Signs (EPA):** sewage backing up into drains or toilets, slow drains, gurgling pipes · **standing
water, damp spots or soft, spongy ground over the tank or field** · sewage smell around the tank
or field · bright green, spongy, lush grass over the field even in dry weather.

**What to do, in order:**

1. **Stop putting water in. All of it.** Move the household to the bucket toilet (→ 13-01) at
   once: this is exactly what it is for.
2. **Let the system rest** for days, not hours. An overloaded field may recover once it drains
   down; there is no way to know how long without trying.
3. **Do NOT dig into a field with sewage on the surface:** the soil is soaked with germs. Fence it
   off from children and animals; sprinkle lime on pooling if you have it (→ 13-07).
4. **Start again slowly** with less water, and keep the bucket toilet in use for part of the
   household.
5. **If it will not recover,** the field is finished while the grid is down. A properly placed
   latrine (→ 13-07) becomes the main system.

## Winter (University of Minnesota Extension)

- **Snow and mulch are the insulation.** An undisturbed blanket of snow insulates the tank and
  field. In a cold snap with bare ground, a layer of mulch **8 to 12 inches** deep (straw,
  leaves, hay or any loose material that will not pack down) over the pipes, tank and field does
  the same job. Let the grass grow a little longer over it in late summer and fall.
- **Never drive over, park on, or pack down snow over the tank or field.** Packed snow loses its
  insulation and drives frost deep, and vehicles crush parts (EPA).
- **Regular warm water helps prevent freezing.** A house in daily use keeps its own lines warm; a
  little use beats none. Do not leave water running all the time to prevent freezing: it can
  overload the field. Fix dripping fixtures: a slow trickle freezes in the pipe.
- **If it freezes anyway:** stop using water, move to the bucket toilet, and wait for the thaw.
  **Do not add antifreeze, salt or septic additives, do not light a fire over the system to thaw
  it, and never pump sewage onto the ground** (University of Minnesota Extension) (→ 13-05).

## The bucket toilet is the relief valve

Every failure above (overload, freezing, overdue pumping, backup) has the same first move: **take
the load off by moving some or all of the household to the bucket system** (→ 13-01). That is why
the bucket kit goes with you even to a place with plumbing. A septic system that is rested
whenever it is stressed can run for years. One forced to take a full household's waste through
every stress will fail, for good, with no truck coming.

**If you don't have it:**

- **Probe:** rebar, a straightened fence post, a sharpened dowel.
- **Mulch:** straw, hay, leaves, corn stalks: anything loose that will not pack down.
- **Records:** the county office, the pumper's office, neighbors who watched it go in.
- **What you cannot substitute: the field itself.** There is no improvised drain field. Protect
  the one you have.

## When it runs out

- **The tank runs out of room** on a clock of years, set by the last pump-out and slowed by saving
  water. Get the pump-out date onto the survey: it turns an unknown into a runway (→ 23-02).
- **Ration by diverting:** separating urine (→ 13-01) and diverting greywater (→ 13-03) can keep
  most of the household's liquid out of the tank.
- **Substitute:** the bucket toilet can take any share of the load for as long as needed; a
  properly placed latrine (→ 13-07) is the fallback if the system dies.
- **What never runs out:** the never-flush list and spreading the load. They cost nothing, and they
  are the whole difference between a system that outlasts the event and one that fails in the
  first spring.

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** {{#if has.septic}}fill in the survey now, tape up the never-flush
  list, and start spreading the laundry across the week.{{else}}not your home system. Keep this
  page for any place you might go that is on septic.{{/if}}
- **B (moving):** nothing to operate (catholes and packing out: → 13-07). But arrival matters:
  **do not let a tired group run a week of laundry and baths into an unknown system on the first
  day.** Do the survey first.
- **C (rural or at a destination):** **operate the existing system as the main one; the latrine
  (→ 13-07) is the fallback, not the first move.** Check the system type on arrival; if it is a
  lagoon or a pumped system, the rules change: note it in the survey.
