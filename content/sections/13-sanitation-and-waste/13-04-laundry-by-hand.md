# Laundry without power

**What this page is for.** Washing clothes by hand when there is no washer, dryer or hot water
on tap: what to wash, how, and how to get it dry.

> **Laundry is a water problem, a fuel problem and a drying problem.**{{#if is.cold_winters}}
> In your winters, drying is the hardest of the three (→ 11-03).{{/if}} Expect a load to take
> a day or more to dry in a cold house. **Drying, not washing, limits how much laundry a
> household can actually do.**
>
> **Safety:** anything soiled with human waste or vomit, or from someone who is sick, is handled
> separately, with gloves, washed hot where possible and dried completely (→ 13-11; CDC). Don't
> mix it with the general wash.

## Do this now

1. **Buy a mop-bucket wringer or a hand wringer.** Squeezing water out mechanically is the
   biggest time-saver in the whole job: it turns a two-day dry into an overnight one.
2. **Get two or three wash tubs kept only for laundry** and label them (wash, rinse, soak).
   Keep them apart from dish tubs and body-washing basins (→ 13-10).
3. **Set up drying:** a high line in the heated room and a line outdoors (→ 11-03).
4. **Buy a washboard or a plunger-style agitator.** A clean new toilet plunger with holes
   drilled in it, pumped up and down in a bucket, is an effective washing machine for a few
   dollars.

## The reality: you will wash far less

In a grid-down winter, a household of {{ household.size }} cannot wash the way it does now. The
honest plan:

- **Wash what must be washed:** underwear, socks, diapers (→ 13-08), anything soiled, anything
  from a sick person, kitchen cloths, and dressings you plan to reuse (→ 12-02).
- **Air and spot-clean the rest.** Outer layers, sweaters and wool can go a long time. **Wool
  resists smell and can be aired instead of washed** (→ 11-05).
- **In cold, dry beats clean** (→ 11-03). A dry base layer worn three days is better than a
  freshly washed damp one. Dryness wins.

## The process

{{figure fig-plunger-agitator}}

1. **Sort:** heavily soiled, lightly soiled, and contaminated (waste, vomit, a sick person).
   Contaminated items are washed separately, with gloves.
2. **Shake out and brush off** dry dirt first. Free, and it saves water and soap.
3. **Pre-soak** heavily soiled items in warm soapy water. **Soaking does most of the work:**
   time stands in for scrubbing and hot water. An overnight soak beats hard scrubbing.
4. **Agitate:** a plunger in a bucket, a washboard for stubborn spots, or feet in a tub (good for
   bedding and big items). **Movement, not temperature, removes most dirt.**
5. **Wring hard, then rinse** in clean water, once or twice. Soap left in fabric irritates
   skin, especially children's.
6. **Wring again, then towel-press:** roll the item in a dry towel and stand on it. This removes
   a surprising amount of water and cuts drying time a lot.
7. **Dry:** a high line in the warm room, or **freeze-drying** outdoors (below).
8. **The wash and rinse water becomes greywater** (→ 13-03): flushing, non-food plants,
   cleaning.

## Water and fuel

- **A hand-washed load uses far less water than a machine**, and you can reuse the water.
- **Clean first, dirty last.** Wash the cleanest items first and the dirtiest last in the same
  water. Rinse water from a clean load becomes wash water for a dirty one.
- **Hot water costs fuel** (→ 06-04, → 07-01). **Warm is usually enough** with soap and soaking.
  **Save truly hot water for contaminated items and diapers**, where it matters for killing
  germs.
- **Rain and snowmelt are ideal for laundry:** soft water lathers better and needs less soap
  (→ 03-05). Melting snow costs fuel, so weigh it.
- **Put laundry in the water budget** (→ 03-01, → 23-02). It is a real line.

## Drying in winter

- **Plan the week around drying.** Wash on a day you can dry.
- **Freeze-drying works.** Hang clothes outside in below-freezing air; the ice slowly turns
  straight to vapour. Bring them in stiff and finish indoors. **No fuel at all.**
- **Indoor drying adds damp to a closed-up house.** Vent on purpose and watch for condensation
  and mould (→ 11-03, → 08-03).
- **Never dry laundry on a stove or heater.** That is how house fires start (→ 12-03). **Near,
  never on.**
- **Stagger the wet work.** If everything is wet at once, nobody has dry clothes for days, in
  winter.

## Special cases

- **Diapers** (→ 13-08, → 20-06): the biggest and most important laundry in a house with a
  baby. Scrape solids into the toilet system (→ 13-01), soak, wash **hot**, rinse thoroughly
  (leftover soap causes rashes), and **dry completely**; sun and freeze-drying both help. This
  is steady, unavoidable work.{{#if has.infant}} It applies to you now.{{/if}}
- **Menstrual cloths** (→ 13-08): soak in **cold** water first (hot water sets blood), then
  wash.
- **A sick person's laundry** (→ 13-11): gloves, kept separate, hot water where possible, and
  dried completely (CDC).
- **Dressings you plan to reuse** (→ 12-02): **boiled**, not just washed, then dried clean and
  stored sealed.
- **Wool:** cool water, gentle, **no hard wringing** (it shrinks and mats into felt); dry flat
  (→ 11-05).
- **Cold hands.** Long sessions with wet hands in cold air chill you fast and crack skin
  (OSHA, Cold Stress). Wear rubber gloves, warm the water if you can, and take breaks.

## Soap

- **Laundry uses more soap than anything else in the house.** Stock it, and **learn to make
  it** (→ 13-09).
- **Less soap than you think** works with soaking and movement, and **less soap means fewer
  rinses**, which saves water. Too much soap is the common mistake.
- **Grated bar soap or home-made soap works** for laundry. Plain soap is better than detergent
  for greywater you will put on soil (→ 13-03).

**If you don't have it:**

- **Tubs:** any bucket, tote, bin, cooler or bathtub.
- **Agitator:** a plunger, a stick, a paddle, a clean boot, your feet.
- **Washboard:** a sheet of corrugated metal, a ridged panel, a smooth rock.
- **Wringer:** two people twisting the item in opposite directions; pressing between boards;
  rolling in a towel and standing on it.
- **Line:** cord, wire, rope, branches. **Pegs:** clothes pegs, clips, split twigs, or just
  drape it.
- **Soap:** any soap; home-made (→ 13-09); water soaked through wood ash (lye water) is a
  traditional laundry cleaner. **It is caustic (burns skin): handle carefully and rinse
  thoroughly.**
- **Softener:** a splash of vinegar in the rinse (it also cuts soap residue).
- **Stains:** sunlight bleaches stains for free; cold water at once for blood; sun on cloth
  diapers removes stains and helps kill germs.

## When it runs out

- **Soap is the consumable, and you can make it** (→ 13-09). **Water and fuel are the ongoing
  costs.** Put all three in → 23-02.
- **Runway:** think in loads per week your **drying space** can handle, not in soap. That is the
  real limit.
- **Ration:** wash less, air more, spot-clean, and choose: **underwear, socks, diapers, kitchen
  cloths and anything contaminated get washed; outer layers get aired.** Wool takes this much
  better than cotton (→ 11-05).
- **Substitute:** sunlight and air for washing (real, if partial); freeze-drying for the dryer;
  ash lye for soap; soaking and movement for hot water and scrubbing.
- **Why it matters:** washing the things that matter properly (**diapers, a sick person's
  things, kitchen cloths, reused dressings**) is infection control (→ 13-11, → 12-04).
  Skipping *those* is how a shortcut becomes an illness. Skipping a sweater wash costs nothing.

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** water is available; drying is the limit. Set up tubs, wringer and
  lines early, and start the "wash what matters" habit before everyone is tired.
- **B (moving):** no laundry on the move. **Rinse socks and underwear at water stops if you can
  dry them**; otherwise carry spares. **Wet clothing in winter is the danger** (→ 11-03), so
  carry dry spares rather than washing.
- **C (rural or at a destination):** room for a real setup: a summer washing area outdoors, a
  winter setup near the stove, long lines, space to freeze-dry. **A hand-crank wringer washer is
  a great find** (→ 16-05); watch for one.
