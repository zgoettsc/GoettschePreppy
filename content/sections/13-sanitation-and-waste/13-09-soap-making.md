# Making soap from ash and fat

**What this page is for.** Why soap matters so much, and how soap is made from wood ash (or
bought lye) and animal fat, so that a household can keep making it once the shops are gone.

> **CAUTION. Lye causes severe chemical burns and can cause permanent blindness.** Sodium
> hydroxide and potassium hydroxide ("lye"), whether bought or made from wood ash, are strong
> caustics: they burn skin and eyes. **A splash in the eye is a medical emergency.** Mixing lye
> with water makes a lot of heat and can boil and spatter.
>
> **Always: eye protection (goggles, not glasses), gloves, long sleeves, outdoors or with real
> ventilation, and always add LYE TO WATER, never water to lye.** Keep plenty of clean water
> within reach. **Children and pets nowhere near it** (→ 20-02). **Floor:** children do not have
> access to dangerous chemicals; store lye like fuel and chlorine, locked and labeled (→ 30-01).
>
> **This page gives no recipe and no ratios**, because lye made from ash has an **unknown and
> changing strength**, which is exactly what makes traditional soap-making unpredictable and
> sometimes caustic. **Learn from a real soap-making reference, practice with measured
> bought lye and a tested recipe first**, and only then try the ash version.

## Do this now

1. **Stock soap instead.** Bar soap keeps almost for ever, costs very little, and is the most
   valuable medical supply in the house (→ 13-02). **Buy far more than seems reasonable:** that
   is the real answer for the first year or two.
2. **Buy bought lye (sodium hydroxide) and a good paper soap-making book, and make one batch
   now, safely, with a tested recipe.** Learning with lye of known strength builds the skill
   without the risk.
3. **Save fat:** every trimming, every jar of drippings, every rendered batch (→ 04-12,
   → 10-04). Fat is the other half of soap.
4. **Save hardwood ash** from the stove, dry and clean, in a covered metal container.

## Why this matters more than it looks

**Soap is the most valuable medical supply a household with no clinic can have** (→ 13-02;
CDC). Handwashing with soap breaks the chain that causes the most likely serious illness
(→ 12-05). Soap also does laundry (→ 13-04), dishes (→ 13-10) and wound cleaning (→ 12-02).

**Soap is also one of the few manufactured goods a household can keep making**, from two things
it will have anyway: **wood ash from the stove** (→ 06-04) and **fat** from animals and cooking
(→ 05-09, → 05-16, → 04-12).

## The chemistry in one paragraph

Soap is what you get when a **strong alkali (lye)** reacts with **fat** ("saponification").
Historically the alkali came from **water trickled through hardwood ash**, which gives
**potassium hydroxide** ("potash lye") and makes a **soft, jelly-like soap**: good for laundry
and washing, not a hard bar. **Modern hard bar soap uses sodium hydroxide.** The difference that
matters: **bought lye has a known strength on its label, and ash lye does not.**

## Why the unknown strength is the whole problem

Soap-making is a ratio. Too little lye and you get greasy fat that spoils. Too much and you get
**caustic soap that burns skin**: exactly what you do not want on a child's hands or in a wound.

Ash lye strength changes with the kind of wood, how completely it burned, how the ash was
stored and how it was soaked. The traditional tests (whether an egg or potato floats in it,
whether it dissolves a feather) are **rough signs, not measurements**, which is why old-time
soap was often harsh.

**The safe path:**

1. **Learn with bought lye and a tested recipe.** Get the process, the safety habits and the
   feel for it.
2. **Then, if you need to, use ash lye**, testing cautiously, adding **extra fat** (so the soap
   stays mild even if the lye was stronger than you thought), and **testing the finished soap
   carefully before it touches anyone's skin, especially a child's.**
3. **When in doubt, add more fat.** Greasy soap is unpleasant. Caustic soap injures.

## Making lye from wood ash (an outline, not a procedure)

{{figure fig-ash-hopper}}

- **Hardwood ash only.** Softwood and pine ash is much weaker. Oak, ash, maple and similar
  hardwoods are what you want.
- **Clean white ash from a hot fire**, sieved of charcoal lumps, kept **dry** in a covered metal
  container. Ash that has got wet has already lost its strength.
- **Leaching:** water trickled slowly through a container of ash and collected underneath. The
  traditional "ash hopper" is a barrel with a false bottom and a spout. **Rainwater or other
  soft water works better than hard water.**
- **The result is a caustic liquid.** Treat it exactly like bought lye: goggles, gloves,
  ventilation, out of reach of children.
- **Boiling it down to make it stronger raises the danger a lot**: boiling caustic spatters.
  **This is where people get hurt.**

## Rendering the fat

Same method as → 10-04: **low and slow, in a heavy pot or a double boiler, watched, strained
through cloth.** Clean, well-strained fat makes better soap. **Melting fat is a real fire
hazard, and water on a fat fire makes it explode into flame** (→ 10-04).

**Fat sources:** beef or deer tallow (best for hard soap), lard, chicken and rabbit fat
(→ 05-16), strained used cooking oil, and rancid fat (**still fine for soap**: a good use for
fat that is no longer good food, → 04-12).

## Safety rules, all of them

1. **Goggles.** Not glasses. Eye splashes cause permanent damage.
2. **Chemical-resistant gloves and long sleeves.**
3. **Outdoors or with real ventilation.** Lye and water give off fumes.
4. **Always add lye to water, never water to lye.** The reverse can boil violently.
5. **The mixture gets very hot.** Use heat-proof containers.
6. **Never aluminum.** Lye reacts with aluminum and gives off hydrogen, a flammable gas. Use
   stainless steel, heavy plastic, or glass (lye slowly etches and weakens glass).
7. **Equipment kept only for soap**, never anything that touches food. {{#if has.condition.CL-04}}Never
   the allergy-safe cookware (→ 02-04).{{/if}} Label everything.
8. **Plenty of clean water within reach.** For a splash on skin, **flush with lots of water at
   once, and for a long time.** An eye splash is a medical emergency: flush and keep flushing
   (→ 12-03). If anyone swallows lye, call Poison Control (→ 12-17).
9. **Children and pets nowhere near it:** the process and the curing soap.
10. **Fresh soap is caustic and must cure for weeks before use.** Do not rush it, and test it
    before it touches skin.
11. **Store lye and ash lye sealed, labeled and locked** (→ 06-06).

## What you get, realistically

- **Ash-lye soap:** soft, jelly-like, brown, and excellent for **laundry** (→ 13-04) and
  **general cleaning**, which use the most soap anyway.
- **Bought-lye tallow soap:** a real hard bar, good for hands and body.
- **Neither will look like shop soap**, and that is fine.
- **Soap is a very good barter good** (→ 18-02): everyone needs it, few can make it, and it
  keeps.

## Gear and supplies

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bar soap, a very large stock** | The real answer for the first year or two | Cheap now; keeps |
| **Bought lye (sodium hydroxide) and a tested recipe** | Learn safely with a known strength | Sealed, labeled, locked |
| **Goggles, chemical gloves, apron** | Protection that is not optional | Goggles, not glasses |
| Stainless steel or heavy plastic pots and utensils kept for soap | **Never aluminum, never food equipment** | Label them permanently |
| Digital kitchen scale | Soap is a ratio; cups and spoons are not accurate enough | Essential for a real recipe |
| Thermometer | Fat and lye temperatures matter | Kept for soap |
| Covered metal ash container | Dry ash keeps its strength | Also safe ash storage (→ 06-02) |
| Jars for saving fat | Every trimming and drippings | → 04-12 |
| A paper soap-making book | Ratios, safety, troubleshooting | **Learn before you need it** |
| Plenty of clean water | Splash response | Within arm's reach |
| Molds kept for soap | Paper-lined boxes, silicone, old pans | Never food containers |

**If you don't have it:**

- **Ash:** free from a wood stove (→ 06-04). **Hardwood only, kept dry.**
- **Fat:** butchering trimmings (→ 05-09, → 05-16), saved drippings, rancid fat, tallow rendered
  by → 10-04.
- **Ash hopper:** a barrel or bucket with a false bottom of gravel and cloth, a spout at the
  bottom, ash on top.
- **Containers:** stainless steel, heavy plastic buckets, glazed crockery. **Never aluminum.**
- **Molds:** paper-lined cardboard boxes, wooden frames, a loaf pan you will never cook in
  again.
- **Cleaning with no soap at all:** ash and sand scrub hands, and ash is mildly alkaline. A real
  traditional method, and better than nothing (→ 13-02).

## When it runs out

- **Making soap is the answer to soap running out.** But **the skill is the resource, not the
  supplies**, and it has to be learned before it is needed.
- **The inputs renew:** ash from a stove you are running anyway, fat from animals and cooking.
- **What limits you:** bought lye (finite: use it to learn), fat (which competes with food and
  light, → 04-12, → 10-04), and time.
- **Fat is food, light and soap at once.** In a real shortage, **rancid and inedible fat goes to
  soap.** That is why you save every scrap.
- **Ration:** home-made soap for laundry and cleaning (the big users); keep shop-bought bar soap
  for hands, bodies and wound care, where mildness and reliability matter most.
- **Trade:** soap is one of the best barter goods there is (→ 18-02). **A household that can make
  soap holds real value in a community** (→ 18-03).

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** stock soap deeply and **learn the process now**, with bought lye,
  outdoors, once. Start saving ash and fat. Do not make ash lye under pressure as your first
  attempt.
- **B (moving):** carry soap; do not make it. A small bar per person, and hand sanitizer
  (→ 13-02).
- **C (rural or at a destination):** **soap-making becomes a real household job:** the stove makes
  ash every day, animals give tallow (→ 05-16), and soap is made in batches a few times a year.
  Set up a permanent, safe, outdoor work area, well away from where children play.
