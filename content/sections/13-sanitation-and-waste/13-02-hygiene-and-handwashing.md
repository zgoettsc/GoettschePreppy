# Hygiene and handwashing (disease prevention)

**What this page is for.** Handwashing with soap and water is the most valuable medical step a
household with no clinic can take. This page covers when and how to wash, a low-water
handwashing station you can build, washing the body when water is short, and children.

> **SAFETY.** Handwashing with soap breaks the chain that carries germs from human waste to
> mouths (the "faecal-oral" route), which causes diarrhoeal illness: the most likely serious
> health problem a household will face without services (→ 12-05). It costs a little water and
> a bar of soap.
>
> **Wash with soap and water for at least 20 seconds** (CDC, About Handwashing), at the key
> moments below. Everything else in the medical and sanitation sections depends on this.

## Do this now

1. **Buy soap in quantity: more than you think, then more.** Bar soap keeps almost for ever in
   a cool, dry place. It is the cheapest medical supply you can own.
2. **Build a handwashing station** at the toilet (→ 13-01) and at the kitchen: **a jug with a
   spigot, soap and a towel.** It has to be effortless or it will not happen.
3. **Learn the tippy-tap** (below). It uses very little water per wash.
4. **Stock alcohol-based hand sanitiser with at least 60 percent alcohol** (CDC) for when
   water is short or you are on the move. Know its limits: it works less well on visibly
   dirty or greasy hands, and it does not work on everything (not norovirus, the "stomach
   bug", → 13-11).
5. **Learn to make soap** (→ 13-09) from wood ash and fat.

## The key moments

Washing at the *right times* beats washing constantly. The CDC says wash hands:

- **After using the toilet** or handling any waste (→ 13-01)
- **After changing a diaper**, or cleaning up a child who used the toilet (→ 20-06)
- **Before, during and after preparing food**
- **Before eating**
- **Before and after caring for anyone who is sick** (→ 13-11)
- **Before and after treating a wound or changing a dressing** (→ 12-02)
- **After touching animals, animal feed or animal waste** (→ 20-09, → 05-04)
- **After handling rubbish** (→ 13-03)
- **After blowing your nose, coughing or sneezing**
- **After touching anything from outside the household** during an outbreak

{{#if has.condition.CL-04}}**Food allergy in your household:** wash hands, and use a clean
towel, before preparing the allergic person's food, every time. Allergen proteins travel on
hands; handwashing is part of keeping them out (→ 02-04).{{else}}**Food allergies:** if anyone
you feed has one, wash hands with a clean towel before preparing their food. Allergen proteins
travel on hands (→ 02-04).{{/if}}

## How to wash properly (CDC)

1. **Wet hands** with clean running water, warm or cold.
2. **Lather with soap:** backs of hands, between fingers, under nails.
3. **Scrub for at least 20 seconds** (about two rounds of "Happy Birthday").
4. **Rinse** under clean running water.
5. **Dry** with a clean towel, or air dry.

**"Running" does not mean a tap.** Water poured from a jug by another person, a bottle with a
hole, or a tippy-tap all count. **What matters is that the rinse water runs off and away**,
rather than you rinsing in a basin of dirty water.

## The tippy-tap: a low-water station worth building

A jug hung from a frame with a small hole near the cap, tipped by a string tied to a foot
pedal:

- **Uses very little water per wash**, far less than a tap.
- **Hands-free:** you never touch the jug, so you do not dirty it.
- **Built from a jug, cord, two sticks and a nail.**
- **Soap hangs on a string beside it.**

**Build one at the toilet and one at the kitchen.** A fifteen-minute job.

## Water for handwashing

Handwashing competes with drinking water (→ 03-01). Be deliberate:

- **A tippy-tap wash uses a small fraction of a tap wash.**
- **The CDC's emergency guidance is boiled or disinfected water for handwashing**, from a jug
  station of clean water (CDC, Guidelines for Personal Hygiene During an Emergency).
- **Some households use untreated rain, snowmelt or pool water for ordinary washes** to save
  treated water, relying on soap and water that runs off. If you do, three limits hold
  whatever you decide:
  - **never greywater** (used wash or laundry water, → 13-03): it carries the very germs
    handwashing exists to remove;
  - **never on open cuts or cracked skin**: those hands get treated water, and a wound is never
    washed in untreated water (→ 12-02). Saltwater-pool water on a cut is an infection route;
  - **the final rinse before preparing food, and before handling an infant, is treated water.**

  Your household's handwashing-water rule: ______________________________
- **Hand sanitiser (at least 60 percent alcohol)** where water is truly scarce. The CDC says it
  works less well on visibly dirty hands and does not handle everything (not norovirus), so it
  is a supplement, not a replacement.
- **Do not cut handwashing below the key moments.** If water is that short, the answer is the
  water plan (→ 03-01). The illness will cost far more water than the washing would have.

## Washing the body when washing is hard

Over months in a cold house with rationed water, full baths become rare. That is survivable.
The priorities:

- **The areas that matter: hands, face, feet, armpits, groin.** A "basin wash" of these with a
  cloth and a little warm water does most of the work and uses very little water.
- **Feet matter more than you would think.** Wash and dry them daily to prevent fungal
  infection and trench foot (feet damaged by days of cold and wet) (→ 11-04).
- **Teeth twice a day** (→ 12-06). This routine quietly lapses, and it costs teeth.
- **Hair** can go a long time; brushing spreads the natural oils. Lice and scabies (tiny mites
  that burrow in skin) are real in crowded conditions: **stock the treatments** (→ 12-07).
- **In cold, dry clothes matter more than clean ones** (→ 11-03), but change and wash
  underwear and socks regularly (→ 13-04).
- **Keep nails short.** Long nails hold dirt and bacteria, and tear.
- **Menstrual hygiene** has its own water and cleanliness needs (→ 13-08).
- **A weekly warm wash in the heated room is a real morale event**, not just hygiene
  (→ 21-02). Do not underrate it.

## Children

- **Children from about age 3 or 4 can learn to wash properly.** Make it a routine with a song
  to time it, and put the station at their height. **They will not do it unless it is easy and
  expected** (→ 20-04).
- **Babies and toddlers put everything in their mouths.** That is the highest-risk habit in any
  household for spreading stomach illness. **Wash their hands often, especially before
  eating**, and keep the waste system completely out of their reach (→ 13-01, → 20-02).
- **Every diaper change is a hygiene event:** hands washed after each change, one changing area
  kept for the job, waste contained (→ 20-06, → 13-08).
- **Model it.** Children do what adults do, not what adults say.

## Gear and supplies

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bar soap, a large stock** | The cheapest, most valuable medical supply | Keeps for years cool and dry |
| Liquid soap and refills | Convenient at stations | Bar soap stores better |
| **Hand sanitiser, at least 60 percent alcohol** | Backup when water is scarce or on the move | Not for norovirus; not on dirty hands |
| Jugs with spigots, two or three | Stations at toilet and kitchen | Or build tippy-taps |
| Basins or wash tubs, two or three | Basin washing; kept apart from dish tubs | Label them |
| Washcloths and towels, many | Basin washing; rotate and wash | → 13-04 |
| Nail brush, nail clippers | Dirt under nails carries germs | Cheap |
| Toothbrushes, toothpaste, floss, years' worth | → 12-06 | Children's sizes |
| Lice and scabies treatment | Crowded conditions | Check expiry dates |
| Lotion or petroleum jelly | Hands cracked by cold and washing get infected | Matters in winter; cracked hands get treated water |
| Wood ash and fat for soap making | The supply you can renew | → 13-09 |

**If you don't have it:**

- **Soap:** any soap works for handwashing: dish soap, shampoo, laundry soap. **Then make your
  own** (→ 13-09).
- **A station:** a jug with a nail hole, a bottle with a pinhole in the cap, a tippy-tap from
  sticks and cord, a bucket with a hardware-store spigot.
- **Sanitiser:** it must be **at least 60 percent alcohol** (CDC). 70 percent isopropyl rubbing
  alcohol works. 40 to 50 percent spirits (vodka, whisky) and 50 percent rubbing alcohol do
  **not**. **Do not improvise strengths**: weak alcohol does not work.
- **Towels:** any clean cloth, dried between uses. **Damp towels grow bacteria.**
- **Ash and sand** scrub hands when there is no soap. Better than nothing, and a real
  traditional method: the scrubbing plus the ash's alkali does real work.

## When it runs out

- **Soap is the one to plan for, and you can make it** (→ 13-09). Ash from the stove plus
  rendered fat is a renewable supply. **Practise before you need it.**
- **Runway:** a bar of soap lasts a household a surprisingly long time for handwashing. Count
  bars and put them in the consumables table (→ 23-02). Soap competes with nothing; there is no
  reason to under-stock it.
- **Ration:** wash at the key moments rather than constantly; use tippy-taps; never go below
  the key moments.
- **Substitute:** ash and sand scrubbing; hand sanitiser; and **more careful behaviour**: not
  touching your face, keeping the waste system separate, keeping small children's hands out of
  their mouths as far as possible.
- **It compounds both ways.** Good hygiene prevents illness, which saves water, energy,
  supplies and the household's ability to function. A hygiene failure becomes a water crisis,
  a medical crisis and a labour crisis at the same time.

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** water is still around, so make hygiene **easy and strict**. Build
  the stations, set the habits, and get children's routines in place now, so they survive later
  fatigue.
- **B (moving):** hygiene slips exactly when illness risk rises. **Hand sanitiser and a small
  bar of soap per person**; wash properly at every real water source; be strict before eating
  and after any waste handling.
- **C (rural or at a destination):** more animals, more dirt, more hard work, further from care.
  **Handwashing stations at the door and the toilet become permanent**, and soap making becomes
  a routine job (→ 13-09).
