# Building a composting or bucket toilet

**What this page is for.** The difference between a bucket toilet (collecting waste) and
composting human waste (treating it), and how to do the second one safely if you have the land
for it.

> **SAFETY. This page is deliberately cautious.**
>
> **Human waste carries germs that survive a long time:** bacteria, viruses, protozoa (single-
> celled parasites) and worm eggs. **Composting them to a truly safe standard needs sustained
> high temperatures throughout the pile and a long curing time.** A small backyard pile often
> does not get there, especially in a cold climate, where it stops working all winter.
>
> **This binder's advice:** build the bucket toilet (→ 13-01): it is safe, simple, and it
> works. **Treat composting as a way to dispose of waste and shrink its volume, not as a way to
> make compost for the vegetable garden. Do not put human-waste compost on food crops.** If you
> use it at all, use it on flowers, trees or areas where nothing is eaten, well away from
> anywhere water flows. The reason: you cannot tell by looking whether compost is free of
> germs, and an infection from food crops could reach the whole household.

## Do this now

1. **Build the bucket toilet** (→ 13-01). That is the part that matters, and it takes an hour.
2. **Stockpile cover material:** sawdust from firewood cutting (→ 06-04), wood shavings,
   shredded leaves, peat moss. **You need a lot, and indoors in winter** (→ 13-05).
3. **Choose the composting spot now, if you will have one:** well away from water, the edible
   garden, the neighbors, and anywhere children go (→ 13-07).
4. **Decide honestly whether you have the land, the time and the discipline.** On a small town
   lot the answer may be no, and bagging and storing (→ 13-01) is a legitimate answer.

> **Before the collapse:** most states and counties regulate composting toilets. Check your
> local rules. They never decide whether you keep waste away from water to stay alive.

## Two things people mix up

**A bucket toilet** is a *collection* system. Simple, safe, cheap, works indoors in winter.
**Covering each deposit with sawdust controls smell almost completely.**

**Composting human waste** is a *treatment* system that comes after collection. It is what
happens to the contents, and it is where the germ risk lives. **These are separate decisions.**
You can run a bucket toilet and bag the contents, or run a bucket toilet and compost the
contents; the collection is the same either way.

## What "safe" composting actually needs

To kill germs, a compost pile needs:

- **Sustained high ("thermophilic") temperatures throughout the pile**, not just in the middle;
- **turning**, so every part passes through the hot zone;
- **the right mix of carbon (dry brown material) and nitrogen (the waste), and moisture**, to
  keep the heat going;
- **a long curing time afterwards**: typically a year or more for human waste, and some worm
  eggs survive a long time;
- **enough volume**: small piles do not hold heat.

**In practice:**{{#if is.cold_winters}} where you live, a pile freezes solid for months each
year and does nothing.{{/if}} Summer piles can get genuinely hot, but getting every part of a
small backyard bin hot enough is hard to check and easy to get wrong. **You cannot tell by
looking whether compost is free of germs.** Use **time and distance** as your safety margin,
not confidence in the process.

## If you build a composting system anyway

{{figure fig-two-bin-humanure}}

**The two-bin method:**

1. **Bin one receives.** Bucket contents are tipped in and **covered at once** with a thick layer
   of carbon material (straw, leaves, sawdust). Covering stops smell, flies and exposure.
2. **When it is full, it rests:** closed and undisturbed for a long time (**a year at the very
   least; longer is better**). Meanwhile bin two receives.
3. **Swap.**

{{photo compost-bins}}

**Building it:**

- **Closed on all sides**, with a lid or heavy cover. **Rodent-proof and child-proof** (→ 13-03,
  → 20-02): pallets lined with hardware cloth (stiff wire mesh), a wire bin, or a built box.
- **Placed by → 13-07:** downhill from and far from any water, far from the edible garden and
  the kitchen, away from the property line.
- **On soil, not concrete**, so liquid soaks into the ground rather than running off, but
  **only** if you are far from any water source and the water table is not shallow.
- **Never where snowmelt or heavy rain will run through it and away.**

**Rules for running it: all of them, every time.**

- **Cover every addition thoroughly.** This is the whole of the smell and fly control.
- **Nothing but human waste, toilet paper and carbon cover material.** No plastic, no wipes, no
  hygiene products, no diapers with plastic, no chemicals.
- **Gloves and boots for handling; hands washed with soap straight after** (CDC, Protecting
  Workers Handling Human Waste or Sewage).
- **Children never near it** (→ 20-02).
- **Tools kept only for this**, touching nothing else. Label them.
- **Do not turn a pile that has just had fresh material added:** it brings fresh waste to the
  surface.

## Urine, again

**Separating urine changes everything for a compost system:**

- Urine is most of the volume; separating it cuts what you handle sharply.
- It keeps the pile from going soggy and airless, which stops the heat and causes smell.
- **Urine from a healthy person is fairly low-risk** and can be diluted heavily and used on
  plants you do not eat and on trees. It is salty and rich in nitrogen, so **dilute it well,
  pour it on soil not leaves, and move where you pour it.** A dilution of about 10 parts water
  to 1 part urine is commonly quoted; it is a convention, not a published standard. The
  direction of the rule is solid: undiluted urine poured again and again in one place burns
  plants and builds salt in soil. **Never on the edible parts of food crops.**

## Winter

- **In a cold winter, a compost pile does nothing.** It freezes and waits.
- **You can keep adding to it**; it just stacks up until spring.
- **The pile may be where the winter's bagged waste goes** (→ 13-05), so **it needs room for a
  whole winter plus the year of curing.** That is usually bigger than people expect.
- **Plan the spring thaw** (→ 13-05): that is the week this becomes urgent.

## The honest comparison

| Option | Good | Bad |
|---|---|---|
| **Bucket, bag, store or dispose** | Simple, safe, works indoors and in winter, no land needed | Bags run out; the pile of bags needs somewhere to go |
| **Bucket and compost** | No bags, smaller volume, nothing to buy | Needs land, discipline and time; **germ risk if done badly**; regulated; freezes in cold winters |
| **Properly placed pit latrine** (→ 13-07) | Simple and proven; nothing to buy | Needs land and safe distances; cannot dig frozen ground; groundwater risk |
| **Pour-flush while the sewer works** (→ 13-01) | **Best of all** | Uses water; needs a working sewer |

{{#if is.urban}}**For your town or suburban home: pour-flush if possible, otherwise bucket and
bag.** Composting belongs where land, distance and time exist.{{else}}**On a rural property**
with real distances to water, a two-bin system is workable; still pour-flush while you can.{{/if}}

## Gear and supplies

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Bucket toilet (→ 13-01) | The part that matters | Two buckets, seat, cover material |
| **Cover material in bulk** | Smell, flies, and the pile's carbon | Sawdust from firewood is free (→ 06-04) |
| **Urine container** | Cuts the problem by half or more | The best single improvement |
| Compost bin materials (pallets, hardware cloth, lid) | Closed, rodent-proof, child-proof | If you build one |
| **A fork or shovel kept only for this, labeled** | Never touches anything else | Not negotiable |
| Rubber gloves and rubber boots | CDC-recommended protection | Kept for this job |
| Long-stem compost thermometer | Shows what the pile is doing | Cheap; still not proof of safety |
| Straw, leaves, other carbon in bulk | The pile needs a lot | Free from the yard |
| Handwashing station next to it | Every single time | → 13-02 |

**If you don't have it:**

- **Bin:** pallets wired together and lined, a wire ring, a barrel with the bottom cut out, a
  dug pit (→ 13-07).
- **Cover and carbon:** sawdust, wood shavings, shredded leaves, dry grass, straw, shredded
  paper and cardboard, peat, dry soil, wood ash.
- **Tools:** any fork or shovel, **permanently marked** so it never goes into the garden.
- **What you cannot substitute:** land, distance from water, and time. **If you do not have
  those, do not compost: bag it** (→ 13-01).

## When it runs out

- **The consumable is cover material**, and it is almost free if you cut firewood (→ 06-04) or
  rake leaves. **Keep an indoor winter supply** (→ 13-05).
- **What runs out is capacity.** The bins must hold everything your {{ household.size }} people
  produce in a year, **plus** a full curing period in which that material is not touched. Be
  honest about whether your lot can hold that.
- **Ration:** separate urine (the biggest cut), and use the sewer whenever it works.
- **Substitute:** bag and store; a pit latrine where the site allows (→ 13-07); the sewer.
- **The long-term answer is land**, where a properly placed latrine or compost system with real
  distances and a real curing area is straightforward, **built in the first weeks, before it
  is urgent** (→ 13-07).
- **What never runs out is the safety margin of time and distance.** When in doubt, wait longer
  and keep it further from everything.

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** bucket toilet, urine separated, bagged and stored. **A compost
  system on a small lot is hard to place safely** and may not be allowed. Don't force it.
- **B (moving):** neither. Bury properly and away from water (→ 13-07), or pack it out.
- **C (rural or at a destination):** **this is where it belongs.** Real distances from water,
  real land, a two-bin system with a full year of curing, downhill and far from the well, the
  garden and the house. **Build it in the first month**, and still keep human-waste compost off
  food crops.
