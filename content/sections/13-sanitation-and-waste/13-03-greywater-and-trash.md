# Greywater and trash management

**What this page is for.** What to do with used wash water and with trash when the drains
and the trash collection stop: how to reuse water safely, shrink the trash stream, deal with
medical sharps and cat litter, and keep rats and mice out.

> **CAUTION. Two hazards.**
> 1. **Greywater is not clean water.** Wash and rinse water holds soap, grease, food bits, skin
>    bacteria and, depending on what was washed, real germs. **It is never for drinking, never
>    for the edible parts of plants, and it turns foul within a day or two if stored.**
> 2. **Trash draws rodents, and rodents spread disease and wreck food stores** (→ 04-11).
>    When collection stops, **rodent control becomes a real health issue within weeks.**

## Do this now

1. **Buy several lidded, rodent-proof bins.** Metal cans with tight lids beat plastic, which
   rodents chew through.
2. **Start composting now** (→ 05-11). It takes the wet, smelly, rodent-drawing part out of
   your trash for good and makes soil for the garden.
3. **Catch greywater at the sink and wash basin:** a basin instead of a drain, so wash water
   becomes a resource.
4. **Work out where a month of trash goes** if collection stops. It piles up faster than
   people expect.

## Greywater: a resource with rules

**Greywater** is wash water: dishes, laundry, bathing, handwashing. **Blackwater** is toilet
waste (→ 13-01) and anything from diapers or cleaning up after a sick person. **They are
handled completely differently and never mixed.**

**Good uses of greywater** when water is rationed (→ 03-01):

- **Flushing:** pour it into the toilet bowl, when the sewer works. **The best single use.**
- **Plants you do not eat, trees and lawn.**
- **A fire-fighting reserve**, cleaning, washing dirty tools.
- **NOT for:** drinking, cooking, washing food, washing hands, or **the edible parts of food
  plants.**

**Rules that keep it safe:**

- **Use it the same day.** Greywater goes foul fast, within about a day in warm weather.
  **Don't store it.**
- **On the soil, not the leaves.** Pour it at the base of plants, never over anything eaten raw.
- **Watch what is in the soap.** For anything going on soil, use plain, biodegradable soap;
  avoid bleach, boron (borax) and heavy salts, which build up and damage soil.
- **Strain out the solids first:** a screen over the basin. Food bits make greywater rot and
  draw pests.
- **Never let it pool.** Standing water breeds mosquitoes (→ 03-08) and smells.
- **Keep it away from wells, water storage, and anywhere it can run into a neighbor's yard or a
  storm drain.**
- **In winter it freezes.** Choose a pouring spot that will not ice your path (→ 11-06).

**Cascade your water.** This is the whole idea, and it stretches supply a long way:
**clean water → rinsing → washing → dishwater → flushing or non-food plants.** Each drop is used
two or three times. Set the basins up so this happens without thinking.

## Trash when there is no collection

**Shrink the stream first.** Every kind of waste you remove is one you do not have to store.

| Waste | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Food scraps** | **Compost** (→ 05-11), or feed to chickens (→ 05-04). Removes the smelly, rodent-drawing part |
| **Paper and cardboard** | **Burn** in the stove (untreated only, → 06-04), use as fire starter, compost, or use as mulch |
| **Wood scraps** | Firewood or kindling (untreated only) |
| **Metal cans** | Rinse (the rinse water is greywater), flatten and **keep**: metal is useful (→ 16-08) |
| **Glass jars** | **Wash and keep.** Jars are storage, lamps and containers (→ 04-08, → 10-04) |
| **Plastic containers** | Keep the useful ones: containers, scoops, funnels |
| **Fabric and clothing** | Rag bag, repair material, wicks, patches (→ 11-07, → 10-04) |
| **Batteries** | Do not burn; store safely (→ 09-03) |
| **Medical sharps, used pump pods** | **See below.** Rigid sealed containers marked SHARPS, never loose in the trash; **pump pods contain a battery: never burn** |
| **Hazardous** (chemicals, fuel, paint) | Sealed, labeled, away from living space and water (→ 06-06) |
| **Diapers** | **Blackwater: treat as human waste** (→ 13-01, → 20-06) |
| **Used cat litter** | **Never compost.** Scoop daily, bag, bury or store like human waste (below) |
| **What is actually left** | Contained, lidded, rodent-proof, away from the house |

**Do not burn:** plastics, treated or painted wood, rubber, foam, batteries, or anything with a
coating. The smoke is toxic (→ 06-04).

## Medical sharps

Anyone who injects medicine (insulin, other injections), uses lancets, wears an insulin pump or
a glucose sensor, or treats an animal with injections produces a **sharps waste stream that
never stops.** Glucose sensor applicators can hide a **spring-loaded needle** inside the plastic.
{{#if has.condition.CL-02}}Your household uses a pump or sensor: count a year's pods and
applicators from your own supply list (→ 02-02) and size the containers to that number.{{/if}}
{{#if has.condition.CL-01}}Count a year's pen needles, syringes and lancets from your own supply
list and size the containers to that number.{{/if}}

**The rules:**

- **Rigid, sealed container (FDA).** Every sharp goes into a rigid, puncture-resistant, lidded
  container **straight after use**: a proper sharps container, or the standard substitute, a
  **heavy-duty plastic laundry-detergent bottle**. **Mark it SHARPS in marker, and tape the cap
  shut when it is about three-quarters full.** Overfilled containers are how people get stuck
  (US FDA, Best Way to Get Rid of Used Needles and Other Sharps). Keep it out of reach of
  children and pets (→ 20-02).
- **Insulin pump pods contain a battery. Never burn a pod, never crush one, never put one in a
  burn barrel or stove.** A damaged battery can vent or catch fire (→ 09-03). Store used pods
  sealed in their own rigid, labeled container and treat them as battery waste.
- **Long term, when collection never comes back:** sealed containers are small; **store them
  indefinitely, or bury them deep, away from water** by the distances on → 13-07 (100 feet or
  more from any well or surface water, above the water table). **Never the burn barrel, never
  loose in the ground, never the compost.**
- A needle stick from a used sharp is a wound plus an infection risk with no urgent care
  (→ 12-04). The container habit is cheap insurance.

## Used cat litter

{{#if has.cats}}This applies to your household.{{/if}}

- **Used litter is never compost and never goes on a garden.** Cat feces can carry
  *Toxoplasma gondii*, a parasite. **The CDC says it becomes infectious 1 to 5 days after it is
  passed, which is why the box is scooped daily**: daily scooping removes it before it is
  infectious (CDC, Preventing Toxoplasmosis).
- **Scoop daily into a bag; bury or store it exactly like human waste** (→ 13-01): sealed, away
  from living space, water and the garden.
- **Wear gloves** when handling litter or soil that may hold cat feces, and wash hands with
  soap afterwards (CDC).
- **Pregnancy:** toxoplasmosis during pregnancy can harm the unborn baby. **A pregnant person
  should not change the litter box if anyone else can. If she must: gloves, daily scooping,
  thorough handwashing** (CDC; → 12-12).{{#if has.pregnant}} This applies in your household
  now.{{/if}}
- Litter runs out. Dirt, sand, wood ash or shredded paper replace it (put it in → 23-02). The
  disposal rule never changes.

## Rodents: the part that becomes serious

When collection stops and food is stored, **rats and mice follow.** They spread disease, destroy
food (→ 04-11), chew wiring (→ 09-08), and get into insulation and vehicles.

- **Seal food in hard containers**, not bags (→ 04-03).
- **Lidded metal trash bins**, away from the house.
- **Compost properly.** A badly kept pile feeds rodents. Keep it hot, turned and contained, with
  **no meat or fat in an open pile** (→ 05-11).
- **Traps, and a lot of them.** Snap traps work and can be reused. Poison bait is a danger to
  children and pets: **use it with real care or not at all** (→ 20-02, → 20-09).
- **Pets help**, but a cat is not a pest-control program.
- **Seal entry points.** Rodents fit through very small gaps. Stuff gaps with steel wool and
  cover them with hardware cloth (stiff wire mesh). Sealing for heat also seals out rodents
  (→ 08-03).

## Winter changes things, mostly for the better

- **Frozen trash does not smell or draw flies.**
- **Rodents come indoors in winter**: sealing and trapping matter more, not less.
- **Greywater freezes:** plan where it goes, and expect ice.
- **Compost slows almost to a stop in a cold winter.** The pile still takes material; it just
  breaks it down in spring. Plan for the volume.

## Gear and supplies

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Metal lidded trash bins, two or three** | Rodents chew plastic | Away from the house |
| Wash basins or tubs, three or four | Cascading water; catching greywater | Label by use (dishes, laundry, body) |
| Sink strainers or screens | Solids out of greywater: no rot, no pests | Cheap |
| **Compost bin or tumbler** | Removes the worst part of the trash | → 05-11 |
| Heavy contractor bags | Containment | Shared with → 13-01 |
| **Snap traps, many** | Rodent control without poison | Reusable |
| Steel wool and hardware cloth | Sealing rodent entry points | → 08-03 |
| **Rigid sharps containers, several** (if anyone uses sharps) | Needles, lancets, applicators | A detergent bottle is acceptable: mark SHARPS, tape shut at three-quarters full |
| **A separate sealed container for used pump pods** (if used) | Battery inside: never burn | Rigid, labeled, away from heat |
| Litter scoop and bags (if you have cats) | Daily scooping prevents toxoplasmosis | Store or bury like human waste |
| Buckets for cascading water | Move greywater to its next use | Label them |
| A watering can kept for greywater | Pouring greywater onto soil | Never used with drinking-water gear |
| Work gloves | Handling trash | Wash hands after (→ 13-02) |

**If you don't have it:**

- **Containers:** buckets, drums, totes, metal cans from anywhere.
- **Strainers:** window screen over a basin, a colander, cloth over a bucket.
- **Rodent-proofing:** steel wool, sheet metal, hardware cloth, glass and metal containers.
- **Traps:** home-made bucket traps work; snap traps are better and cheap.
- **Compost bin:** a circle of wire fencing, pallets wired together, a pit, a pile.
- **What not to improvise:** a sharps container worse than the FDA fallback. A rigid, lidded,
  labeled heavy plastic bottle at minimum: **never a bag, never a thin milk jug, never anything
  unlabeled.**

## When it runs out

- **Trash bags are the consumable**, shared with → 13-01. **Stock boxes.** When they are gone,
  containers replace bags: buckets and cans that are emptied and cleaned, not thrown away.
- **The real shortage is space and disposal.** **Shrink the stream hard:** compost, burn what is
  safe to burn, and treat glass, metal, fabric and plastic as materials. **In a long event, most
  of what a household throws away is a resource** (→ 22-02).
- **Ration greywater by cascading it:** the same water doing three jobs.
- **The failure to avoid:** trash piling up uncontained near the house. That is how a rodent
  population starts, and once settled it is very hard to remove, and it threatens your whole
  food store (→ 04-11).

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** collection may keep running for a while: use that window. Compost
  at once, set up the cascade, get rodent-proof bins before you need them. Neighbors'
  uncollected trash becomes your rodent problem too (→ 18-01).
- **B (moving):** pack out what you must, bury nothing that draws animals near where you sleep,
  and **never leave food waste at a camp.**
- **C (rural or at a destination):** more room and no collection at all, so **the system has to
  be complete from the first day**: compost, a burn pile (safe materials only, and mind the fire
  risk in dry grass, → 17-05), scrap and materials storage, and a greywater spot away from the
  well and the vegetable beds (→ 13-07).
