# Menstrual supplies and diapering without stores

**What this page is for.** Two of the biggest, most relentless consumables in a household, and
the reusable alternatives that end the dependence on shops: menstrual cups and cloth pads, and
cloth diapers.

> **CAUTION.** A baby or toddler in diapers needs them every day: you cannot ration, put off or
> wait out that need. **Menstrual supplies are needed for years.** Together they are probably
> the two most under-planned consumables in most stockpiles.
>
> **Both are hygiene issues.** Wash hands before and after changing a menstrual product or a
> diaper (CDC). **A speck of stool too small to see holds millions of germs** (CDC): diapering
> is a direct route for stomach illness into a household with small children who touch
> everything (→ 13-02, → 12-05).

## Do this now

1. **Buy menstrual cups and cloth pads, and start using them now.** One of the most valuable
   purchases in this binder: **one purchase that permanently removes a recurring
   consumable.** There is a learning curve; get through it before a crisis.
2. **Buy cloth diapers and covers** in the sizes your child will need next.{{#if has.infant}}
   Even if you use disposables day to day, a stock of cloth diapers turns an impossible
   shortage into a laundry job.{{/if}}
3. **Stock disposables deeply as the buffer.** Bulky, but they get you through the first weeks
   without adding laundry to everything else.
4. **Set up the wash system now** (→ 13-04): a lidded pail, a tub kept for diapers, and a way to
   heat water.

## Menstrual supplies

**The reusable options:**

| Option | Lasts | Needs | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Menstrual cup** (a soft silicone cup worn inside, emptied and rinsed) | **Years** | Clean hands, water, boiling between periods | **The best preparedness item in this category.** Compact, no consumables, no laundry |
| **Cloth pads** | Years | Washing and drying | Easy to learn; can be made from cotton flannel |
| **Period underwear** | Years | Washing and drying | Comfortable; bulkier laundry |
| Disposable pads and tampons | One use | Storage space | The buffer, not the plan |

**A cup needs almost nothing in a crisis:** clean hands, a little water for rinsing, and
boiling between periods. It works on the move when nothing else does. Get one and learn it now.

**Hygiene rules (CDC, Menstrual Hygiene):**

- **Wash hands with soap and water before and after** changing any product.
- **Change regularly, and follow the product's own instructions.** CDC: change pads every few
  hours whatever the flow, more often for heavy periods; change tampons every 4 to 8 hours and
  never wear one longer than 8 hours; use the lowest absorbency you need. For tampons and cups
  the label matters: toxic shock syndrome (a rare, dangerous infection) is real.
- **Wash reusable products thoroughly and dry them completely.** Sunlight helps and is free
  (→ 13-04). Soak cloth in **cold** water first; hot water sets blood.
- **Throw away used disposables in a closed, lidded bin:** not into a compost system (→ 13-06)
  and not into a pit you will use later (→ 13-07).
- **Clean a cup daily, and boil it for 1 to 2 minutes after each period** (CDC), in a small
  pot on a fire already going for cooking (→ 07-01).

**Making cloth pads:** flannel or old cotton (a worn flannel sheet, a soft towel), several
absorbent layers, and a tightly woven or waterproof backing, sewn (→ 16-04). **Snap or tie it
around the underwear.** A straightforward sewing project that makes the supply renewable.

More on menstrual health and contraception: → 02-25.

## Diapering a baby or toddler

**This is the relentless one.** A baby or toddler goes through several diapers a day, every day,
for a year or more. Disposables at that rate are a very large volume of stock and of waste
(→ 13-03), and once they are gone, they are gone.

{{#if has.infant}}**Your household has a child in diapers.**{{/if}}

### Diaper worksheet: fill it in, then copy to → 23-02

No published figure fits every child: newborns use far more diapers a day than toddlers.
**Count your child's real week**, then work out the stock.

- Child's current diaper size: ______ · Next size up: ______
- Diapers on hand now (count the packs): ______
- Wipes packs on hand: ______ · Barrier cream tubes on hand: ______
- Real diapers per day this week (count once, honestly): ______
- **Days of stock = diapers on hand ÷ diapers per day = ______ days.**
- **A 90-day buffer = diapers per day x 90 = ______ diapers.** Round up: illness (diarrhea,
  → 12-05) raises the rate exactly when resupply is least likely.
- **Buy for the next sizes, not just today's.** A diaper one size too big still works (snugger
  waist, more leaks); one size too small does not close. A child outgrows a size during a long
  event, so split the stock across the current size and the next one or two, heavier on the
  next size (→ 11-09).
- **Wipes:** count how long one pack lasts: ______ days; packs for 90 days = 90 ÷ that =
  ______. Washcloths and warm soapy water are the fully renewable substitute.

### Cloth diapers: the system

{{figure fig-flat-diaper-fold}}

- **Enough diapers for at least two days of changes**, so you can wash every other day instead
  of daily: your diapers per day x 2, plus spares for the day they do not dry. **Flats** (a single large square of cotton you fold) or **prefolds** (a pre-sewn thicker
  rectangle), plus covers, are the toughest, cheapest and most repairable setup. **Flats dry
  fastest**, which matters a lot in a cold house (→ 11-03).
- **Covers** (waterproof outer pants): several per size; reused between changes unless soiled.
- **A lidded pail.** A dry pail (just a lidded container) is simpler and safer around a toddler
  than a pail full of water, which is a drowning risk.
- **Fasteners:** snap-style fasteners or safety pins, with spares.

{{photo diaper-station}}

**The routine:**

1. **Scrape or shake solids into the toilet system** (→ 13-01). Solids do not go in the wash.
2. **Store in the lidded pail** until wash day.
3. **Wash:** soak, then **hot water and soap**, agitate, **rinse thoroughly**: leftover soap
   causes rashes, so rinse more than you think (→ 13-04).
4. **After a stomach illness,** add a sanitizing rinse of diluted bleach to the diapers
   themselves, following the bleach label's laundry-sanitizing directions, then extra
   clean-water rinses. Never bleach the covers: bleach ruins the waterproof layer.
5. **Dry completely.** Sun-dry when you can (sunlight bleaches stains and helps kill germs, for
   free), freeze-dry outdoors in winter, or use the indoor line (→ 11-03).
6. **Wash your hands** properly after every change and after handling the pail (CDC).

> **Floor:** Bleach or pool shock is never mixed with ammonia, acids or other cleaners. Urine-soaked diapers
> contain ammonia: rinse them before any bleach rinse, and never add bleach to a pail of soiled
> diapers. The mix gives off toxic gas. (→ 30-01)

**Preventing rash matters more without a doctor:**

- **Change often.** A wet diaper against skin in a cold house is a rash risk and drains heat
  (→ 11-02).
- **Dry the skin fully** before the new diaper.
- **Air time:** a little time with no diaper, in the warm room, every day.
- **Barrier ointment:** stock it; petroleum jelly works (→ 12-07).
- **Rinse thoroughly** in the wash: soap residue is a leading cause of rash that will not go
  away.
- **Watch for rash that is not getting better.** It can turn into a yeast or bacterial infection
  that needs treatment (→ 12-07, → 12-04).

**Older children who are out of diapers** often go backwards under stress. That is normal and
common (→ 20-08). Have some larger cloth options and treat it as unremarkable.

## Water and work

**Both reusable systems swap a supply problem for a water-and-labour problem.**

- **Diaper laundry is an almost daily job:** real water, soap, fuel for hot water and drying
  space (→ 13-04, → 11-03).
- **In winter, drying is the limit:** that is why **flats** are the right choice, and why you
  keep more than two days' worth when drying is slow.
- **Put water, soap and fuel for diaper laundry in the household budget** (→ 03-01, → 23-02).
  It is easy to leave out.
- **This is the case for the disposable buffer:** use disposables during illness, on a move,
  in a cold snap when drying is impossible, and in the first chaotic weeks.

## Gear and supplies

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Menstrual cups, two per person who menstruates** | Years of use, no consumables, works on the move | **Learn to use them now** |
| **Cloth pads or period underwear** | Reusable backup | Can be made (→ 16-04) |
| Disposable menstrual supplies | Buffer for the first weeks and travel | Deep stock |
| **Cloth diapers, at least two days' changes plus spares (flats or prefolds)** | Flats dry fastest | Plus the next size |
| Diaper covers, six to eight | Reused between changes | Waterproof |
| Snap fasteners or pins | Fastening | Spares |
| **Lidded diaper pail** | Containment, smell, keeps a toddler out | Dry pail |
| A wash tub and large pot kept for diapers | Diaper laundry is its own job | → 13-04 |
| Barrier ointment or petroleum jelly | Rash prevention without a doctor | Many uses |
| **Disposable diapers, deep buffer** | Illness, travel, cold snaps, chaos | Your daily count x 90, split across sizes |
| Wipes, by your own count | 90 days' worth | Washcloths substitute |
| Soap in bulk | Diaper laundry uses a lot | → 13-09 |
| Drying lines and racks | The real limit | → 11-03 |
| Hand sanitizer at the changing area | For when washing is not immediate | Not a replacement (CDC) |

**If you don't have it:**

- **Cloth diapers:** flannel sheets, cotton towels, T-shirts, receiving blankets cut and hemmed.
  **A flat is just a square of absorbent cotton, folded.** The simplest design, and it works.
- **Covers:** wool "soakers" (wool resists water and breathes: a traditional, excellent choice,
  → 11-05), or a cover from any water-resistant fabric.
- **Menstrual cloth:** folded cotton flannel or towel, washed. This is what people used for most
  of history.
- **Fasteners:** safety pins (stock plenty), ties, a snug cover holding the folded cloth.
- **Pail:** any bucket with a lid.
- **What you cannot easily improvise:** a menstrual cup. **Buy them now:** small, cheap, and
  they last years.

## When it runs out

- **Disposables run out; reusables do not.** The reusable versions turn a hard shortage into an
  ongoing water, soap and labour cost, which is a much better problem to have.
- **What does run out:** soap (makeable, → 13-09), fasteners and elastic (**stock spares**,
  → 11-07), covers (they wear out: stock extras), and **the child's size** (buy ahead, → 11-09).
- **Runway:** count disposables in **days of child**, not packages: the worksheet above does
  it. Put the days in the diapers row of → 23-02 and re-count monthly as the rate and size
  change.
- **Ration:** **use disposables on purpose:** illness, cold snaps, travel, the first weeks.
  Cloth the rest of the time.
- **Substitute:** cotton cloth from any source. **Fabric is one of the most plentiful things in
  empty houses** (→ 22-02).
- **Trade:** children's supplies are one of the easiest, least tense things to barter (→ 18-02):
  everyone with children has outgrown gear and needs the next size.

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** water and washing are available, so **run cloth as the default and
  keep disposables as the buffer.** Set up the pail, tub and line early and settle the routine
  while it is still easy.
- **B (moving):** **disposables, without hesitation.** You cannot wash and dry diapers on the
  move in winter, and a wet child in the cold is a serious problem (→ 11-02). The menstrual cup
  is the one item here that is actually *better* while traveling. Pack out diaper waste or bury
  it properly, away from water (→ 13-07).
- **C (rural or at a destination):** cloth throughout, with more drying space and water. **Build
  a real laundry setup near the stove** and make diaper washing a fixed daily job.
