# Teaching and preserving knowledge

**What this page is for.** Keeping what your household knows alive when screens fail, and passing it
on. A dead phone, a burned house, a failed drive or an internet that stops working takes with it
almost everything a modern household knows how to look up. Two jobs: **preserving** knowledge (paper
books, your own written records, copies of this binder) and **passing it on** (teaching your
children and neighbours, and being taught). Teaching costs nothing, multiplies a community's
abilities, and makes the teacher someone a community protects (→ 18-03).

## Do this now

1. **Buy the paper books** in the list below. Printed and on a shelf, not bookmarked. A few hundred
   dollars for a library that cannot be bought later.
2. **Keep this binder on more than one device**, charged (→ 00-10), and **export and print the pages
   you would need first** (the emergency quick reference, the medical pages for your household's
   conditions, the water pages). An app on a dead phone is not a binder.
3. **Start writing things down** (→ 25-05): what you learn, what works, what your household actually
   uses. Your own records will be more useful than any book.
4. **Teach one thing to someone this year** (→ 18-03): a neighbour, one of the children (→ 20-07).
   Teaching shows whether you really know it.

## Why paper

- **No power, device, network or battery needed.** No file format goes out of date.
- **Survives** a dead phone, a lost laptop, a failed drive and, stored sensibly, a wet basement.
- **Anyone can read it**, including someone who is not you; another adult may be the one using it.
- **You do not have to have predicted the question**, as you do when choosing what to download.
- **You can write in it**, which is how a reference becomes yours.
- **Digital storage fails all at once and silently.** You find out when you need it.

**And the one people forget: books for pleasure.** On a long winter night, a stack of novels is a
morale asset (→ 21-02).

## Your own records: more useful than any book

The most valuable reference in a year will be the one you wrote (→ 25-05):
- **What you actually use:** water, wood, food, batteries (→ 23-02). Your real numbers, not planning
  guesses.
- **What worked and what did not:** the vegetable variety that produced, the repair that held, the
  road that was passable.
- **Weather and seasons:** frost dates you saw, when things bloomed, when the ice went out. Over a few
  years this becomes a valuable local record (→ 25-01).
- **A medical log:** illnesses, treatments, results, and the daily records any condition needs
  (→ 12-11).
- **Annotated maps:** where the water is, where the wild fruit is, which routes work (→ 25-04).
- **A household journal:** what happened. Good for morale now (→ 21-06) and for the children later.
- **Pencil or waterproof ink**, in a bound book, kept dry.

- **In ten years they will be capable people, and what they know will be what you taught them.**
- **Teach by doing it alongside them.** That is how young children learn (CDC, Positive Parenting
  Tips).
- **Practical skills as they are ready:** knots (→ 16-03), sewing (→ 16-04), gardening, cooking,
  animals, and later tools, fire and first aid.
- **Reading and arithmetic** are the foundation everything else needs (→ 20-03).
- **Let them see you learning.** It models the most important skill of all.
- **Write down what you want them to know** in case you are not there to teach it. A hard thought,
  and a worthwhile document.

## Teaching neighbours, and being taught

- **Teaching multiplies a community's abilities** and costs you nothing (→ 18-03).
- **A community where three people can do first aid is far safer** than one where one person can
  (→ 12-16), and that protects you too.
- **Being taught is just as valuable.** Asking someone to teach you is one of the best ways to build
  a relationship (→ 18-01). People are glad to be asked.
- **Trade lessons, not just goods** (→ 18-02).
- **Teach other people's children** where it is welcome. It builds the community your children will
  grow up in.

## Keeping it safe

- **Dry, off the floor, out of direct sun.** Basements flood (→ 17-03); attics bake.
- **A waterproof box** for the things you cannot replace: printed binder pages, medical references,
  documents (→ 19-01).
- **A second copy of the critical few stored away from the house** (→ 19-03). A house fire takes
  everything at once.
- **Digital backups as well.** They are cheap and cover different failures (→ 19-03). Just do not
  rely on them alone.
- **Write in the margins.** A used reference is worth more than a clean one.

## The bigger picture

In a long disruption, knowledge decides whether a community recovers or only survives. The skills in
this binder (growing, preserving, treating, building, fixing, communicating) are the same skills
that rebuild. Preserving and teaching them helps beyond your household, and it is the most lasting
security there is: a community that needs what you know wants you in it.

**Substitutes:** libraries' sale shelves, thrift stores and estate sales are full of these books.
Swapping books with neighbours is one of the easiest community activities there is. **People are the
other library**: someone who knows how to do something is a reference you cannot print.

## When it runs out

- **Books do not run out.** They get wet, burned or lost: hence the waterproof box and the second
  set.
- **Paper and pencils do run out.** Stock them; they carry everything on this page.
- **What really fades is knowledge nobody teaches.** A skill not passed on is gone in one
  generation. Teaching is the upkeep.
- **Teaching by doing, telling and repeating** is how most human knowledge moved for most of history.
  It still works, and it is how your children will learn most of what they learn.
- **Knowledge is the one asset in this plan that grows in value.**

## By phase

- **A, at home:** buy the library, print the key pages, start the records, teach one thing.
- **B, on the move:** the binder on a charged phone and a few printed pages; a small notebook.
- **C, settled:** records become a local almanac; teaching becomes a contribution the community
  values.
