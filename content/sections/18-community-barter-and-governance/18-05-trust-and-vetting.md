# Trust, vetting and strangers

**What this page is for.** How to judge people, answer a knock at the door, decide who learns your
household's sensitive facts, and teach children the family rules, without sliding into either "trust
everyone" or "trust no one". Neither is a plan.

> **CAUTION. Start with what disaster research shows: in real disasters, most people help.**
> Looting and predation are the exception. The common pattern is spontaneous cooperation, sharing
> and rescue by ordinary people. Households that assume the opposite cut themselves off from the
> help they will need (→ 18-01). **And** not everyone is safe, and judgment matters, especially for a
> household with small children or a life-critical medicine.
>
> **This page is about judging people, not about force.** When and whether force is used is a
> separate question your household writes down on its own worksheet: {{choice DC-12}} (→ 30-13,
> → 14-11).

## Do this now

1. **Build relationships now** (→ 18-01). Judging someone is far easier when you have known them for
   years than when you meet them at your gate.
2. **Agree, among the adults, how you will answer a knock at the door**, what you will disclose and
   who decides (→ 00-07, → 21-05).
3. **Agree a duress signal** between the adults (→ 15-07): an ordinary-sounding phrase that means
   "this is not fine".
{{#if has.child}}4. **Teach the children the family rules** about strangers and about not telling (below), simply
   and without frightening them (→ 20-04).{{/if}}

## What disaster research shows

- **Most people behave helpfully in disasters.** They help strangers, share and organize themselves.
- **Panic and mass lawlessness are rare** and are often over-reported.
- **The bigger real risks are usually** disorganization, scarcity, illness, rumors and exhaustion.
- **Neighbors usually respond faster and better than officials** in the first hours and days
  (→ 18-01).

**So a posture of cautious openness is both more accurate and more useful than default suspicion.**
Isolation costs you the help, information, skills and morale that protect you most (CDC, About
Social Connection).

## But judgment still matters

- **Not everyone is safe**, and desperate people do things they otherwise would not.
- **The longer and harder a situation, the more the balance shifts** (→ 14-01).
- **You can be wrong in both directions**, and both errors cost.

## Judging people: practical, not paranoid

**Time is the best test there is.** Almost everything else stands in for it.
- **Known beats unknown.** Someone your neighbors vouch for is a different matter from a stranger.
- **Consistency over time.** Do their story and behavior line up across several meetings?
- **Small tests before large ones.** Trade small (→ 18-02), lend something minor, share information
  before supplies.
- **Reciprocity.** Do they give as well as take? Only taking is information.
- **How they treat people with nothing to offer**: children, the elderly, the sick. One of the most
  reliable signals there is.
- **How they talk about others.** Someone who boasts of cheating people is telling you something.
- **Your instinct.** If something feels wrong, act on it and think later. Instinct is fast but also
  biased, so **use it to create distance, not to justify hostility** (→ 21-01).

## The knock at the door

Decide this in advance (→ 00-07):
- **Answer or not?** Not answering is a complete option and needs no justification.
- **Talk through the door or from a window.** You can help without opening it.
- **Do not let a stranger inside** in an uncertain situation. Once inside, they have seen everything
  (→ 14-03).
- **Meet away from the house** for anything like a trade (→ 18-02).
- **Two adults aware:** one talks; one is present somewhere else.
- **"We don't have anything to spare" is a complete answer.** It needs no argument or apology.
- **You can help without revealing anything:** a cup of water, directions, information.
- **Small, bounded help is usually possible and usually right.** It costs little, and a neighborhood
  where people help each other is safer for you.

## The hard case

**Someone in real need whom you cannot fully help**: hungry, cold, with children. There is no clean
answer, and it is worth having thought about before it happens (→ 12-16, → 21-03).
- **Small, bounded, immediate help** is usually possible: a meal, water, an hour of warmth,
  directions to where help is.
- **Open-ended help you cannot keep up hurts everyone**, them included. A promise you have to
  withdraw is worse than none.
- **Pointing people to real resources** (→ 25-03) is genuine help and costs nothing.
- **Your first duty is to your own household**, and that is not a failing.
- **Decide as a household, in advance where possible** (→ 21-05). Disagreeing about this in the
  moment is corrosive.

## Bringing people in

If a situation goes long, joining with others comes up (→ 18-04):
- **Skills and character matter more than supplies** (→ 18-03).
- **Slowly.** Cooperation before combining.
- **Agree the terms first:** work, resources, decisions, and how someone leaves (→ 18-04, → 18-06).
- **Everyone who might feed or mind your children knows their allergies and medical needs.**
- **Family and close friends are the usual first circle**, and even there the terms are said out
  loud.

## Medical privacy: who learns what, and when

If anyone in your household depends on a medicine or device they cannot live without (insulin,
epinephrine, oxygen, a pump, anti-seizure medicine), that fact is two exposures in one. It says
there is a supply of valuable, hard-to-replace medicine in the house, and it shows exactly what
taking or destroying that supply would do to your family. Most leaks reveal *what you have*; this
one reveals *what you cannot live without*.

**A framework many households use.** Decide your own circles and write them below.

| Circle | Usually | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A household you will move in with (→ 18-04) | **Yes, fully**: before arrival where there is a "before", or in the first hour | Living together while hiding a life-support need is planning built on sand: refrigeration, supplies and the "if they are down" plan all depend on them knowing |
| Trusted neighbors (T on the → 18-01 roster) | The fact, if useful; **never quantities or where it is kept** | They may need to help in an emergency |
| Other neighbors | Capabilities, not vulnerabilities | They can know you are a nurse or a mechanic without knowing a member is insulin-dependent |
| Strangers and trading contacts | **Never** | No trade or conversation is worth handing a stranger your household's pressure point |

**Disclosure follows commitment, not acquaintance.** When two households truly commit to live
together, full medical disclosure is owed and essential (→ 18-04). Until then, the same information
is pure exposure. The order is: trust, then commitment, then disclosure. Never disclosure as a way
to build trust.

## Teaching children

{{#if has.child}}Children in your household: {{#each children as c}}{{c.label}} ({{c.age_text}}); {{/each}}{{/if}}

- **Simple rules for a young child:** stay with a grown-up; do not go anywhere with anyone; do not
  tell people about our house or our things; tell us if someone talks to you (→ 20-04).
- **A rule, not a fear.** "That's our family rule" works better than describing danger (→ 20-08).
- **Let them also see adults being kind to people.** Children learn the whole posture, not just the
  caution. You want them to learn "we're careful and we help", not "people are bad".
- **Babies and toddlers** are kept safe by supervision and barriers, always (→ 20-02).

## The two ways to get it wrong

| Error | What it costs |
|---|---|
| Trusting everyone | Losing supplies, revealing your position, letting the wrong person in |
| **Trusting no one** | **Isolation, a documented risk factor** (CDC); no medical help (→ 12-16); no relief; no information; worse morale (→ 21-02); and a reputation that makes later cooperation impossible |

**The second error is more common in prepared households and less talked about**, and the damage is
hard to undo.

## Gear

| Item | Why | Notes |

| Your posture and roles, written | Decided calmly, not at the door | → 00-07 |
| **A duress signal agreed between adults** | A phrase that means the opposite of what it says | → 15-07 |
| A way to see who is at the door | Window, peephole | |
| Exterior lighting, used deliberately | Balanced with light discipline | → 10-05, → 14-02 |
| A written list of real local resources | Helping by pointing costs nothing | → 25-03 |
| Something small to give | Bounded help is often right | Water, a meal |

**Time, observation and your neighbors' knowledge** substitute for every other vetting method, and
they are free. **Distance** substitutes for confrontation in almost every case: not answering, not
engaging and not being where a problem is are complete strategies (→ 14-01). **What you cannot
substitute is judgment, and exhaustion, fear and hunger degrade it** (→ 21-01).

## When it runs out

- **What runs out is the benefit of the doubt.** It erodes as things get longer and harder. Notice
  it: a household that has slid into default hostility has cut itself off from its strongest
  protection (CDC).
- **Re-check your posture deliberately** from time to time (→ 14-01). Conditions change both ways: a
  chaotic neighborhood in week two may be organized by month two.
- **For most households, the biggest risks are medical and environmental, not human.** The people
  around you are far more likely to be part of the solution.

## By phase

- **A, at home:** neighbors close, mostly known, mostly helpful. Build relationships, keep ordinary
  discretion, and be someone people are glad lives on their street.
- **B, on the move:** strangers are unavoidable and cannot be judged over time (→ 01-03). Distance and
  avoidance are the main tools (→ 14-01). Help where it is cheap and safe; do not camp near roads or
  where you can be approached unseen.
- **C, settled:** fewer people, more consequential meetings. Rural communities often take time to
  accept newcomers, then are strongly cooperative. Arrive as someone who contributes (→ 18-03) and
  the judging takes care of itself.
