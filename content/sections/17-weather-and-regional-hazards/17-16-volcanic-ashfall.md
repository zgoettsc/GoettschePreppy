# Volcanic ashfall

**What this page is for.** An eruption can drop ash over a huge area, hundreds of miles downwind.
Volcanic ash is not soft like wood ash: it is tiny, sharp bits of rock and glass. It hurts lungs and
eyes, wrecks engines and electronics, fouls water, and is heavy enough when wet to break roofs.
Closer to a volcano there are other dangers, below. This page is about living through ashfall.
{{#if is.volcano_region}}**Your home county is rated 2 or 3 for volcanic hazard in the region
data.**{{else}}Your home county is rated lower for volcanic hazard; ash travels far, so keep this
page if you live downwind of the western volcanoes or travel near them.{{/if}}
Ratings in this binder are FEMA's National Risk Index for your county, on a 1-to-3 scale: they measure expected harm to people and property there, not how likely the hazard is (→ 29-10).

> **CAUTION.**
> - **Stay indoors during ashfall** with windows, doors and vents closed, until officials say it
>   is safe to go out (Ready.gov, Volcanoes).
> - **Outside, wear a well-fitting N95 respirator**; a dust or cloth mask only as a last resort
>   (Ready.gov).
> - **Avoid driving in heavy ash.** It cuts visibility, makes roads slick, and clogs engines.
> - **Do not get on your roof to remove ash** (Ready.gov, Volcanoes). Wet ash is heavy and roofs
>   are slippery.
> - **Near a volcano, valleys and river channels are where mudflows (lahars) run.** Get to high
>   ground out of the valley if one is reported.

## Do this now

1. **Know whether you live near a volcano or downwind of one**, and whether your valley is a lahar
   path. Local emergency plans and the USGS volcano observatories map both.
2. **Keep N95 respirators** for every adult and older child, and **glasses** for anyone who wears
   contact lenses (ash under a lens scratches the eye).
3. **Plan to cover stored water and disconnect rain barrels** from downspouts when ash is forecast
   (→ 03-05).
4. **Keep spare air filters** for the vehicle and the furnace.

## Alerts

- **Ashfall Advisory** and **Ashfall Warning** from the NWS: ash is expected to fall where you are
  (→ 29-10).
- **USGS volcano observatories** issue alert levels for each volcano, from normal through advisory
  and watch to warning. A warning means a hazardous eruption is happening or about to.
- **Evacuation orders** come from local officials, mostly for areas near the volcano and in lahar
  paths. **If told to leave, leave** (Ready.gov).

## During ashfall

- **Stay inside.** Close windows, doors and fireplace dampers; cover vents and seal gaps
  (Ready.gov). Set heating or cooling to recirculate, or turn it off.
- **Make one clean room** as for wildfire smoke (→ 17-14): a closed room, filtered air, no
  indoor dust-raising.
- **Protect electronics and machinery:** cover them; wet ash conducts electricity and can short
  equipment and power lines. Expect outages (→ 17-08).
- **Bring pets and livestock in** or under cover; give them clean water.
- **If you must go out:** N95, goggles or glasses, long sleeves. Change and leave dusty clothes at
  the door.
- **Driving:** avoid driving in heavy ash. If you must, keep the windows up and do not use the air
  conditioning (Ready.gov); go slowly with headlights on. Ash stirred up by traffic stalls
  engines.

{{#if has.mobile_home}}**Mobile or manufactured home:** many have low-pitched roofs with long spans,
the shape most at risk from ash load. Keep gutters clear and plan where you would go if ash builds
up and rain is forecast.{{/if}}
{{#if has.apartment}}**Apartment:** shared ventilation can bring ash in; seal your door gaps and
make the clean room inside your unit.{{/if}}

## Roofs: the ash load

- **Roof collapse from ash is rare** and usually needs very thick ash, typically more than about 4
  inches (100 mm) (USGS, Roof Loading).
- **Rain makes it much worse.** Wet ash is up to twice as dense as dry ash, so the load is up to
  twice as heavy (USGS).
- **Long-span, low-pitched roofs that were not engineered** (carports, barns, wide spans) are the
  most at risk, possibly at about 4 inches or less (USGS).
- **Gutters fill with ash,** stop draining and add weight. Clear them.
- **Clearing:** Ready.gov's advice is not to get on the roof. Where you can reach, work from the
  ground with long-handled tools. Damp the ash lightly to keep dust down, but **do not soak it**:
  water adds weight.

## After

- **Clean up with masks on.** Lightly dampen ash before sweeping or shovelling; dry sweeping raises
  thick dust. Bag it; do not wash it into drains, where it sets and blocks them.
- **Water:** ash in an open water source can make it cloudy, acidic or salty-tasting. Use stored
  water (→ 03-02); treat any other source (→ 03-04). Clean rain-barrel systems before reconnecting.
- **Gardens:** wash produce well; light ash can be dug into soil.
- **Vehicles:** change air and oil filters often; wash ash off before it scratches paint and glass.
- **People with asthma or lung disease** stay in clean air and keep rescue medicine close
  (→ 02-07, → 02-08).

**If you do not have it:** a damp cloth over nose and mouth is a poor last resort; wet towels along
door gaps; plastic bags over electronics.

## When it runs out

- **Filters and masks run out first** in a long ash event. Stock more than seems needed.
- **Ash keeps coming back** with every wind and every passing car for weeks; clean up in steps.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** stay in, seal up, protect water and equipment.
- **B (moving):** avoid driving through heavy ash; never drive into a valley where a lahar is
  reported (→ 01-03).
- **C (rural or new site):** check whether a site sits in a lahar path or a common ash plume
  direction from a nearby volcano (→ 01-05).
