# Wildfire smoke and fire evacuation

**What this page is for.** Wildfire reaches far more people as smoke than as flame: smoke from a
fire hundreds of miles away can make the air unhealthy for days or weeks. And when a fire is close,
leaving early is what saves lives. This page covers reading the air quality number, making one
clean room in the house, masks, and getting out when told. How fires behave and how to protect the
house is on → 17-05.
{{#if is.wildfire_region}}**Your home county is rated 2 or 3 for wildfire in the region data.**{{else}}Your
home county is rated lower for wildfire, but smoke travels far; the smoke half of this page applies
almost everywhere.{{/if}}
Ratings in this binder are FEMA's National Risk Index for your county, on a 1-to-3 scale: they measure expected harm to people and property there, not how likely the hazard is (→ 29-10).

> **SAFETY.**
> - **When officials say evacuate, go at once.** Fire can move faster than the warnings. Do not wait
>   to see the flames.
> - **Smoke harms children, older adults, pregnant women and anyone with heart or lung disease
>   first** (EPA, Reduce Your Smoke Exposure). Act for them at a lower air quality number.
> - **No respirator on a child under 2** (EPA). Their protection is clean indoor air and distance.
> - **Never run a generator, charcoal, or any engine indoors, in a garage, or near an open
>   window**, even to power an air cleaner (→ 30-01).

## Do this now

1. **Choose a clean room** (below) and work out how you will filter its air.
2. **Buy N95 respirators** in sizes that fit each adult and older child, and practice the fit.
3. **Pack go-bags and the document kit** and keep them by the door in fire season (→ 22-03,
   → 19-01).
4. **Know how your area announces evacuations** (phone alerts, sirens, door-to-door, radio) and
   your two routes out.
5. **Decide your leaving rule now:** {{choice DC-19}} → 30-20

## Reading the air: the Air Quality Index

The **Air Quality Index (AQI)** is a 0 to 500 scale for how polluted the air is. Smoke shows up as
"particle pollution". The EPA categories (AirNow, AQI Basics):

| AQI | Colour | Category | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 to 50 | Green | Good | Little or no risk |
| 51 to 100 | Yellow | Moderate | Acceptable; some risk for people unusually sensitive to air pollution |
| 101 to 150 | Orange | Unhealthy for sensitive groups | Sensitive groups (children, older adults, pregnant women, heart or lung disease) may have health effects; the general public less likely |
| 151 to 200 | Red | Unhealthy | Some of the general public may have health effects; sensitive groups more serious ones |
| 201 to 300 | Purple | Very unhealthy | Health alert: the risk of health effects is increased for everyone |
| 301 and up | Maroon | Hazardous | Health warning of emergency conditions: everyone is more likely to be affected |

**Alert words:** an **Air Quality Alert** means the air is or will be unhealthy; a **Dense Smoke
Advisory** means smoke is cutting visibility; a **Red Flag Warning** means fire weather (→ 17-05,
→ 29-10).

**With no AQI to read:** if you can see haze indoors, smell smoke, or cannot see landmarks you
normally can, treat the air as unhealthy. If eyes sting and people cough, it is worse.

## What to do when the air is smoky

- **Stay indoors, with windows and doors shut,** and limit hard work outside.
- **Make one clean room** and spend as much time in it as you can, especially at night (EPA, Create
  a Clean Room):
  1. **Pick a room** big enough for everyone, ideally with a bathroom, whose door you can keep
     shut.
  2. **Close its windows and doors**, as long as you can still get out quickly. If the room has an
     exhaust fan or range hood, use it only briefly: it can pull smoke in from elsewhere in the
     house.
  3. **Keep it cool.** If your heating, cooling or window unit has a fresh-air setting, turn it
     off, close the intake, or set it to recirculate.
  4. **Filter the air** with a portable air cleaner sized for the room, on its highest setting, of
     a type that makes no ozone. **If you have none, a box fan with a furnace filter taped to it**
     is a temporary substitute that EPA describes for when better options are not available.
  5. **Make no smoke inside:** no candles, incense, frying, broiling, smoking, aerosol sprays,
     fuel-burning stoves, or vacuuming without a HEPA filter. Dust with a damp cloth.
- **Central air or furnace fan:** set to recirculate, with the best filter the system accepts.
- **In a vehicle:** windows up, air on recirculate.

## Masks

- **A NIOSH-approved N95 or P100 respirator, well fitted,** significantly reduces the smoke you
  breathe. Choose one with two head straps, not ear loops. Do not rely on dust masks or bandanas
  (EPA, Protect Your Lungs From Wildfire Smoke and Ash; EPA, Reduce Your Smoke Exposure).
- **Fit is most of it:** no gaps at the nose and cheeks; a good seal is not possible with facial
  hair (EPA).
- **No respirator on a child under 2** (EPA). Respirators do not come in children's sizes; some
  older children can use an adult small one if it fits and they will keep it on. For them too,
  clean indoor air and distance come first.
- **Ask a doctor first** if you have heart or lung disease or are pregnant: a respirator makes
  breathing harder, and in heat it raises the risk of heat illness (EPA).
- A mask helps when you must be outside; it is not a reason to stay out longer.

## Who needs protecting first

{{#if has.condition.CL-07}}- **Asthma:** keep rescue medicine in reach and follow the asthma action
  plan; smoke is a common trigger (→ 02-07).{{/if}}
{{#if has.condition.CL-08}}- **COPD or oxygen:** smoke can turn a stable day into an emergency. Never
  have oxygen near a flame (→ 02-08).{{/if}}
{{#if has.condition.CL-09}}- **Heart disease:** smoke raises the risk of heart trouble; chest pain or
  unusual breathlessness is an emergency (→ 02-09).{{/if}}
{{#if has.young_child}}- **Young children** breathe more air for their size and spend time close to
  the floor and outside; they go into the clean room first.{{/if}}
{{#if has.elder}}- **Elders:** check on them twice a day in smoke.{{/if}}
{{#if has.pregnant}}- **Pregnancy:** limit exposure; clean room and fitted mask outside.{{/if}}
- **Anyone:** trouble breathing, chest pain, or confusion in smoke is an emergency. Get them to the
  cleanest air you can.

**If the clean room cannot keep the air clean**, going to a cleaner place (family, a public cleaner
air shelter, a town upwind) is the next step. Leave before people are struggling.

## Fire evacuation

**Many areas use three steps**: be **ready** (bags packed, plan made), **get set** (prepare to
leave now; leave already if you need extra time, have animals, or have someone who moves slowly),
and **go** (leave now). Names differ by place. Your area's words: ______________.

**When you get the order, go**, and do not return until officials say it is safe (Ready.gov,
Wildfires). Before you leave, if there is time:
- **Medicines and devices first**, then documents, bags, phones, chargers (→ 01-07, → 19-01).
- **People who move slowly leave first.** Children into car seats and pets into carriers take
  time; start them first (→ 20-09).
- **Close all windows and doors** so embers cannot blow in; move burnable furniture away from
  windows.
- **Back the vehicle in**, loaded, facing the way out, windows closed.
- **Leave lights on** so firefighters can see the house through smoke.
- **Do not go back** for anything.

**On the road:** drive with headlights on, windows up, air on recirculate. Never drive toward a
fire to get past it. If you are trapped by fire in a vehicle, the steps are on → 17-05.

## By dwelling type

{{#if has.mobile_home}}- **Mobile or manufactured home:** lets smoke in easily and catches fire
  readily. Go to cleaner air sooner, and leave on the first evacuation warning.{{/if}}
{{#if has.apartment}}- **Apartment:** a bedroom with the door shut makes a good clean room. Shared
  ventilation may bring smoke in from the halls; seal the gap under the door with a towel.{{/if}}
- **Any home:** the room with the fewest outside walls and windows is the easiest to keep clean.

## After the fire

- **Return only when officials say it is safe.** Hot spots smoulder for days.
- **Ash:** wear an N95 and gloves to clean; dampen ash before sweeping; keep children away from
  ash and burned areas.
- **Water:** a burned area's water system may be contaminated; follow local notices.
- **Burned slopes flood and slide** in the next rains, for years (→ 17-17).

**If you do not have it:** the smallest interior room with a towel under the door; a damp cloth
over the face is a poor last resort for an adult; leaving early is the best mask there is.

## When it runs out

- **Filters clog.** Smoke seasons can last weeks; keep spares and change them when dark.
- **Patience runs out.** Long smoke episodes wear people down; the clean room rules still apply on
  day ten.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** clean room ready, masks fitted, bags by the door in fire season.
- **B (moving):** smoke cuts visibility and closes roads; check the route and leave early
  (→ 17-11).
- **C (rural or new site):** check how a site would be evacuated: one road in through brush is a
  trap. Defensible space comes first (→ 17-05).
