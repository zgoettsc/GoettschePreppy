# Pandemic and widespread illness

**What this page is for.** A pandemic (a disease spreading widely across many countries) usually
does not break the power grid or the water supply. **It breaks the supply chain and the health
care system.** For a household that depends on a daily medicine, the main danger is often not "we
get sick" but "the pharmacy cannot fill it" and "the clinic is full." This page is household
preparation that works whatever the germ is.

> **CAUTION. Disease-specific guidance must come from CDC and public health authorities at the
> time.** How a disease spreads, how long it incubates, isolation rules, treatment and vaccines
> are specific to each illness. Nothing written in advance replaces current guidance. Where none
> can be had, use the offline default below.

## Do this now

1. **Deepen the medicine supply** (→ 12-07). For a household with a life-sustaining medicine, this
   is the most valuable pandemic preparation by far. Count days on hand for each daily medicine
   and supply, and talk to the prescriber and pharmacy about a longer supply while that system
   works:

   - **{{x.name}}** ({{x.member}}): **{{x.days_on_hand}}** days on hand.{{else}}
   - No medicines are entered in your profile. Add them in settings so this list and → 23-02 can
     track them.{{/each}}
2. **Build food and household supply depth** (→ 04-01, → 04-02) so weeks without going out are
   routine.
3. **Build the hygiene system** (→ 13-02): soap, water, a hand-washing station. It works against
   nearly every germ.
4. **Stock sick-care supplies** (→ 13-11, → 12-05): oral rehydration salts, thermometers, gloves,
   disinfectant, masks, a dedicated sick kit.
5. **Know your household's specific risks** and talk them over with your clinician (→ 12-11).

## The three ways a pandemic hits a household

1. **Direct illness.** Someone gets sick. This is what people plan for, and → 12-05 and → 13-11
   cover it.
2. **Health care saturation.** Often the bigger risk. **An overwhelmed system makes ordinary
   emergencies worse**: an injury, a childbirth, a flare of a chronic condition. Care that is
   normally there becomes unreliable (→ 12-16).
3. **Supply chain disruption.** Medicine shortages, food gaps, shortages of specific goods. For a
   household that depends on a medicine made by few producers, this can matter more than a power
   outage.

## What reduces risk

Layered, and none is complete alone:
1. **Hand washing** with soap at the key moments (CDC). Works against nearly everything.
2. **Fewer exposures:** fewer trips, fewer contacts, staying home when sick. The most effective
   single step and the hardest to keep up.
3. **Cleaning and disinfecting** often-touched surfaces (CDC; → 13-11).
4. **Fresh air.** Air exchange matters for illnesses spread through the air. In winter it fights
   with keeping warm (→ 08-03); the answer is short, deliberate airing several times a day, not a
   sealed house.
5. **Masks** (below). Germ-specific guidance overrides this.
6. **Vaccination** per current public health guidance, **including routine childhood vaccines**
   (→ 12-04), which matter more when the health system is stretched.
7. **Supply depth** so you do not have to go out.
8. **Separating the sick person inside the house** where you can (→ 13-11).

## The offline default: isolation and masks when there is no current guidance

Germ-specific public health guidance overrides this. When there is none to be had, run this (CDC,
Preventing Spread of Respiratory Viruses When You're Sick):
- **The sick person stays home and apart from others while they have symptoms.** They go back to
  normal activity when, **for at least 24 hours, their symptoms are getting better overall and they
  have had no fever without using fever-reducing medicine**, then take **added precautions over
  the next 5 days**: cleaner air, hygiene, a well-fitted mask, distance.
- **Stomach illness:** the same shape. CDC: a person who has had norovirus does not prepare or
  handle food or care for others until at least 2 days (48 hours) after symptoms stop (CDC, How
  to Prevent Norovirus; → 13-11).
- **Masks, best first:** a NIOSH-approved **N95** respirator, then an international respirator
  such as a **KN95**, then a **surgical** (disposable) mask, then **cloth**. Fit matters: gaps let
  air leak in and out (CDC, Masks and Respiratory Viruses Prevention). **No respirator on a child
  under 2** (EPA, Reduce Your Smoke Exposure): their protection is distance and the sick adult
  wearing one.
- **Hands:** soap and water for **20 seconds**, before eating or feeding a child, after coughing or
  caring for the sick, after the toilet. Hand sanitizer with **at least 60% alcohol** when hands are
  not visibly dirty (CDC, Handwashing; → 13-02).
- **One heated room in winter:** true isolation is nearly impossible. The sick person takes the far
  side, wears a mask, does no food preparation, and the room is aired briefly several times a day.

**Sepsis** (the body's extreme reaction to an infection) can follow any infection. Confusion, fast
breathing, a racing heart, extreme pain or clammy skin in someone who is ill is an emergency (CDC,
About Sepsis; → 12-04).

## The stock-up window

**When a situation is developing, the window to prepare is early and it closes fast.** Shelves
empty, prescriptions get limited, and everyone acts at once.
- **Be prepared beforehand; do not panic-buy during.** That is the whole argument for the depth in
  Section 04 and → 12-07.
- **Panic-buying damages the community you will depend on** (→ 18-01).
- **Medicine is the exception worth acting on at once:** if a supply problem looks likely, call
  the prescriber and pharmacy early. That is not hoarding; it is a medical step for a
  life-sustaining drug.
- **Sharing or borrowing prescription medicine** and **antibiotics without a prescription** are
  decision cards, not things to improvise: {{choice DC-15}} → 30-16; {{choice DC-16}} → 30-17

## Isolation with small children

- **You cannot isolate a baby or toddler from their carers**, and trying makes things worse.
- **You can** reduce spread with hand washing, cleaning, separate personal items, fresh air, and
  separating a sick adult where possible.
- **Assume everyone gets it** and plan for that: water, oral rehydration salts, easy food and fuel
  set out so a fully sick household can still function.
- **Every adult should be able to run every life-sustaining routine**, because the person who
  usually does it may be the one who is sick (→ 00-07).

## The long tail

- **Schools and childcare close** (→ 20-03). **Work changes or stops** (→ 19-08).
- **Supply chains stay disrupted after the worst is over.**
- **Health care backlogs last** (→ 12-11).
- **Mental strain is heavy and long** (→ 21-01, → 21-02, → 12-15). Plan for it on purpose.
- **Communities strain under this kind of stress** (→ 18-06), and community is also what gets
  people through it (→ 18-01).

## When the medicine is the problem and everything else works

A pandemic is the scenario most likely to cut a medicine supply **while the grid, water and roads
still work**:
- **You can still travel**, so the go/no-go decision (→ 01-02) may point toward **seeking supply**,
  not sheltering.
- **You can still communicate**, so clinicians, pharmacies and patient networks matter.
- **Money still works.**
- **The right response can be early, active and outward.** The instinct in a pandemic is to
  shelter; for a household whose central need is a medicine, that may be exactly wrong.

**If you do not have it:** soap and water replace almost every product on this page; a surgical
mask under a snug cloth one fits better than either alone; fresh air is free; oral rehydration
solution can be made from salt, sugar and safe water using the published WHO recipe on → 12-05; a
neighbourhood that runs errands for its sick households protects everyone (→ 18-01). **What you
cannot substitute is a life-sustaining medicine.**

## When it runs out

- **The supply that decides the outcome is medicine**, not food or fuel. Track days on hand and
  treat supply-chain news as an early trigger (→ 23-02, → 01-02).
- **Medicine is never rationed below what the condition needs.** That is a clinician's
  conversation, not a household decision (→ 12-11). With no clinician left, the labels' own
  missed-dose and stopping lines, printed on each condition page, are the guidance (→ 12-11,
  "When there is no prescriber").
- **Resupply is still possible in this scenario**: pharmacies, mail order, other regions,
  clinicians, community. **Act early, before scarcity sets in.**

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** sheltering in place works, provided the medicine supply holds. Use early warning
  to deepen it, reduce exposure, and keep hygiene strict.
- **B (moving):** in a pandemic, movement is usually to get medicine or care, not to relocate.
  Travel raises exposure; weigh it against what you are going for (→ 01-02).
- **C (rural or new site):** distance protects against spread, and worsens the supply and care
  problem. Weigh both honestly (→ 01-05).
