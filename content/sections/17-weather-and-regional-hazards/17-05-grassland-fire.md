# Grassland and wildland fire

**What this page is for.** Wildfire is not only a mountain and forest problem. Fire in dry grass,
brush and crops happens on the plains, in the desert, in the suburbs and in the woods, and grass
fire is often the fastest of all. This page covers how these fires behave, the weather that
drives them, protecting the house, your own household as the ignition source, and what to do when
a fire is coming. Smoke, evacuation zones and the clean room are on → 17-14.
{{#if is.wildfire_region}}**Your home county is rated 2 or 3 for wildfire in the region data.**
Do the "Do this now" list before the next dry season.{{else}}Your home county is rated lower for
wildfire, but a grid-down household running fires every day is its own risk; the household part
of this page applies to everyone.{{/if}}
Ratings in this binder are FEMA's National Risk Index for your county, on a 1-to-3 scale: they measure expected harm to people and property there, not how likely the hazard is (→ 29-10).

> **SAFETY.**
> - **You cannot outrun a wind-driven grass fire on foot.** It moves at the speed of the wind.
> - **Leave early.** Fire evacuation is a decision of minutes, not hours. Do not wait to see what
>   it does, and **do not go back for things**.
> - **A grid-down household starts fires:** cooking fires, wood heat, ash, lamps, generators,
>   saws. You are the most likely ignition source in your own emergency.
> - **Fuel is never stored or transferred near an open flame or a running appliance** (→ 30-01).

## Do this now

1. **Learn what a Red Flag Warning is and make sure your weather radio sounds for it** (→ 15-01).
   **Red flag means: no outdoor fire, no ash dumping, nothing that throws sparks.**
2. **Clear the first five feet around the house** of anything that burns, and **move the woodpile
   away from the house** (below). A woodpile against a wall is a wick.
3. **Set the ash rule and post it** (below).
4. **Make a fire evacuation plan** that works in minutes: bags, documents, medicines, people, pets
   (→ 17-14, → 19-01, → 22-03).
5. **Read the burn conditions off the sky and the radio, not off a notice:** dry grass plus low
   humidity plus wind means no open burning, whether or not anyone is still posting bans.

> **Before the collapse:** while counties and states post burn bans and fire restrictions, check
> them and obey them.

## How these fires behave

- **Speed.** Fire in cured (dead, dry) grass, pushed by wind, often moves faster than a person can
  run, and it speeds up going uphill.
- **A thin flame front.** A grass fire's front is often shallow and passes quickly. That is why
  **"the black", ground that has already burned**, is a refuge: fire does not come back over
  ground with nothing left to burn.
- **Spotting.** Wind carries embers ahead of the fire and starts new fires downwind, sometimes far
  ahead.
- **Wind shifts change everything at once.** A fire moving away can turn toward you.
- **Smoke** cuts visibility to nothing and harms lungs, especially children's (→ 17-14).
- **Houses mostly catch from embers**, not the flame front: in gutters, on decks, under eaves, in
  woodpiles, and in dry grass against the wall.
- **Forest and brush fires** burn hotter and longer than grass fires and can throw embers a long
  way; the same rules apply with more distance and more time.

## The weather that drives fire

**Red Flag Warning:** warm, dry, windy weather in which any fire spreads fast. **Fire Weather
Watch:** those conditions may come in the next day or two (NWS, Wildfire Safety; → 29-10). Watch
for:
- **Spring before green-up:** last year's dead grass, dry air, wind. Easy to underestimate.
- **Late summer and fall after the grass cures**, worst in drought (→ 17-07).
- **Any windy day with low humidity.**

**On a red flag day: no outdoor fire, no ash dumping, no sparks, no machines in dry grass.**

## Protecting the house

Fire agencies describe three zones around a house (USFA, Take Action; Ready.gov, Wildfires):

{{figure fig-defensible-space-zones}}

- **0 to 5 feet: nothing that burns.** Remove wood mulch, dead or dry plants, leaves and pine
  needles, including from the roof and gutters; trim branches that hang over the roof and chimney
  (USFA). No firewood or stored items against the walls; gravel or bare soil is best.
- **5 to 30 feet:** "lean, clean and green": dead branches removed, plants spaced apart and
  watered in dry summers, no low branches that let fire climb into trees (USFA).
- **Woodpiles at least 30 feet from the house**, on bare ground or gravel or in an enclosed shed
  (USFA).
- **30 to 100 feet:** fuel reduced by thinning and spacing plants and limbing up trees (USFA).
- **The house itself:** clean gutters (dry leaves in a gutter catch embers), screened vents, nothing
  stored under decks, nothing burnable on decks or against walls, propane cylinders moved away
  (→ 06-06).

Your state and county may have their own distances for your kind of vegetation; use the larger
number.

## Your own fire risk

| Source | Control |
|---|---|
| **Wood ash** | Metal can with a tight lid, on a non-burnable surface, away from buildings; **treat it as live for days**, and soak it with water before it touches soil, garden or compost. A can that feels cool outside can still hold live coals |
| **Outdoor cooking fire** (→ 07-01) | Cleared ground, contained, water and a shovel at hand, never on a red flag day, never left alone |
| **Chimney and stovepipe sparks** | Spark arrestor; clean flue (→ 06-09) |
| **Fuel** (→ 06-06) | Away from the house and from anything that sparks or burns |
| **Lamps and candles** (→ 10-02) | Contained, watched, never left alone. Flashlights instead in an outage where you can (USFA) |
| **Generator** (→ 09-04) | Hot exhaust lights dry grass. Run it on bare ground or pavement |
| **Saws, mowers, engines** | Sparks and hot exhaust in dry grass; work early in the day, with water at hand |
| **Burn piles and trash** (→ 13-03) | Never on a red flag day; cleared ring; water at hand; never in wind |

**Ash is worth saying twice.** Dumping "cold" ash on grass, in a plastic bin or against the house
has started a huge number of fires.

## When a fire is coming

- **Go early.** When you would leave home is a household decision written in advance:
  {{choice DC-19}} → 30-20
- **Medicines first, then documents and bags** (→ 01-07, → 19-01). **Children into car seats and
  pets into carriers are the slow steps; start them first.**
- **Drive away from the fire and across the wind**, not ahead of it along its path.
- **Caught in the open with no way out:** get to the black, or bare ground, a plowed field, a road
  cut, gravel, or water. Get low, cover bare skin, protect your airway. If phones work, call 911
  and give your location, but be aware that emergency response could be delayed or impossible
  (Ready.gov, Wildfires).

**Caught in a vehicle** (Fire Safe Marin, Are Vehicles Safe?). **Stay in the car: driving through
wildfire is dangerous, but much less dangerous than running from it on foot.** The steel body
protects you from flames, heat and embers.
1. **Drive slowly with headlights on**, avoid heavy smoke, and watch for other vehicles and people.
   Never abandon the vehicle in the middle of the road.
2. **If you have to stop, park away from heavy trees and brush.**
3. **Roll up the windows and close the air vents.**
4. **Headlights on, ignition off.**
5. **Get on the floor and cover up with a blanket or coat** (wool if you have it). Children out of
   car seats onto the floor with an adult over them.
6. **Stay in the vehicle until the main fire passes.** It will get hot, smoke and sparks may get
   in, air currents may rock the car, and the engine may stall and be hard to restart. Metal fuel
   tanks rarely explode. Then drive or walk out **into the black**.

**Smoke:** stay low, cover mouth and nose, and get children out of it first. **No mask on a child
under 2**; for them the protection is distance, so leave earlier (→ 17-14).

## When there is no fire department

In a long grid failure a house or grass fire is yours alone. That moves everything toward
**prevention**: defensible space, ash discipline and fire-safe habits become rules, not good
ideas.
- **Losing the house means losing everything in it**: food, water, fuel, medicine. Keep a go-bag
  and a second set of critical medical supplies somewhere other than the house if you can
  (→ 22-03).
- **Water for firefighting:** rain barrels, stock tanks, buckets staged around the property.
  {{#if has.pool}}Your pool is a large firefighting reserve; a pump and a long hose make it usable.{{/if}}
- **A fire extinguisher is for a small fire and a clear way out**; when the fire is bigger than a
  wastebasket, leave (→ 29-12).

**If you do not have it:** a shovel and a rake are the real grass-fire tools; wet burlap or a wool
blanket beats out small fires; **a mowed or disced strip of bare ground around buildings is the
classic plains firebreak** and costs only work; any metal can with a lid is an ash can, never
plastic or cardboard.

## When it runs out

- **Prevention does not run out**; it costs work, not supplies.
- **What runs out:** extinguishers (one use each; check gauges every year), masks, hose.
- **What wears down is discipline.** The tenth month of dumping ash is when someone gets careless.
  Keep the rule posted and the metal can where the ash goes.
- **With no fire department, a house fire is effectively unrecoverable.** That makes every
  prevention step worth more than it looks.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** in town the risk is mostly your own fires and your neighbours': cooking, wood
  heat, ash, lamps. Defensible space, ash discipline, extinguishers.
- **B (moving):** do not camp or cook in dry grass; clear a wide circle; never leave a fire; check
  the wind before lighting anything. Smoke also gives your position away (→ 14-03).
- **C (rural or new site):** grass and wildland fire become a main hazard. Defensible space, a
  firebreak, stored firefighting water and an evacuation plan in the first month (→ 01-04); fire
  risk belongs on the site checklist (→ 01-05).
