# Reading the sky: forecasting with no forecast

**What this page is for.** Every other page in this section assumes a warning reaches you. In a
long grid or internet failure it may not. Then **the sky, an analog barometer and a battery NOAA
Weather Radio** are the whole warning system. This page teaches the signs that matter most.

> **SAFETY.** Reading the sky buys **minutes to hours**, not days, and it gives false alarms.
> **Take the false alarms.** Going to shelter for a shelf cloud that blows through costs ten
> minutes; ignoring a wall cloud can cost everything. When a sign on this page fires, act on the
> matching page (→ 17-02, → 17-06, → 17-01) and argue about it afterward.

## Do this now

1. **Get an analog (aneroid) barometer** and hang it where you pass it every day (→ 22-01). Tap the
   glass gently before reading; the needle sticks. **Write the reading and the time on a card next
   to it twice a day.** A barometer is useless without its trend.
2. **Learn the wall cloud and shelf cloud difference below** until you can say it from memory.
3. **Practice on ordinary storms now**, while forecasts still exist to check yourself against
   (→ 24-02).
4. **Keep the weather radio's batteries as a protected reserve** (→ 15-01). NOAA Weather Radio
   transmitters reach you with no internet at all (NWS, NOAA Weather Radio).

## Tornado signs the storm shows you

- **Wall cloud or shelf cloud: the difference that matters** (SPC; NSSL):
  - A **wall cloud** is an **abrupt, isolated lowering** under the storm's **rain-free base**,
    usually at the **back** of the storm (in much of the central US, its southwest side), and
    often **rotating**. It marks the updraft, where tornadoes form. **Rotation you can watch is the
    alarm.**
  - A **shelf cloud** is a **long, low, wedge-shaped** cloud on the **leading edge** of the storm,
    often across the whole horizon. It marks the gust front of cold outflow. It looks terrifying
    and usually means **straight-line wind**, not a tornado. Still get inside.
  - **Memory aid:** shelf cloud = front of the storm, shaped like a shelf, coming at you. Wall
    cloud = back of the storm, hanging down like a wall, often turning.
- **A green sky is folklore-grade.** NSSL says scientists do not agree why the sky turns green,
  though it is often reported near hail; SPC lists no sky colour as a tornado sign.
- **Large hail, or a sudden calm:** SPC says neither is a reliable predictor of a tornado, but
  large hail does mean an unusually dangerous storm. The quiet is not the all-clear.
- **A roar that does not fade like thunder:** tornado.
- **At night:** this is what the radio is for. Lightning flickering in one part of the sky at least
  shows where the storm is.

## Derecho signature

A derecho (a long, wide windstorm; → 17-02) may show **a shelf cloud across the horizon** and an
**advancing wall of dust and rain** below it, then an abrupt **wind blast**. SPC is blunt about the
limits: shelf clouds are not always present, and because derechos usually move at 50 mph or more,
the signs appear on very short notice, often too short to act on (SPC, About Derechos). **When
you see them, take the tornado posture at once, without waiting for rotation**; the radio is what
gives real warning.

## Winter storm onset: a day's notice

The classic warm-front cloud sequence. High wispy cirrus is often the first cloud to appear ahead
of a low-pressure system (NOAA JetStream, The Four Core Types of Clouds):
1. **A halo** around the sun or moon (thin ice-crystal cloud, cirrostratus): moisture arriving high
   up. The Met Office says that when a sheet of cirrostratus covers the sky, a warm front is likely
   to bring persistent rain within a day (Met Office, High clouds). The faster the sky thickens,
   the sooner.
2. **High cloud thickens and lowers** until the sun is a dim smear behind grey: the weather is
   hours away, not days.
3. **The wind backs** and **the barometer falls steadily.**
4. **Temperature tells you which storm it is.** Rising toward freezing as precipitation starts
   means snow that can turn to **freezing rain**, the outage maker (→ 17-08). Falling hard behind a
   northwest wind shift means the back side: blowing snow and ground blizzards (→ 17-11).
5. **When step 2 is under way in winter, run the → 17-01 "before" list.** Being half a day early
   costs nothing.

## The barometer

An analog barometer needs no power and no internet. **Set it once** to the sea-level pressure read
out on the NOAA Weather Radio observation (the nearest airport's reading), not to a guessed number.
Your home sits at about **{{ region.home.elevation_ft }} ft**, and a barometer set without a real
reading at that height can be far off. **The number matters less than the trend.**
- **Falling more than about 1 millibar an hour:** a storm may be approaching. Read it every hour
  or two while it falls.
- **Falling faster than that, or falling for hours on end:** a stronger storm or a fast-deepening
  low. Full preparation, no travel.
- **Rising fast after a storm:** clearing, but in winter often wind and bitter cold behind it.
- The 1-millibar-an-hour figure is a **mariners' rule of thumb** (Barometer Rules), not an NWS
  product. Treat it as a reason to prepare, never as a reason to relax.

**Pressure and wind together** beat either alone:

| Barometer | Wind from | Read it as |
|---|---|---|
| Falling fast | East to southeast | A major low approaching |
| Falling fast | South to southwest, warm and humid | Severe thunderstorms possible today: watch the sky hourly |
| Steady or rising | West to northwest | Fair, or clearing |
| Rising fast | Northwest, in winter | Clearing, wind and plunging cold: blowing snow on open roads |

These are for the middle latitudes of the US, where weather generally moves west to east. Near
the coasts and in the mountains, local winds can change the pattern; learn yours by logging.

## Folk and animal signs: label them honestly

These only back up the signs above. **Never act on them alone; never let them overrule the table.**
- **"Red sky at night, red sky at morning"**: weakly true where weather moves west to east; wrong
  often.
- **Birds going quiet, animals bunching, insects vanishing:** often true, never reliable, never
  specific.
- **Chimney smoke hanging low, smells carrying far:** a rough low-pressure sign; the barometer says
  it better.
- **Aching joints, "the smell of rain":** real things, useless timing.
- **When a folk sign and the barometer agree, stop and read the sky carefully.**

## What the NOAA radio still gives you

The NWS transmitter network broadcasts on VHF with backup power at many sites, independent of phone
networks and the internet (→ 15-02, → 29-09):
- **Watches and warnings with the SAME alarm**, which wakes you.
- **Routine forecasts and observations**, including the pressure that sets your barometer.
- **If even the transmitters go quiet, this page is what is left.** That is why it is here.

**If you do not have it:** a watch or vehicle with a pressure sensor; for wind direction a flag,
smoke, a wet finger or moving trees. **What you cannot improvise is a practiced eye.** Practice on
storms now.

## When it runs out

- **Nothing here wears out** except radio batteries; protect them.
- **What fades is skill.** Keep logging the barometer in quiet weeks and check yourself against
  real storms every season.
- **The failure is alarm fatigue.** After three false alarms the fourth shelf cloud gets ignored.
  The response to a sign is cheap on purpose, so you can afford to take every alarm.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** the radio leads; the sky backs it up. Log the barometer daily so the habit
  exists before it matters.
- **B (moving):** this page and → 17-11 are the trip's weather service. Read the sky at every stop.
  A falling barometer or a darkening western sky is a reason to stop early, not drive faster
  (→ 01-03).
- **C (rural or new site):** sky reading becomes a daily working skill for field work, travel and
  fire risk (→ 17-05). Teach it to children as they grow.
