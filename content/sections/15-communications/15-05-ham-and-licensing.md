# Amateur (ham) radio: licensing and getting started now

**What this page is for.** Amateur radio ("ham" radio) is the only communications option in
this binder with real reach, beyond line of sight and across a region or the country, **without
any infrastructure at all**. It is also where the knowledge lives: antennas, propagation, 12 V
power and repair (→ 15-06, → 09-08). This is a "do it this month" page: the licence, the skills
and the local network of people are all available now, cheaply, and not later.

> **Before the collapse:** transmitting on amateur bands requires an FCC licence while the FCC
> operates (47 CFR Part 97). The entry-level **Technician** exam is one written test of 35
> questions and needs **no Morse code** (47 CFR 97.501, 97.503; ARRL, Getting Licensed). Licences
> run for ten years (47 CFR 97.25) and expire quietly:
> write the expiry date down and renew in good time; an FCC fee may apply, so check the current
> fee when you apply. Part 97 also says nothing in its rules stops an amateur station using any
> means at its disposal for essential communication about the immediate safety of life when
> normal communications are down, or a station in distress from using any means to get help
> (47 CFR 97.403, 97.405). **Listening needs no licence at all.**

## Do this now

1. **Study for and take the Technician exam.** A few evenings of study, one test session, one fee.
   **The single most valuable action in this section.** Having more than one licensed adult means
   one person is not a single point of failure.
2. **Find the local amateur radio club and its nets** (scheduled on-air meetings). In an
   emergency these are the people on the air with real information, and being known to them
   beforehand matters (→ 18-01).
3. **Buy a dual-band handheld** (inexpensive) and **use it**: talk through the local repeaters,
   check into a net, get comfortable.
4. **Write the local repeaters into the table below** and check them by listening. Repeaters come
   and go.
5. **Then consider General.** Technician covers local and regional work; **General opens most of
   the HF bands, where long-distance capability lives.**

## Why this matters more than any radio purchase

When cell networks, the internet and commercial broadcast all fail, **amateur radio keeps
working**, because it needs only a transmitter, an antenna and power, all of which a household can
supply (→ 09-02). In real disasters, amateur operators often provide the last working links,
running organised emergency nets and passing "health and welfare" messages (ARRL, What is Ham
Radio?).

**Three things you get:**

1. **Reach.** VHF and UHF (very and ultra high frequency) through repeaters cover a region; **HF
   (high frequency) covers hundreds to thousands of miles with no infrastructure at all.**
2. **Information.** Organised nets carry structured, first-hand reports (→ 15-04).
3. **Knowledge and people.** Antennas, propagation, power and repair, plus a local community of
   technically capable people who help each other (→ 18-01, → 18-03).

## The licence classes

| Class | What it opens | Exam |
|---|---|---|
| **Technician** | All amateur privileges above 30 MHz (VHF and UHF, local and regional repeaters), plus limited HF | Entry level, no code |
| **General** | **Most HF privileges**: long distance | A moderate step up |
| **Amateur Extra** | Full privileges | Hardest; not needed for anything in this binder |

Details: ARRL, Amateur Radio Licenses; the current Part 97 is the authority. **Technician now,
then General.**

## What you would use it for

- **Regional awareness**: nets during a blizzard, an ice storm, a flood or a wider emergency. The
  main one (→ 15-04).
- **Reaching family or contacts outside the region** when nothing else works (→ 15-03).
- **Keeping in touch with a rural site** at a distance no other service covers (→ 01-04).
- **Being useful to a wider community**: an amateur station in a neighbourhood is a real
  contribution (→ 18-01).
- **Listening**, which is legal unlicensed and often the point (→ 15-04).

## Equipment, in a sensible order

1. **A dual-band VHF/UHF handheld.** Inexpensive, works on local repeaters. **Buy a better antenna
   for it at once**: the supplied antennas are poor (→ 15-06).
2. **A better antenna**: a roll-up J-pole or a magnetic-mount antenna on the vehicle transforms a
   handheld.
3. **A mobile or base VHF/UHF radio** on **12 V** from a battery bank (→ 09-08): more power, a
   better receiver.
4. **An HF radio (with General)** and a **wire antenna** (→ 15-06). A wire dipole in a tree and a
   100-watt radio reach remarkably far.
5. **Power:** 12 V direct from a solar battery bank, no inverter (→ 09-08). Radios are an ideal
   solar load: modest, DC-native and used in short spells.
6. **Spares:** coaxial cable, connectors, wire, fuses, a spare handheld (→ 15-06).

## The skills that outlast the equipment

- **Antennas**: how they work, how to build one from wire, why height matters (→ 15-06).
- **Propagation**: why the same radio reaches 5 miles at noon and 500 at night.
- **12 V power and wiring**, directly useful for the power section (→ 09-08).
- **Repair and improvisation**: amateurs fix and build things, which a long emergency rewards.
- **Operating discipline**: passing accurate information, briefly, under pressure. Useful even off
  the air.

## How to operate

- **Identify with your call sign.** It is how nets know who is talking, and it gets you answered.
- **Plain language, no codes or encryption.** That is what a net can relay, and Part 97 requires
  it while it is enforced.
- **Skill and courtesy matter.** Amateur radio is a community with norms; a competent, known
  operator is the one who gets help in a crisis.
- **Every transmission is an announcement** (→ 14-03). Whether to transmit is a security
  decision, not only a rules question.

## Security

**Amateur transmissions are unencrypted by law, and a call sign is publicly linked to the licence
address.** For a household that values discretion that is a real consideration (→ 14-03). What
helps: **listen far more than you transmit**, keep transmissions practical and bland, and be
deliberate about what you say about your household. **A post office box as the licence mailing
address is a legitimate and common choice.**

**If you do not have it:**

- **Radios** from estate sales, swap meets ("hamfests"), clubs and other operators. The used
  amateur market is large and cheap, and older gear is often easier to repair.
- **Antennas**: wire, coax and knowledge build excellent antennas for almost nothing (→ 15-06).
- **Power:** a battery bank, a vehicle battery or a small solar panel (→ 09-02).
- **Knowledge:** free study material and a local club full of people who will teach you.
- **What you cannot scavenge is the licence and the practice.** Both take time, and only now
  works.

## When it runs out

- **Little here runs out.** Radios last decades, antennas are wire, and 12 V power comes from
  solar (→ 09-02).
- **What fails:** connectors corrode, cable degrades in sun, antennas come down in ice storms,
  handhelds get dropped. **Stock cable, connectors, wire and a spare radio.**
- **Power is the consumable, and it is small** (→ 09-01).
- **Knowledge gains value over time.** Someone who understands antennas and DC power can keep a
  station running from salvage and **build one for someone else** (→ 18-03).
- **The network of people fades if you are not part of it.** In an emergency you do not want to
  be a stranger with a radio; you want to be someone the local operators know.

## By phase

- **A (at home):** get licensed, get on the local repeaters, join a net, learn the frequencies,
  and **be known before anything happens.** In town a handheld and a modest antenna cover a lot.
- **B (on the move):** the handheld travels; a magnetic-mount antenna on the vehicle makes it far
  more useful (→ 15-09).
- **C (rural or a new place):** **this is where amateur radio becomes essential and works best**:
  real distances, and space and height for proper antennas (→ 15-06). A wire dipole between two
  trees and a 12 V solar bank make a capable long-distance station, which may be the household's
  only link to the wider world.
