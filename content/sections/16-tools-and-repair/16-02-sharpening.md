# Sharpening knives, axes and saws

**What this page is for.** How to put an edge back on every cutting tool you own, with a file, a
stone and a strip of leather. Sharpening costs little, takes an afternoon to learn, and keeps
every edge tool working for years. When you cannot buy a new axe, restoring the one you have is
what "tools last forever" really means.

> **CAUTION. A dull tool is more dangerous than a sharp one.** A dull edge does not bite. It
> skates and glances off, it needs force, and it goes where you did not intend with your weight
> behind it. OSHA says dull tools cause more hazards than sharp ones (OSHA, Hand and Power Tools)
> (→ 12-02). Learn to sharpen now, on tools you do not depend on, before you have to do it by
> lamplight with cold hands (→ 24-01).

## Do this now

1. **Buy files and stones:** a mill bastard file (a common flat file, a hand-length or longer) for axes,
   chainsaw files in the exact size for your chain, and sharpening stones in a coarse and a fine
   grit.
2. **Sharpen everything you own this month:** kitchen knives, the axe, the hatchet, the shovel,
   the loppers. You will find half of it is dull, and you will build the skill.
3. **Learn the burr** (next section). It is most of what sharpening is.
4. **Get a strop**, or make one from an old leather belt, and use it. Stropping keeps an edge
   sharp much longer and removes almost no metal.

## The one idea: the burr

{{figure fig-burr-and-bevel}}

An edge is the line where two sloped faces (bevels) meet. **You sharpen by grinding one side
until a tiny wire of metal, the burr, curls over along the whole opposite side.** Then you do the
other side. Then you remove the burr.

- **Feel for the burr** by drawing a fingertip *across* the edge, from the thick back of the
  blade outward. Never along the edge. A burr feels like a slight catch.
- **A burr along the whole length means you have reached the edge everywhere.** No burr means you
  have only polished the slope, not sharpened.
- **Then refine on a finer stone, and strop** to remove the burr and straighten the edge.

Everything else (angles, stones, technique) is refinement of that one idea.

## Angles, roughly

The angle is measured on one face, from the flat of the blade. **The best guide is the angle the
tool already has:** match it, and the maker's instructions where they give one.

| Tool | Edge | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Kitchen or fillet knife | The thinnest | Sharpest; for slicing, not chopping |
| Butchering knife | Thin, a little sturdier | Sharp but durable (→ 05-09) |
| General outdoor knife | Medium | Compromise |
| Axe or hatchet | Thicker, with a rounded ("convex") profile | Must survive impact; a thin edge chips (→ 16-06) |
| Machete or brush hook | Thicker | Impact tool |
| Chisel or plane blade | Medium, one bevel, back kept flat | Fine woodwork |
| Scissors and shears | The factory angle, bevelled side only | Never sharpen the flat inner side |

**Thinner cuts better; thicker lasts longer.** An axe sharpened like a kitchen knife chips on the
first knot.

## Sharpening a knife

1. **Coarse stone** if the edge is chipped or very dull. If it is only tired, start on the fine
   stone.
2. **Hold one steady angle.** This is the hard part. A clip-on guide helps while you learn.
3. **Work one side until a burr forms along the whole edge.**
4. **Turn the knife over and repeat** until the burr forms on the first side.
5. **Finer stone**, both sides, lighter pressure.
6. **Strop:** draw the blade along leather with the edge trailing (spine first), to remove the
   burr.
7. **Test** by slicing a sheet of paper. **Never test by running your thumb across the edge.**

**Water or oil on the stone** carries away the metal grit so the stone keeps cutting. Use what
the stone was made for.

## Sharpening an axe

{{figure fig-axe-convex-filing}}

1. **Secure the axe** in a vise, or braced firmly. **Never hold it loose in one hand.**
2. **File first** with a mill bastard file, pushing *into* the edge. A file cuts only on the push
   stroke. Work from the middle of the edge outward, on both sides.
3. **Keep the rounded profile.** An axe edge should be slightly curved in cross-section, not a
   flat bevel. Blend the bevel back a couple of inches into the side of the head.
4. **Then a stone**, in small circles, both sides.
5. **Strop or hone** to finish.
6. **Wear gloves and file away from your hand.** A file that slips off an axe edge takes your
   knuckles with it.
7. **Grind off any mushrooming** on the striking end of a maul or wedge. OSHA says mushroomed heads
   can shatter on impact and send sharp fragments flying (OSHA, Hand and Power Tools), a classic
   eye injury.

A properly sharpened axe cuts far more wood per swing, which matters when you are cutting a
winter's firewood by hand (→ 06-04).

## Saws

- **Bow saw blades are hardened and disposable.** You cannot usefully resharpen them. **Stock many
  spare blades.**
- **Chainsaw chains are not disposable.** They are sharpened with a round file many times over
  their life, a light touch-up whenever the chain starts making dust instead of chips. A chain is
  used up only when the teeth are filed back to the wear marks.
- **Traditional hand saws can be sharpened and "set"** with a saw file and a saw set (a plier-like
  tool). This is a learnable skill, and it is why an old hand saw is a better long-term buy than
  a disposable-blade saw.
- **Set** means the teeth are bent slightly left and right in turn, so the cut is wider than the
  blade and the blade does not jam. A saw with no set binds and burns.

## Chainsaw chains

**The file size, the filing angles and the depth-gauge setting come from your saw's and chain's
manufacturer and the Extension guides cited here, not from this page.** Getting them wrong makes
kickback more likely. Kickback is the saw's tip catching and throwing the bar up and back toward
you, one of the main ways chainsaws injure people (University of Missouri Extension, Operating a
Chain Saw Safely; → 16-06).

- **Sharpen often.** Always run a sharp chain: it cuts better and reduces the fatigue that leads to
  accidents (University of Missouri Extension, Selecting and Maintaining a Chain Saw). Wear gloves or
  lay a heavy cloth over the chain while filing (MU Extension).
- **A sharp chain pulls itself into the wood and throws chips. A dull chain makes fine dust and
  you have to push.** That is the test.
- **Depth gauges** (the small humps in front of each cutting tooth, also called rakers) must be
  filed down too, or the chain stops cutting even with sharp teeth. MU Extension says chain makers
  recommend lowering them every third filing (MU Extension, Selecting and Maintaining a Chain
  Saw).
- **Stock the right size files and a depth-gauge tool** (University of Missouri Extension,
  Selecting and Maintaining a Chain Saw). They are small, cheap and specific to your chain. Write
  the size on your tool inventory (→ 16-01).

## Other things worth sharpening

- **Shovels and spades:** a sharpened shovel cuts sod and roots far better.
- **Hoes** (→ 05-01): a sharp hoe makes weeding a fraction of the work.
- **Loppers, pruners and shears** (→ 05-13): a clean cut is better for the plant too.
- **Scissors and kitchen knives:** daily comfort and safety.
- **Drill bits and auger bits** can be filed back to life.
- **A scythe**, if you have one, needs constant honing and "peening" (hammering the edge thin).
  A real skill worth learning for a rural move.

## Gear

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mill bastard file with a handle | The axe file; general use | Buy several |
| Chainsaw files in your chain's size, and a depth-gauge tool | Chain-specific | Stock many; they wear |
| Sharpening stones, coarse and fine | Knives, chisels, plane blades | Diamond plates last longest |
| Strop (leather and polishing compound) | Keeps an edge without removing metal | An old belt works |
| Metal files: flat, round, half-round, triangular | Tools, hardware, repairs | → 16-08 |
| Saw file and saw set | Sharpen a hand saw indefinitely | The long-term choice |
| **Spare bow saw blades, many** | Cannot be resharpened | Over-stock |
| Bench vise | Holds work safely while filing | → 16-01 |
| Gloves and eye protection | Filing throws metal; edges cut | Every time |
| Honing guide (optional) | Steady angle while learning | Helps most on chisels |

**Substitutes if you have nothing:**
- **Stone:** a flat, fine-grained river stone (people sharpened on them for thousands of years);
  the rough unglazed ring on the bottom of a ceramic mug as a fine hone; a smooth concrete edge as
  a coarse stone.
- **Strop:** a leather belt, a boot tongue, a piece of cardboard. Polishing compound helps but is
  not needed.
- **Wet-and-dry sandpaper on a flat surface** (glass or a tile) is an excellent sharpening system.
  Stock a range of grits.
- **Files** can be salvaged from any shop or farm. A file too worn to cut can be ground into a
  knife or chisel (→ 16-08).
- **What you cannot improvise:** a chainsaw file of the right diameter, and hardened saw blades.
  Buy those now.

## When it runs out

- **Files and stones wear out.** Files dull after a lot of steel; stones wear hollow. Both are
  cheap now. Stock several and add them to → 23-02.
- **Saw blades are the real limit** (→ 06-04, → 16-06). Either stock many, or own a traditional
  hand saw and a saw file. For a plan measured in years, the traditional saw is the sturdier
  choice even though it is slower.
- **Strop often, sharpen rarely.** Stropping removes almost no metal. Heavy sharpening uses up
  the tool. A knife stropped daily may not need a stone for months.
- **The skill is the resource.** Someone who can sharpen keeps every edge in a neighborhood
  working, and that is a service people will trade for (→ 18-03).

## By phase

- **A, at home:** learn now, on your own tools, in daylight. Stock files, stones and blades.
- **B, on the move:** a small file and a pocket stone weigh almost nothing and keep a knife and a
  folding saw working. The knife is the tool you use most.
- **C, settled:** constant edge work: axes, saws, knives, shovels, butchering (→ 05-09). Set up a
  permanent sharpening station with a vise, files and stones. A hand-crank grinder is an
  excellent tool that needs no power.
