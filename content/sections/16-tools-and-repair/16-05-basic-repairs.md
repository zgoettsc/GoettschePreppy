# Improvised repairs: tape, wire, glue, patch

**What this page is for.** Fixing broken things with what is on hand, and knowing which things
must never be fixed that way. With no store, "repair it" replaces "replace it" for good, and the
ability to fix things from a shelf of tape, wire and glue is one of the most valuable skills in a
long emergency.

> **CAUTION. Know what you must NOT improvise.** Some repairs kill people when they fail:
> - **Fuel lines and fuel containers** (fire, → 06-06).
> - **Anything that carries smoke or exhaust:** stovepipe, chimney, vent connectors. A leak lets
>   out carbon monoxide, a gas you cannot see or smell that kills in minutes (→ 08-02, → 29-03).
>   A taped stovepipe joint is a poisoning waiting to happen.
> - **Pressure vessels and propane or gas fittings.**
> - **Beams, joists and posts holding up a roof or floor.**
> - **Brakes and steering on a vehicle.**
> - **Electrical wiring inside walls** (→ 09-08, → 29-04).
>
> Those get a proper repair or they are taken out of service. Everything else is fair game.

## Do this now

1. **Build a repair shelf** and stock it: tapes, glues, wire, fasteners, patches, sealants. All
   cheap now.
2. **Write the date of purchase on every glue.** Two-part epoxy, super glue and construction
   adhesive all age. Rotate them.
3. **Fix three broken things this month** that you have been putting off. Repair is a skill, and
   skills need practice.
4. **Start a parts bin and a materials pile:** screws, hinges, brackets, wire, scrap wood, sheet
   metal, fabric, anything salvaged from what you throw out (→ 22-02).

## Three questions before any repair

1. **What happens if this repair fails?** If the answer involves fire, carbon monoxide, a fall or
   a vehicle at speed, do not improvise.
2. **What kind of force is on it?** Pulling (tension), squeezing (compression), sliding
   (shear) or shaking (vibration)? **Vibration breaks glued joints and loosens fasteners.**
   Pulling needs mechanical fastening. Squeezing is forgiving.
3. **Is this temporary or permanent?** Say it out loud. A temporary repair that becomes
   permanent by neglect is how things fail badly later. **Mark temporary repairs with tape and
   write them on a list.**

## Glues, and what each is for

Read every product's label for the cure time and the ventilation it needs (→ 29-14).

| Glue | Best for | Fails at |
|---|---|---|
| **Two-part epoxy** | The strongest general repair: metal, wood, ceramic, filling gaps | Heat; flexing; sunlight over time |
| **Super glue (cyanoacrylate)** | Small, tight, rigid joints; with baking soda as a hard filler | Impact; gaps; damp over time. **Reacts hot with cotton and wool** |
| **Construction adhesive** | Wood, subfloor, large surfaces | Small precise parts |
| **Contact cement** | Boot soles, laminates, large flat bonds. **Coat both sides, wait until dry to the touch (about 10 to 20 minutes), then press together once, hard. No clamping; it grabs on contact** | Gaps; oily surfaces; pressing it while still wet (a weak bond that lets go in the cold) |
| **Flexible urethane shoe adhesive** | Boots, gaskets, gear that bends | Slow cure |
| **PVC cement** | Rigid plastic pipe of that type | Only that plastic |
| **Silicone sealant** | Sealing gaps against water | Any load |
| **Steel-filled epoxy putty** | Metal repair, filling stripped threads and gaps | Heat; high pressure |
| **Wood glue or hide glue** | Wood joints; hide glue can be made and undone with heat | Damp |
| **Pine pitch, powdered charcoal and a little fat** | Traditional glue you can make | Heat; only modest strength |

**Super glue plus baking soda.** Put down a drop of glue, dust it with baking soda, repeat. It
hardens instantly into a hard filler you can file and drill. Good for filling holes and rebuilding
broken plastic tabs. **The fill gets hot for a few seconds: set the part down, do not hold it,
and keep the fumes out of your eyes.**

## Tapes, and their real limits

| Tape | Good for | The truth |
|---|---|---|
| Duct tape | Fast temporary anything | Lets go with heat, cold, sunlight and time. **Never on skin** |
| Heavy cloth tape | Stronger duct tape | Same limits, better glue |
| **Self-fusing (self-amalgamating) silicone or rubber tape** | Sealing and waterproofing; it fuses to itself, not to the object | Excellent for radio cable joints (→ 15-06), hoses, handles |
| Electrical tape | Insulating, wrapping | Unwinds with heat and time |
| Gaffer tape | Temporary; peels off cleanly | Not waterproof |
| **Ripstop nylon repair tape** | Tarps, tents, jackets, sleeping bags | Durable; keep a roll in every kit |
| Aluminium foil tape | Heating ducts | The tape for duct joints, **but never a chimney or stovepipe repair** (→ 08-02) |
| PTFE thread tape | Threaded pipe joints | Seals; adds no strength |

**Duct tape is not a repair, it is a delay.** Use it, then do the real fix.

## Mechanical fixes: the ones that last

Mechanical repairs outlast glued ones, especially with shaking, cold and load.

- **Baling or mechanic's wire.** Twisted tight with pliers, ends tucked in, it holds things
  together indefinitely. The most underrated repair material there is (→ 16-07).
- **A bolt right through, with washers on both sides**, beats a glued joint every time.
- **Screws into a backing block** spread the load into sound material.
- **Sister boards:** a broken wooden piece gets a new board bolted alongside it (→ 16-08).
- **Hose clamps** (screw-tightened metal bands) hold and join round things. Stock many sizes.
- **Cotter pins and safety wire** stop things shaking loose.
- **Pop rivets:** joins sheet metal from one side. A rivet gun is cheap and very useful.
- **Lashing** (→ 16-03): for poles, a good lashing is stronger and more forgiving than most
  hardware.
- **Rawhide:** wrapped wet and left to dry, it shrinks and grips like a clamp. Excellent for tool
  handles (→ 16-08).

## The repairs you will actually face

| Broken | How |
|---|---|
| Tool handle | Replace or re-hang it (→ 16-08); wedge tight. Wire and epoxy a crack only as a stopgap |
| Boot sole coming off | Clean and roughen both faces. **Urethane shoe glue:** apply, press, clamp or weight for 24 hours. **Contact cement:** coat both faces, let dry to the touch, press once hard, no clamp. Follow the label (→ 11-06) |
| Tarp or tent tear | Ripstop tape both sides now; sew and seal later (→ 16-04) |
| Leaking container (water, not fuel) | Epoxy putty, silicone, or a gasket cut from rubber sheet |
| Broken plastic part | Super glue and baking soda; epoxy; a metal splint; or make a metal replacement (→ 16-08) |
| Stripped screw hole in wood | Pack it with wooden matchsticks or toothpicks and glue, let dry, drive the screw again |
| Stripped thread in metal | Fill with epoxy putty and re-cut; a thread insert; or go up one size |
| Broken zipper | → 16-04, → 11-07 |
| Cracked wooden furniture | Glue and clamp; sister a board; bolt through |
| Water hose or pipe leak | Self-fusing tape and a hose clamp. **Never on gas or fuel lines.** Burst pipes: → 16-12 |
| Broken window | Plastic sheet and tape for now; plywood is warmer (→ 08-03, → 16-12) |

## Working safely

- **Ventilate for glues and solvents.** The vapours are toxic and many are flammable. Read the
  label.
- **Never near an open flame or a running stove or heater.** Many glues and solvents catch fire,
  and a wood stove is an ignition source (→ 06-06).
- **Eye protection** for glue, wire ends and springs (→ 16-01).
- **Nitrile gloves, never cotton, around super glue.** Epoxy and super glue stick to skin
  instantly, and **super glue soaking into cotton or wool (a glove, a rag, a sleeve) reacts fast
  and hot enough to smoke and burn.** Never wipe super glue with a cotton rag.
- **Tetanus:** sharp wire and metal edges cause puncture wounds. Keep vaccinations current
  (CDC, About Tetanus; → 12-04).
- **Children away from glues and solvents**, which are a poisoning and eye hazard; store them
  locked (→ 20-02).
- **Never hide a weakness.** A repair you can see is safer than one you cannot.

## Gear

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Two-part epoxy and steel-filled epoxy putty** | The strongest general repairs | Date and rotate |
| **Super glue and baking soda** | Instant filler; many uses | Stock several small tubes |
| Contact cement and urethane shoe glue | Boots, flexible repairs | → 11-06 |
| Construction adhesive, wood glue, silicone | Wood, sealing | |
| **Baling or mechanic's wire, a lot** | Mechanical repairs outlast glue | The most underrated item |
| **Self-fusing tape** | Sealing, waterproofing, cable joints | → 15-06 |
| Duct tape, cloth tape, electrical tape, **ripstop tape**, foil tape | Different jobs | Ripstop in every kit |
| **Hose clamps, assorted** | Clamping and joining | Endlessly useful |
| Pop rivet gun and rivets | Sheet metal and hardware | Cheap |
| Screws, bolts, nuts, washers, nails | → 16-07 | Deep stock |
| Cotter pins, safety wire, cable ties | Stop things shaking loose | Cable ties weaken in sunlight |
| Rubber sheet or old inner tubes | Gaskets, straps, patches | Free from a bike shop |
| Clamps of several types | You always want more | → 16-01 |
| Materials pile and parts bin | Raw material for everything here | Organized enough to find things |

**Substitutes:**
- **Wire:** coat hangers, fencing, electrical cable.
- **Rubber:** inner tubes make gaskets, straps, bindings and rubber bands; also tyres, hoses, shoe
  soles.
- **Sheet metal:** cans, roof flashing, ductwork, appliance panels (→ 16-08).
- **Fasteners:** from anything being thrown out (→ 22-02).
- **Glue:** pine pitch with charcoal and a little fat; hide glue from hides and hooves (→ 05-09).
- **Clamping:** a loop of rope twisted tight with a stick (a "Spanish windlass"), wedges, weight,
  drying rawhide.
- **What you cannot make: modern epoxy.** Stock it and rotate it.

## When it runs out

- **Glues run out and expire; wire and fasteners barely do.** So stock wire, fasteners and
  hardware heavily (→ 16-07), and treat glue as precious.
- **Rotate glues every year** at the annual review (→ 25-10). Buy fresh rather than hoarding old.
- **Use mechanical repairs where you can** and save glue for what truly needs it. Repair early and
  small.
- **Resupply:** every abandoned garage, shed and workshop is full of these materials (→ 22-02).
  Hardware and fasteners are good barter goods (→ 18-02).
- **The real resource is the habit.** A household that fixes by default uses a fraction of the
  materials and keeps working far longer. Build the habit now, while replacing is still possible
  (→ 23-05).

## By phase

- **A, at home:** build the repair shelf, fix things, start the parts bin. Practise on things that
  do not matter.
- **B, on the move:** a tiny kit: ripstop tape, a few feet of wire, cable ties, super glue, needle
  and thread (→ 16-04), a multi-tool. Field repairs only need to reach the next stop.
- **C, settled:** repair becomes the default and the workshop becomes real infrastructure: a vise,
  an anvil, a materials pile, a parts bin (→ 16-01, → 16-08). Being the household that can fix
  things is a real position of value (→ 18-03).
