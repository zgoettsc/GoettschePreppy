# Fasteners, wire and hardware to stock

**What this page is for.** The screws, nails, bolts, wire and small hardware that every other
repair in this binder depends on. It is the dullest page in the binder and one of the most quietly
important. Fasteners cost almost nothing, never expire, and nobody stocks enough. Running out of
the right screw stops a repair dead. They are also among the best barter goods there are (→ 18-02).

> **Two cautions.** **Do not use salvaged bolts of unknown strength for anything that holds weight
> or holds people.** A bolt that looks fine can be the wrong grade, and you cannot tell by looking.
> **Rusty metal punctures carry a tetanus risk** (CDC, About Tetanus): keep every household
> member's vaccination current (→ 12-04).

## Do this now

1. **Buy assorted fastener kits** (screws, bolts, nuts, washers, nails in many sizes) **plus bulk
   boxes of the sizes you use most.**
2. **Buy wire:** mechanic's or baling wire, galvanized fence wire, stainless safety wire. Wire is
   the most underrated repair material there is (→ 16-05).
3. **Buy hardware:** hinges, brackets, hose clamps, eye bolts, screw eyes, hooks, chain,
   turnbuckles, springs.
4. **Set up sorted, labeled storage** so any size can be found in seconds. Unsorted hardware is
   nearly useless.
5. **Start the salvage habit:** strip fasteners and hardware from anything you throw away (→ 22-02).

## What to stock

**Screws: the most-used fastener**
- Wood or construction screws in #6, #8 and #10 thickness, from 3/4 inch to 4 inches long. Buy the
  common sizes by the pound.
- Coated deck screws for anything outdoors.
- Drywall screws are cheap and everywhere **but brittle**: fine for light work, not for anything
  structural or under sideways load.
- Sheet-metal (self-tapping) screws for metal, roofing and ductwork.
- Machine screws and nuts in common sizes.
- Lag screws (big, hex-headed wood screws) for heavy timber.

**Nails**
- Common nails from 6d to 16d ("d", or "penny", is the old size system: bigger number, longer nail),
  framing nails, finish nails, roofing nails, masonry nails.
- **Nails are cheaper than screws and hold better against sideways load; screws hold better against
  being pulled straight out.** Stock both.

{{figure fig-nail-vs-screw-shear}}

**Bolts, nuts and washers**
- Carriage bolts and hex bolts, 1/4 inch through 1/2 inch, in a range of lengths.
- Matching nuts, plus nylon-insert lock nuts ("nylock"), which do not shake loose.
- **Washers, flat and lock, in every size.** They are the most commonly missing item, and they
  matter: they spread load and stop a bolt head pulling through.
- Threaded rod in a few lengths. Cut it to make any bolt you need.
- **Both inch (SAE) and metric**, because you will be repairing things of unknown origin.

**Wire**

| Type | Use |
|---|---|
| **Mechanic's or baling wire** (soft, dark annealed steel) | General repair. Twist it and forget it. Buy a big roll |
| Galvanized fence wire | Outdoors; fencing, garden, structures |
| Stainless safety wire | Fine, strong, rust-free; locking bolts so they cannot turn |
| Copper wire | Electrical work (→ 09-08), and soft enough for delicate binding |
| **Hardware cloth** (stiff welded steel mesh) | Rodent-proofing (→ 04-11, → 13-03), tree guards (→ 05-13), screens, sifting |
| Chicken wire | Fencing, plant protection, compost bins |
| Smooth high-tensile wire | Fencing on rural land |

**Hardware**
- Hinges in several sizes, hasps and latches, brackets, angle iron, corner braces, flat mending
  plates.
- **Hose clamps in a full range of sizes.** Endlessly useful (→ 16-05).
- Eye bolts, screw eyes, hooks, S-hooks, shackles, quick links.
- Light and medium chain, and turnbuckles (for tightening a wire or cable).
- Springs, cotter pins, clevis pins, R-clips.
- Casters and wheels. Moving heavy things without a vehicle matters more than you would think.
- Pulleys (→ 16-03).
- Cable ties in several sizes: fast and useful, but **sunlight and cold make them brittle.** Not a
  permanent outdoor fastener.

**Consumables that live with the hardware**
- Sandpaper in a range of grits, steel wool (also stuffs rodent holes), wire brushes, penetrating
  oil, thread-locking liquid, anti-seize paste, grease and machine oil, rust converter, primer and
  paint for exposed steel.

## The details that matter

- **Dissimilar metals corrode each other** when wet. Do not use bare steel fasteners in aluminum,
  or in pressure-treated lumber, which eats plain steel fast. **Use hot-dip galvanized or stainless
  fasteners outdoors.**
- **Drill a pilot hole in hardwood and near board ends**, or the wood splits, especially old, dry
  or frozen lumber.
- **Nails bend; screws snap.** For a joint carrying sideways load, use nails or bolts. For holding
  against pull-out, use screws.
- **Anything that vibrates** gets lock washers, nylock nuts, thread locker or safety wire.
- **On a rusted bolt, penetrating oil and patience beat force**, and heat helps a great deal
  (→ 16-08).
- **Do not over-tighten.** A stripped thread or snapped bolt turns a five-minute job into an hour.
- **Old painted hardware and wood may carry lead paint.** Do not sand or burn it indoors, and keep
  children away from the dust (CDC, About Lead in the Home).

## Storage that makes it usable

- **Labeled, sorted, visible:** jars, parts bins or compartment boxes.
- **Dry.** Rust destroys fasteners in storage. Use sealed containers with a desiccant packet (or a
  little rice).
- **Keep salvage apart from good stock** so an unknown bolt never ends up where it matters.
- **A small grab bin** of mixed common fasteners near the work area for quick jobs.

## As barter goods

Fasteners and hardware are close to ideal trade items (→ 18-02): everyone needs them, they keep
forever, they are compact, and they divide easily into small trades. Each piece is worth little,
which makes them low-risk to trade compared with food, medicine or fuel, because trading them does
not advertise that you have a stockpile (→ 14-03). A coffee can of assorted screws is a valuable
thing to have on a shelf.

**Salvage:** everything on this page is easy to salvage (→ 22-02): appliances, furniture, vehicles,
pallets, decks, fence lines, farm buildings. Coat hangers are the classic improvised wire. You can
also **make** fasteners: a nail cut and re-headed, twisted wire, a wooden peg driven through a
drilled hole (the traditional "treenail" joint, → 16-08), rawhide that shrinks as it dries.
**Do not substitute** graded structural bolts, or anything holding weight over people.

## When it runs out

- **They never expire**, so the only way to run out is not buying enough. Stock deeply now.
- **Track it as "do I still have the common sizes"**, and restock the moment a bin runs low
  (→ 22-04, → 23-02).
- **Salvage before buying, and before improvising.** Straighten and reuse nails; it works.
- **Substitute:** wire (→ 16-05), lashing (→ 16-03), wooden pegs, rawhide, glue. A household that
  knows lashings and pegs needs far less hardware.
- **Over-stocking on purpose is sensible**: surplus screws become things you need, by trade.

## By phase

- **A, at home:** buy and sort now, while hardware stores exist. One of the best returns per dollar
  in the acquisition plan (→ 22-07).
- **B, on the move:** almost none of it travels; it is heavy. Cable ties, some wire and a few screws
  in the repair kit (→ 22-03).
- **C, settled:** very heavy use: building, fencing, animal housing, gates. Bring what you can and
  plan salvage for the rest. Old farm buildings are often full of usable hardware, and an old fence
  line is itself a wire supply.
