# Trailer maintenance and repair

**What this page is for.** Keeping a small utility or enclosed trailer safe to tow: the walkaround
before every trip, the wheel bearings that cause the worst failures, trailer tires, lights, and the
hitch. Nothing on a trailer is exotic, and all of it can be checked in ten minutes. This page is
the ten minutes.

{{#if has.trailer}}**You own a trailer.** Fill in the record block below the day you read this.
**Vehicles that can tow:** {{#each vehicles as v}}{{v.type_text}} (can tow: {{v.can_tow}}); {{/each}}{{else}}**You have
no trailer recorded.** Keep this page for a trailer you borrow, buy or find; read it before towing
anything.{{/if}}

> **SAFETY. A wheel bearing that fails at highway speed can lock the wheel, throw it off, or start a
> fire.** Most small trailers have **one axle: no second axle to limp on.** The two checks that
> prevent this are the bearing service (yearly) and the **hot-hub check at every stop** (below).
>
> **Never tow with an unlatched or unpinned coupler, or without both safety chains crossed under
> the tongue** (NHTSA, Trailer Safety). A trailer that comes loose crosses into oncoming traffic.
>
> **Never let anyone ride in a trailer.**
>
> **Your trailer's own axle maker and tire sidewall govern** where they differ from this page.

## Do this now

1. **Fill in the record block** (bottom of the page). A used trailer's history is unknown until you
   write it down. The bearing service date and the tire date codes decide everything else.

{{photo trailer-plate}}

2. **Do the full walkaround once in the driveway**, slowly, with this page in hand. After that it
   takes five minutes.
3. **Have the bearings inspected and repacked now** unless there is paper proof of a recent
   service. On a used trailer, "unknown" means "due". Watch it done, and ask the shop the axle brand
   and whether the hubs have grease fittings.

{{photo trailer-hubs}}

4. **Buy a mounted spare** (a trailer tire on a wheel with the right bolt pattern, plus a carrier or
   strap point) (→ 26-09). It turns a tire failure from abandoning the trailer into a 20-minute
   delay.
5. **Buy a grease gun, a tube of marine wheel-bearing grease, and dielectric grease** (a
   non-conducting grease that keeps water out of electrical plugs). None can be improvised later.

## The walkaround: every hitch-up, no exceptions

{{figure fig-trailer-hitch-checks}}

These checks follow NHTSA's trailer safety guidance (NHTSA, Trailer Safety).

```
[ ] COUPLER fully down on the ball, LATCH CLOSED and PINNED or
    LOCKED. Lift test: crank the tongue jack UP a few inches. The
    back of the vehicle should rise with it. If the coupler pops
    off, you found out in the driveway.
[ ] SAFETY CHAINS crossed in an X under the tongue (so they catch a
    dropped coupler), hooked to the hitch, not to each other. Enough
    slack to turn, not enough to drag.
[ ] TONGUE JACK fully raised (or removed and stowed).
[ ] LIGHTS plugged in. With a helper or a phone video: running
    lights, LEFT signal, RIGHT signal, BRAKE lights. Fix before
    moving.
[ ] TIRES at the MAXIMUM pressure printed on the trailer tire's
    sidewall, checked COLD. Look for cracks, bulges, wear.
[ ] LUG NUTS: check snug with a wrench, in a star pattern. Re-tighten
    after the first ~50 miles on any wheel that has been off; lug
    nuts loosen as they seat.
[ ] LOAD: heavy items forward, over or slightly ahead of the axle,
    so roughly 10 to 12 percent of the loaded trailer's weight
    presses down on the hitch ("tongue weight"). Too little makes
    the trailer sway. Everything strapped (01-03).
[ ] DOOR or gate latched. Anything on the roof or tongue secured.
```

## Wheel bearings: the killer, for a non-mechanic

{{figure fig-wheel-bearing-cutaway}}

Each wheel spins on two cone-shaped steel bearings sitting in grease inside the hub (the metal
centre the wheel bolts to). Over time the grease dries out, leaks past a worn seal, or lets water
in. The bearing then runs metal on metal, overheats, and can weld itself to the axle stub. On a
two-axle trailer that is a bad day; on a one-axle trailer it is the whole trip.

**Service rule:** inspect and repack the bearings **every 12 months or 12,000 miles, whichever
comes first** (Dexter Axle, Trailer Axle Operation, Maintenance and Service Manual), and at once on
a used trailer with no records. For a trailer that mostly sits, the yearly rule is the one that
matters.

A **repack** means: hub off, old grease cleaned out, bearings inspected (any pitting, blue heat
marks or roughness means replace; bearings are cheap), packed full of fresh grease, a new seal
fitted, and the axle nut set to the maker's specification, snug and then backed off slightly, not
forced tight. **Learn this once beside someone who has done it before doing it alone.**

**Grease fittings ("easy-lube" hubs).** Some hubs have a grease fitting in the end of the axle so a
grease gun can push fresh grease through. If yours do: jack the wheel up and turn it slowly while
pumping slowly, until fresh grease appears at the cap. **That is a top-up between repacks, not a
replacement for them**: it cannot inspect the bearings, and pumping too fast can burst the rear
seal and push grease onto brakes (etrailer.com, Trailer Bearings). No fitting means no shortcut:
repack or nothing.

**The hot-hub check: every stop, every time.**

```
Hold the back of your hand NEAR (not on) the centre of each hub.
  Warm, like metal left in the sun  -> normal.
  Too hot to hold your hand near    -> STOP. Do not drive on.
A hub you cannot touch is a bearing failing. Driving on means a
seized wheel, a lost wheel, or fire. Let it cool. Add grease if it
has a fitting. Creep at low speed to help, or make the leave-the-
trailer decision (01-03).
```

**What a failing bearing sounds like:** a low growl or rumble that rises and falls with road speed
(not engine speed), sometimes a rhythmic grinding or chirp, often changing in curves. Test with the
windows down at low speed on a quiet road. **Any new speed-linked noise from the trailer: stop and
do the hot-hub check.**

## Trailer tires: different rules from the car's

- **Trailer tires are marked "ST"** (special trailer). They are built stiff for towing, not for
  grip. **A light-truck ("LT") tire of the same or higher load rating is an accepted substitute.**
  A passenger-car ("P") tire is a last resort only, carrying about 10 percent less, because its
  soft sidewalls flex, overheat and encourage sway.
- **Pressure is the MAXIMUM printed on the sidewall, cold.** That is the opposite of a car, which
  uses its door placard (→ 16-10). Under-inflated trailer tires overheat and blow out.
- **Trailer tires age out at roughly 5 to 6 years even with good tread.** Trailers sit, and sun
  kills sidewalls from inside. **Read the date code:** the last four digits of the "DOT" code on the
  sidewall are the week and year it was made ("3921" means week 39 of 2021; → 29-14). A used trailer
  often arrives with tires many years old that look new. **Check the date, not the tread.**
- **Cracks in the sidewall, bulges, or an old date mean replace before the first long tow**, not
  after.

## The trailer spare

- **One mounted spare** with the correct bolt pattern, on a carrier or strapped inside.
- **Your vehicle's roadside kit works on the trailer** (→ 16-10): the plug kit, inflator, jack and
  lug wrench. A small trailer's corner is light; jack under the frame near the axle. Chock the
  vehicle and the trailer's other wheel. Same star-pattern lug-nut rules (→ 16-10, Case 4).
- **Tread punctures plug exactly as on a car** (→ 16-10, Case 2). **Sidewall damage means the
  spare.** That is the whole decision.

## Lights: the troubleshooting ladder

{{figure fig-four-pin-connector}}

Trailer lights fail constantly, and the fix is almost always the same. Work down the ladder and stop
when they work.

1. **The ground wire first. It causes most trailer light faults.** In a common 4-pin plug, the white
   wire and the light housings' mounting screws carry the return path. A bad ground looks crazy: dim
   lights, turn signals that flash everything, lights that work only at an angle. **Fix:** unbolt the
   white wire where it meets the trailer frame, wire-brush the frame and the terminal to bright metal,
   bolt it back tight. Do the same for each light's mounting screws.
2. **The plug itself.** Unplug, look for green corrosion, brush the pins clean, and **pack both
   halves with dielectric grease.** Make that a habit at every hitch-up. A dab in each bulb socket
   ends most repeat failures.
3. **The bulb or lamp.** Swap it (carry spares). Sealed LED lamps swap as a whole unit: two screws,
   two wires. Join wires with crimp connectors or solder, never twist-and-tape (→ 16-07).
4. **The wiring run**, last and rarest: look for a pinched or rubbed-through wire where the harness
   crosses the frame, especially at the tongue.

**Nothing lights at all?** Test the vehicle's socket first, with a cheap plug-in 4-pin tester or a
helper and a test light.

## Coupler, latch and frame

- **Coupler wear check** (every few months and before any long tow): hitched, latched, tongue jack
  lightly loaded, rock the tongue. **A clunk between coupler and ball means a worn coupler or the
  wrong ball size.** The required ball size is stamped on the coupler. A coupler on a ball one size
  too small tows fine until the first hard bump lifts it off.
- The latch moves freely, snaps fully shut, and takes the pin or padlock. Oil it. Replace a bent
  latch; couplers are cheap bolt-on parts.
- **Safety chain hooks and their welds:** look for stretching, cracks or rust-through.
- **Frame, tongue welds, spring hangers and U-bolts:** once a season, look for cracks at welds (a
  crack often leaves a rusty streak down the paint). **Anything cracked goes to a welder before it
  tows again.**
- **Wooden floor:** probe for soft spots; water gets in at door seals.

## Gear

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Mounted spare wheel and trailer tire, with carrier** | Turns a tire failure into a delay | Match the bolt pattern |
| **Grease gun and marine wheel-bearing grease** | Top-ups and repacks | One tube lasts years |
| Spare bearing and seal kit for your axle | Repack parts; keep on the shelf | Match the axle in the record |
| **Dielectric grease** | Ends most light problems | Plug and bulb sockets |
| Spare bulbs or a spare LED lamp, crimp connectors | Ladder step 3 | → 16-07 |
| 4-pin plug tester | Splits trailer faults from vehicle faults in seconds | Cheap |
| Two wheel chocks | Every jacking job | Wood blocks work |
| Padlocks for the coupler latch and the door | Theft is the everyday risk | |
| The roadside tire kit (→ 16-10) | Covers trailer tires too | Already in the vehicle |
| This page | The walkaround and the ladder | In the trailer |

**Substitutes:** bearings and seals are standard sizes, sold at auto parts and farm stores or taken
from a scrapped trailer with the same axle. A length of wire run from the light housing to bare frame
metal proves a bad ground in the field. A bottle jack or floor jack replaces a scissor jack.

## When it runs out

- **Sealed grease keeps for years.** Stock two tubes.
- **A boxed bearing-and-seal kit** turns a repack into an hour instead of a hunt. They do not age on
  the shelf.
- **Trailer tires age whether you tow or not.** Park out of direct sun, or shade the tires, to slow
  it. In a long event, nobody stocks trailer sizes, so the tire-conservation rules of → 23-04 apply
  twice over.
- **Lights:** sealed LED lamps are the buy-once answer; no filaments to shake apart on gravel.

## By phase

- **A, at home:** service the bearings, fill in the record, practise the walkaround and backing up
  in an empty lot (→ 22-05). Load the trailer as → 01-03 says.
- **B, on the move:** walkaround before leaving; **hot-hub check at every stop**; re-tighten lug
  nuts at the first stop. Winter towing multiplies every rule on → 17-01 and calls for much lower
  speeds. A problem caught at a fuel stop is a delay; one missed is the leave-the-trailer decision
  (→ 01-03).
- **C, settled:** the trailer becomes the hauler. Make the yearly repack a fall routine (→ 26-08).
  At a place with no shop, the grease gun and the boxed bearing kit you carry are the shop. Gravel
  eats lights; the ladder gets used.
