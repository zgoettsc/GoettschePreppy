# Knots and cordage

**What this page is for.** Six knots that cover almost every job (tying down, hanging, lashing,
tensioning), the rope to stock, how to keep it, how to make cord from plants and cloth, and the
one rope job that kills people: pulling a stuck vehicle. People try to learn thirty knots and
remember none. **Learn six, practice until your hands do them without thinking, and stop.**

> **Nothing on this page is rated for life safety.** These are utility knots for tying
> things down, lashing and hanging. **Do not use them, or any rope in this binder, for climbing,
> lowering a person, rescue, or anything where a failure drops a person.** That needs rated rope,
> rated hardware and training.
>
> **Never pull a stuck vehicle with rope, chain or a ratchet strap, or from a hitch ball.** A
> snapped line or a torn-off ball flies through windshields and people. The safe method is in its
> own block below.

## Do this now

1. **Buy rope in several sizes:** a lot of 550 paracord, 1/4-inch and 3/8-inch utility rope, bank
   line or tarred twine, and jute or sisal twine for anything you will burn or compost. Buy more
   than you think.
2. **Learn the six knots below this week:** bowline, taut-line hitch, clove hitch, two half
   hitches, square knot, trucker's hitch.
3. **Practice them in gloves and in the dark.** That is when you will need them (→ 11-04).
4. **Make a practice cord:** cut a 4-foot length of paracord, tie it to a drawer handle, and do one
   knot each evening against the steps below until all six are hands-only. Tick each one off.
5. {{#if has.child}}**Teach the children one knot**: the overhand first, then the square knot.
   Children enjoy this and it is a real skill (→ 20-07).{{else}}**Teach one knot to someone else.**
   Teaching it is the best test that you know it.{{/if}}

## Two words used in every step

{{figure fig-knot-parts-of-a-rope}}

- The **working end** is the short end you move.
- The **standing part** is the long rest of the rope, going to the load or the anchor.
- A **bight** is a fold of rope, a U-shape, with no crossing.
- A **loop** is a circle where the rope crosses itself.

The pictures for each knot come from the US Army's rigging manual (US Army, FM 5-125, Rigging
Techniques, Procedures, and Applications). The steps are written so each step is one hand motion,
and they work without the picture.

## Warm-up: the overhand knot

{{figure fig-knot-overhand}}

The simplest knot, the one children learn first, and a stopper at the end of a rope.

1. Make a loop near the end of the rope by crossing the working end over the standing part.
2. Push the working end through the loop from behind.
3. Pull both ends.

*Check:* a small lump that will not pass through a hole the rope itself passes through.

## The six knots

### 1. Bowline: a fixed loop that will not slip or jam

{{figure fig-knot-bowline}}

*Use:* a loop in the end of a line, tying to a ring or post, hauling. It unties easily even after
a heavy load.

1. Hold the standing part in your left hand. The last 2 feet of rope is the working end.
2. Make a small loop in the standing part by crossing it over itself, so that **the part of the
   rope leading toward the working end lies on top** of the part going down to the load. This
   small loop is the "rabbit hole".
3. Push the working end up through the small loop from underneath ("the rabbit comes out of the
   hole").
4. Pass the working end behind the standing part ("around the tree").
5. Bring the working end back down through the small loop, the same way it came out ("back down
   the hole").
6. Hold the working end and the side of the big loop together in one hand and the standing part
   in the other. Pull apart to tighten.

*Check:* the working end finishes lying inside the big loop, with a tail about as long as your
fist is wide. **Then pull the big loop hard. If it shrinks or slides, you made the small loop the
wrong way round and tied a slip knot.** Untie it and cross the rope the other way in step 2.

### 2. Taut-line hitch: an adjustable loop that grips under load

{{figure fig-knot-taut-line-hitch}}

*Use:* tent and tarp lines, clotheslines, anything you tighten and re-tighten (→ 11-03). One of
the most useful knots there is, and almost nobody knows it.

1. Pass the working end around the anchor (a stake or a tree). The standing part runs back to the
   tarp. You now have a big open loop between the anchor and the tarp.
2. Bring the working end back alongside the standing part, toward the tarp.
3. Wrap the working end around the standing part, inside the big loop, turning back toward the
   anchor.
4. Make a second wrap the same way, right beside the first, still inside the big loop.
5. Move the working end to the tarp side of those two wraps (outside the big loop) and make one
   more wrap around the standing part.
6. Tuck the working end under that last wrap and pull it snug.

*Check:* hold the knot and slide it along the standing part. It should slide by hand and hold when
the line is pulled.

### 3. Clove hitch: quick fastening to a post or pole

{{figure fig-knot-clove-hitch}}

*Use:* starting a lashing, tying to a rail or post. **It can slip when the pull keeps changing**,
so back it up with a half hitch.

1. Pass the working end over and around the post, then cross it diagonally over the standing part.
2. Take the working end around the post a second time, beside the first wrap.
3. Tuck the working end under its own second wrap, beneath the diagonal crossing.
4. Pull the working end and the standing part in opposite directions to set it.
5. For security, add a half hitch: the working end around the standing part and back through.

*Check:* two wraps sit side by side on the post with the diagonal crossing pinned between them.

### 4. Two half hitches (with a round turn): tying off to anything

{{figure fig-knot-round-turn-two-half-hitches}}

*Use:* tying a rope to a ring, rail, branch or post.

1. Pass the working end around the object. **For a real load, go around twice** (a "round turn");
   the friction does half the work.
2. Bring the working end over the standing part, around behind it, and up through the gap between
   itself and the object. That is one half hitch.
3. Repeat step 2 in the same direction, just beyond the first. That is the second.
4. Pull the working end to slide both hitches down against the object.

*Check:* the two hitches together look like a clove hitch around the standing part.

### 5. Square knot (reef knot): joining two ends of the same size

{{figure fig-knot-square-knot}}

*Use:* tying a bundle, a bandage (→ 12-02), a sack. **Not for joining two ropes under serious
load**; it can roll apart. For that, use the sheet bend below.

1. Hold one end in each hand.
2. Cross the right end over the left and wrap it under, as if starting to tie a shoe.
3. Cross the left end over the right and wrap it under.
4. Pull all four strands to tighten.

*Check:* each rope's two strands leave the knot on the same side, and the knot lies flat. If it
looks twisted and lopsided, it is a "granny knot", which slips. Untie it and start again
("right over left, then left over right").

### 6. Trucker's hitch: a rope pulley for pulling things tight

{{figure fig-knot-truckers-hitch}}

*Use:* strapping a load down, tightening a tarp ridge line, pulling something taut. In theory it
triples your pull; in practice about double, because the rope rubs on itself. Still surprising.

1. Tie the far end of the rope to something fixed (a bowline or two half hitches). Bring the
   working end over the load to your anchor point.
2. In the standing part, about 2 feet above the anchor, make a small loop by crossing the rope
   over itself.
3. Reach through that small loop, grab a bight of the standing part from the anchor side, and pull
   it through to form a hanging loop. This is your "pulley". It will spill free later.
4. Pass the working end down around the anchor (a hook, a rail, a stake).
5. Bring the working end up and through the pulley loop from step 3.
6. Pull down on the working end. Haul until tight.
7. Pinch the tight strands with one hand. With the other, lock the working end off with two half
   hitches around both strands below the pulley loop.

*Check:* undo the half hitches and pull the working end back out; the whole thing should fall
apart at once. That is the point.

### Two worth adding

**Sheet bend: joining two ropes, even of different sizes.** {{figure fig-knot-sheet-bend}}

1. Make a bight (a U) in the end of the thicker rope. Hold it.
2. Push the end of the thinner rope up through the U from underneath.
3. Wrap the thin end around behind both legs of the U.
4. Tuck the thin end under itself, across the front of the U (not through the U).
5. Pull the thin rope's standing part to tighten.

*Check:* both short ends finish on the same side of the knot.

**Prusik: a loop that grips a rope.** {{figure fig-knot-prusik}} A short loop of thinner cord
wrapped around a thicker rope. It slides when loose and grips when pulled. Useful for an
adjustable attachment point on a ridge line. (Again: not for holding a person.)

1. Tie a short length of thin cord into a closed loop (a double overhand or sheet bend joins the
   ends).
2. Lay the loop behind the thick rope. Pass one end of the loop around the rope and through the
   other end of the loop.
3. Repeat twice more, inside the first wrap, so three wraps sit neatly side by side.
4. Pull the loop to tighten. It should grip when pulled and slide when you push the wraps.

### Lashings: turning poles into furniture

A lashing ties two poles together with many turns of rope. With a **square lashing** (poles
crossing at right angles) and a **diagonal lashing** (poles crossing at an angle, or springing
apart), you can build a sawbuck (→ 16-06), a drying rack (→ 11-03), a trellis (→ 05-10), a shelter
frame or an antenna mast support (→ 15-06).

{{figure fig-lashing-square}}

**Square lashing.**
1. Lay the two poles crossed at a right angle. Tie a clove hitch on the upright pole just below
   the crosspiece.
2. Wrap the rope over the crosspiece, behind the upright, under the crosspiece on the other side,
   and behind the upright again. That is one wrap around the joint.
3. Do three or four such wraps, keeping each tight and laid beside the last, not on top.
4. Now make two or three "frapping" turns: wrap between the two poles, around the wraps
   themselves, pulling hard. This tightens everything.
5. Finish with a clove hitch on the crosspiece.

{{figure fig-lashing-diagonal}}

**Diagonal lashing.**
1. Tie a timber hitch (a loop wrapped back around itself) around both poles, across the joint
   diagonally.
2. Make three or four wraps along that same diagonal.
3. Make three or four wraps along the other diagonal.
4. Make two frapping turns between the poles, then finish with a clove hitch.

## Cordage: what to stock and why

| Type | Strength | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 550 paracord | Strong for its size | Everything general | **Its core is seven separate thin strands**: thread, snares, fishing line, repairs (→ 16-04) |
| Bank line or tarred twine | Strong, thin | Lashing, snares, repairs | Resists rot and sunlight; grips well |
| 1/4 to 3/8 in utility rope | Real loads | Hauling, tying down, ridge lines | Nylon stretches; polyester does not |
| Rope 1/2 in and up | Heavy loads | Dragging logs, heavy lifting. **Not vehicle recovery** | See the block below |
| Natural fiber (jute, sisal, hemp) | Moderate | Anything burned or composted: garden ties, kindling bundles | Rots, which is sometimes the point (→ 05-10) |
| Baling wire, mechanic's wire | n/a | The metal version of cord | → 16-07 |
| Ratchet straps | Very strong | Securing loads | Sunlight weakens the webbing |
| Bungee cords | Light | Light securing | Perish; **the hooks cause eye injuries** |

**How much a rope can hold.** Never load a rope past **one-fifth of its labeled breaking
strength**, and remember that **a knot cuts a rope's strength by a third to a half** (US Army,
FM 5-125). Military-specification Type III paracord breaks at 550 lb when new, dry and unknotted,
so about 100 lb is its working load; commercial "550" cord is often weaker. Rubbing, sunlight and
age lower every figure further.

## Pulling a stuck vehicle: rope is the wrong tool

{{figure fig-vehicle-recovery-setup}}

{{#if has.vehicle}}Getting {{#if has.trailer}}a vehicle and a loaded trailer (→ 16-11){{else}}a
vehicle{{/if}} out of a ditch or a snowdrift is the most likely rope job on the road, and one of the
deadliest amateur mistakes.{{else}}If you ever ride in or borrow a vehicle that gets stuck, this is
the most likely rope job on the road, and one of the deadliest amateur mistakes.{{/if}} A snapped
line, a broken hook or a torn-off hitch ball at thousands of pounds of pull goes through windshields
and people.

```
[ ] DIG AND ADD TRACTION FIRST (22-05: shovel, traction mats,
    sand). Most "recoveries" are a shovel job. Pull only when
    that fails.
[ ] ONLY a rated recovery (kinetic) strap or rated tow strap with
    NO metal hooks. Never rope, never a chain, never a ratchet strap.
[ ] ATTACH ONLY to rated recovery points, or to the hitch RECEIVER
    with a rated shackle or soft shackle. NEVER to a hitch ball,
    a bumper, a tie-down loop or a suspension part.
[ ] LINE DAMPER: drape a blanket, coat or floor mat over the middle
    of the strap. It drops a broken strap instead of launching it.
[ ] EVERYONE ELSE stands at least 1.5 x the strap's length away and
    OUT OF LINE with the pull. Children well back or in the far
    vehicle, never beside the line.
[ ] PULL GENTLY, IN LINE, from a slack start. No running starts
    unless the strap is a kinetic rope made for it, and even then
    a few feet of slack, not a run.
[ ] Inspect the strap after every pull. A cut or glazed strap is done.
```

**Your strap's own printed instructions govern over this page.** Keep them with the strap
(→ 22-05).

## Care and failure

- **Sunlight destroys synthetic rope.** Rope left outdoors weakens within a season or two, and you
  cannot see it happen. Store rope out of the sun.
- **Rubbing is the main way rope fails.** A rope running over an edge cuts itself. Pad the edge with
  cloth, a piece of hose or wood.
- **Wet natural fiber rots.** Dry it before storing.
- **Knots weaken rope.** That is normal, and one reason utility rope is not for life safety.
- **Inspect before every load:** fuzz, cuts, shiny glazed patches, stiffness, discolouring.
- **Seal the ends** so they do not fray: melt synthetic rope with a lighter; wrap natural rope ends
  tightly with thin twine ("whipping").
- **Coil it and hang it.** A tangled rope in an emergency is a rope you do not have.

## Making cord from nothing

A real skill, and it works. It is slow; practice in summer with fresh material.

- **Plant fibers:** the inner bark of basswood, elm, willow or dogwood; the stems of stinging
  nettle, milkweed and dogbane; yucca leaves (→ 05-17 for what grows near you, and identify every
  plant with certainty).
- **The reverse-wrap method:** hold two bundles of fiber, twist each one away from you, and wrap
  them around each other toward you. The opposing twists lock. Learn it once and you can make cord
  from a ditch.
- **Rawhide and sinew** from animals you process (→ 05-09): very strong, and rawhide shrinks as it
  dries, which makes it excellent for binding a tool head (→ 16-08).
- **Cloth strips:** cut a sheet, shirt or tarp in one continuous spiral to make a long strip.

**Scavenge:** rope and cord from garages, boats, farms, tarps, tents and curtain pulls; wire from
fencing and coat hangers (→ 16-07); baling twine around any farm; seat belts and webbing from
vehicles (very strong); cloth strips; plant fiber.

## When it runs out

- **Cord barely runs out if you keep it out of the sun.** Synthetic rope stored indoors lasts
  decades.
- **What gets used up:** paracord cut into short pieces (it is so useful it disappears), strap
  webbing, and bungees.
- **Length is the resource.** Do not cut a 100-foot rope into ten short pieces unless you must; you
  can always tie a bowline in the middle.
- **Substitute:** wire (→ 16-07), cloth strips, rawhide, and **lashings instead of hardware**:
  knowing knots means needing fewer brackets, screws and fittings.
- **The skill costs nothing.** Six knots and two lashings is an afternoon of learning that pays for
  years, and it is easy to teach (→ 18-03, → 20-07).

## By phase

- **A, at home:** stock rope, learn the knots, and use them: a clothesline (→ 11-03), a trellis
  (→ 05-10), a tarp over the woodpile, an antenna support (→ 15-06).
- **B, on the move:** paracord is one of the best weight-for-use items you can carry: a hundred
  feet, plus ratchet straps if you are driving.
- **C, settled:** fencing, animals, building, hauling, gates. Lashing skills reduce your need for
  hardware when there is no store.
