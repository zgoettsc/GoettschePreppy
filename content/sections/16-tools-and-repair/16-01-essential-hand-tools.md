# Essential hand tools

**What this page is for.** Which hand tools to own, how to keep them, and how to use them
without getting hurt. Hand tools need no fuel and no power, break in ways you can fix, and last
for generations. Power tools are faster while batteries and fuel last. The rule for this whole
section: **own the hand-powered version of every job you depend on.**

> **CAUTION. With no clinic, the tools chapter is a medical chapter.** OSHA warns that even simple
> hand tools can cause severe injuries when used or kept badly (OSHA, Hand and Power Tools). Cuts,
> punctures, crushed fingers and eye injuries from tool work are hard to treat without a clinic
> (→ 12-02). Sharp tools, the right technique, eye protection, and not working
> when you are exhausted or cold prevent more harm than any first-aid kit can treat.
>
> **Check that every household member's tetanus shot is up to date** (CDC, About Tetanus).
> Tetanus is a serious infection that enters through deep, dirty wounds, and tool punctures are
> exactly that kind of wound (→ 12-04). CDC counts anyone not current with the 10-year booster
> as at higher risk; ask a clinic now while one is open.

## Do this now

1. **Lay out every tool you own** on a table and write it down (→ 22-04). Most households find
   three screwdrivers and no file, no hacksaw and no hand drill.
2. **Buy the hand-powered version of what you depend on:** a hand drill or brace, a hand saw,
   files. These are the tools that still work in year three.
3. **Buy several pairs of eye protection** and keep one at every place where work happens. Eye
   injuries are permanent and there may be no eye doctor.
4. **Buy files and sharpening stones** (→ 16-02). A dull tool is dangerous and useless, and these
   are cheap now and hard to get later.
5. **Organize and label** so anyone can find a tool in the dark, including someone who did not
   put it away.

## Buy good ones once, for what you depend on

Cheap tools fail under load, and they fail suddenly, often into your hand. For the tools you
will rely on (axe, saw, knife, pliers, wrenches, files), buy good ones once. For tools you use
now and then, cheap is fine. **Old tools from estate sales, farm auctions and flea markets are
often better than new ones** and cost little: older hand tools were made to be resharpened
for a lifetime.

## The core kit, by job

{{figure fig-core-tool-kit}}

**Cutting and shaping wood** (→ 16-06)
- Axe (for felling and splitting), splitting maul (a heavy, blunt splitting axe) and steel wedges
- Hatchet (a small one-hand axe) for kindling
- Bow saw (a steel frame holding a thin blade) with spare blades; crosscut hand saw; folding saw
- Wood chisels, a draw knife (a two-handled blade pulled toward you to shave wood), a hand plane
- Brace and bit (a crank-shaped hand drill) or an "eggbeater" hand drill, with a set of bits
- Rasp and wood files

**Metal and general** (→ 16-08)
- Hacksaw and a large supply of spare blades (**the blades are what wears out**)
- Metal files: flat, round, half-round, triangular
- Ball-peen hammer (rounded back, for metal), claw hammer, small sledge
- Cold chisels (for cutting metal) and punches
- A bench vise (a clamp bolted to a bench). It is one of the most useful things you can own; a
  portable one also works.
- An anvil, or any heavy flat block of steel

**Gripping and turning**
- Pliers: slip-joint, needle-nose, linesman's (heavy square-jawed pliers that also cut wire),
  locking pliers, and adjustable groove-joint ("channel-lock") pliers
- Wrenches: a full set, plus adjustable wrenches and pipe wrenches
- Sockets and ratchet, in both SAE (inch) and metric sizes
- Screwdrivers: flat, Phillips, Torx (six-point star) and square drive
- Clamps. You will always want more clamps.

**Measuring and marking**
- Tape measure, folding rule, combination square, level, chalk line, pencils and a scribe
  (a sharp steel point for marking metal), calipers

**Digging and earth**
- Round-point and square shovels, a spade, a mattock or pick, a post-hole digger (→ 13-07), a
  garden fork, a pry bar

**Sharpening** (→ 16-02)
- Mill bastard file (a common flat file) for axes; chainsaw files in your chain's size;
  coarse and fine stones; a strop (leather for polishing an edge); a honing guide

**Safety gear. Not optional.**
- Eye protection, several pairs, one at every work area
- Hearing protection. Power tools and chainsaws cause permanent hearing loss (CDC/NIOSH, About
  Noise and Hearing Loss), and there may be no hearing specialist.
- Work gloves, several pairs, plus cut-resistant gloves
- Sturdy boots, steel-toe if you can
- Dust masks or respirators for sawdust, insulation, mold and ash
- A first-aid kit and a bleeding-control kit at the work area, not in the house (→ 12-01,
  → 12-08). A tourniquet and the training to use it are the core of bleeding control (American
  College of Surgeons, Stop the Bleed).

## Power tools and the power you have

Power tools save a great deal of labor while there is power for them. What limits them is fuel,
charged batteries and sun, not the tool.

{{#if has.power_station}}**You own a power station** (about {{ gear.station_wh_total }} watt-hours
in total, → 09-03). Many corded tools can run from a power station's AC outlet if the tool's
running watts are below the station's continuous rating. Read the watts or amps on the tool's
label (→ 29-14) and the station's label; if the tool lists amps, multiply by 120 to get rough
watts (→ 29-02). Motors pull a short burst of extra power when they start, so test each tool on
the station now, unloaded first, before you rely on it. Some stations have a mode for heaters
that lowers the output voltage; read your station's manual and turn any such mode off for
motors.{{else}}If you later own a power station or generator (→ 09-03, → 09-04), many corded tools
can run from it: compare the tool's label watts with the station's continuous rating (→ 29-02,
→ 29-14).{{/if}}

- **Charging cordless tool batteries** is the cheaper habit: a tool-battery charger is a small
  load, and the charged pack then works far from the power source.
- **Power for medical devices and food cold comes first** (→ 09-01). Tool charging uses what is
  left over.
- **Hearing protection** every time a power tool runs.

| Job | Power tool | Hand tool to own as well |
|---|---|---|
| Cutting firewood | Chainsaw (fuel-limited, → 16-06) | Bow saw, crosscut saw, axe, maul |
| Drilling | Cordless or corded drill | Brace and bit, hand drill, gimlet |
| Cutting metal | Angle grinder | Hacksaw and spare blades, cold chisel, files |
| Sharpening | Bench grinder | Files, stones, hand-crank grinder |
| Sawing boards | Circular saw | Hand saw, panel saw |
| Sanding and shaping | Sander | Rasps, files, planes, scrapers |
| Driving screws | Impact driver | Screwdrivers, brace with a screwdriver bit |

**Own the hand version of everything in that table.** The day the power fails is usually the day
the work is urgent.

## Care, which is what makes tools last

- **Clean and dry after use.** Rust destroys more tools than wear does.
- **Oil the steel.** Wipe every steel tool with light oil each season. A rag kept in a closed can
  of mineral oil or machine oil makes this easy. **Mineral or machine oil only in that can.**
- **Sharpen before the edge is dull** (→ 16-02).
- **Look after handles.** Rub linseed oil into wooden handles and check them for cracks. A loose
  axe head is dangerous (→ 16-06).
- **Linseed-oil rags can catch fire by themselves.** One maker's label: rags, steel wool or waste
  soaked with boiled linseed oil may spontaneously catch fire if improperly discarded, and the time
  and conditions are unpredictable. **Immediately after each use, put them in a sealed metal
  container filled with water** (Sunnyside, Boiled Linseed Oil use and safety information). Never
  pile them, put them in a pocket or leave them near stored fuel. Your product's own label
  governs.
- **Store tools organized and off the ground.** A tool you cannot find is a tool you do not have.
- **Mark anything you lend** so it comes back.

## Working safely (OSHA basics)

These follow OSHA's hand and power tool guidance (OSHA, Hand and Power Tools, OSHA 3080).

- **Right tool for the job.** Improvised tool use is where hands get hurt.
- **Tools in good condition.** No splintered or cracked handles. No "mushroomed" heads on chisels,
  wedges and punches (the struck end spread into a ragged lip): OSHA says they can shatter on
  impact and send sharp fragments flying. No dull edges: dull tools cause more hazards than sharp
  ones.
- **Cut away from yourself.** Before every cut, ask where the tool goes if it slips, and make sure
  no hand, leg or person is there.
- **Eye protection every time**, not just for the jobs that look dangerous.
- **Do not work when exhausted, cold, rushed or in poor light.** In a winter without power that is
  the normal condition, so notice it and stop.
- **Children stay well clear of tool work**, and tools are stored out of small children's reach
  (→ 20-02). Whether and when older children use tools is a household choice: {{choice DC-24}}
  (→ 30-25).
- **Bleeding-control kit at the work area** (→ 12-08), and know how to use it (→ 12-02).

## Getting more tools

- **Estate sales, farm auctions, flea markets and old sheds** have excellent hand tools for very
  little money. Do this now.
- **Abandoned buildings and garages** (→ 22-02): tools, fasteners, wire and hardware. Take only
  what the law and your own ethics allow at the time.
- **Vehicles** often hold a socket set, a jack and a pry bar.
- **Improvised:** a rock as a hammer; a vehicle leaf spring as chisel or blade steel; rebar as a
  pry bar; an old file ground into a knife or scraper (→ 16-08); any heavy steel block as an anvil.
- **What you cannot make: files and saw blades.** They are hardened steel with cut teeth. Stock
  them heavily.

## When it runs out

- **Hand tools barely wear out. Their consumables do:** saw blades, files, sandpaper, drill bits
  and sharpening stones. Over-stock those now (→ 23-02).
- **Count saw blades and files.** A bow saw blade cutting a winter of firewood wears out; a file
  sharpening axes and chains wears out. They are the quiet bottleneck of firewood (→ 06-04).
- **Make them last:** sharpen instead of replacing (→ 16-02); use the right tool so nothing wears
  early; avoid cutting frozen wood where you can, because it is hard on edges.
- **Resupply:** scavenging (→ 22-02) and trade (→ 18-02). Tools are good barter goods, and tool
  skills are better (→ 18-03).
- **The real asset is the skill.** A person who can sharpen, fix and improvise keeps a household
  running on salvage, and is one of the most valuable people in any community (→ 18-03, → 23-05).

## By phase

- **A, sheltering at home:** buy and organize now, while tools are cheap. Build hand-tool backups
  for everything you depend on. Set up a work area with eye protection and a bleeding-control kit.
- **B, on the move:** a very small kit: a good knife, a multi-tool, pliers, a folding saw, a small
  file, cord, tape and wire. Weight is brutal on the move (→ 22-03), and tools compete with water.
- **C, settled somewhere new:** this chapter comes into its own: more building, more repair, no
  store. A vise, an anvil, a full hand-tool kit and a real work area are basic infrastructure.
  Set them up early (→ 01-04).
