# Bicycles and human-powered transport

**What this page is for.** Moving people, water, wood and supplies when fuel is gone: bicycles,
bike trailers, carts and sleds, and the three bicycle repairs that cover most failures. A bicycle
carries several times what a person can walk with, at several times walking speed, on nothing but
food. **A bicycle with a trailer is the most capable human-powered transport there is.**

> **CAUTION. Cold and ice.** {{#if is.cold_winters}}Your winters are cold enough that cycling is
> impractical or dangerous for months.{{else}}In ice, deep snow and hard cold, cycling is
> impractical or dangerous.{{/if}} Riding adds your own speed to the wind chill (→ 29-10), and a
> fall on ice is a broken wrist with no emergency room (→ 12-09). In those months, **the sled
> and the cart are the transport, not the bicycle.**{{#if has.young_child}} **A young child
> riding in a trailer makes no heat of their own**; check them constantly in the cold, or do not
> go (→ 11-02, → 12-10).{{/if}}

## Do this now

1. **Make sure every adult's bicycle actually works:** tires, tubes, brakes, chain. A bike with
   flat tires and a rusted chain is not transport.
2. **Buy the consumables, several of each:** inner tubes, patch kits, tires, chain, quick links,
   brake pads, cables.
3. **Buy a bike trailer** (cargo or child). It turns a bicycle into a hauling vehicle.
4. **Buy a plastic sled and a garden cart.** Between them they cover most winter and summer
   hauling, and both are cheap.
5. **Learn to fix a flat and adjust the brakes** (Procedures 1 and 2). Twenty minutes, once.
6. **Photograph your rear derailleur now** (Procedure 3) and add the photo below.

## Why it matters

Fuel runs out, and vehicles also need tires, batteries and parts. **A bicycle needs food and a
small kit of spares.**
- It moves several times faster than walking and carries far more.
- **With a trailer it hauls far more than anyone can carry:** water (→ 03-02), firewood (→ 06-04),
  salvage (→ 22-02), food. The trailer's label gives its load limit.
- It is quiet and does not announce itself the way an engine does (→ 14-03).
- It can be repaired with simple tools and salvaged parts.

**The main use is hauling, not travel:** water from a source, wood from a lot, supplies from a
salvage run. That is what the trailer is for.

## Seasons

| Season | What works |
|---|---|
| Dry and mild | **Bicycle and trailer**: excellent. Cart and wheelbarrow for short hauls |
| Mud | Bike is poor. **Cart with wide wheels**, or walk |
| Ice and snow | **Sled.** A bike on ice is a broken wrist. Studded tires help on packed snow, not in drifts |
| Deep snow or blizzard | **Nothing. Do not move** (→ 17-01) |

{{#if is.snow_region}}**You live in snow country.** Plan on the sled for several months a year.{{/if}}

**The sled is the underrated winter tool.** A cheap plastic toboggan hauls water, firewood,
supplies and children across snow and ice safely. Carrying a heavy load or a child across ice on
foot is one of the most likely ways to break a bone in this whole binder (→ 11-06, → 12-09).

## Choosing and keeping a bicycle

**Choose sturdy over fast:** a hybrid or mountain bike with **wide tires**, a **steel frame** (it
can be brazed if it cracks, → 16-08), **rack mounts**, and simple parts. Complicated modern gears
have more to go wrong and fewer spares.

| Part | Why | Stock |
|---|---|---|
| **Inner tubes** | The number one failure by far | **Two or more per bike** (size is printed on the tire's side) |
| Patch kits with vulcanizing patches and glue | A tube can be patched many times | Several kits; the glue dries out once opened |
| Tires | Wear; sidewalls crack with age and sunlight | A spare pair per bike |
| Chain and quick links | Stretches and snaps | One or two spare chains; two quick links per bike |
| Brake pads and cables | Safety-critical; wear steadily | Several sets |
| Spokes and a spoke wrench | Loaded wheels go out of true | A handful per wheel size |
| Grease, chain lube, oil | Neglect kills bikes | Bulk |
| Tire levers, pump, multi-tool, chain tool | The field kit | On the bike |

**Puncture-resistant tires or tire liners** are worth the money: glass and thorns are constant. A
frame pump on the bike and a floor pump at home. A pump is not optional.

**Upkeep that matters:**
- Keep the chain clean and oiled. A dry chain wears everything fast.
- Keep tires at the pressure printed on the sidewall. Low pressure causes "pinch" flats and rim
  damage.
- Keep the bike dry and under cover. Rust destroys bikes.
- Straighten ("true") a wheel that wobbles.

## Procedure 1: fix a flat

{{figure fig-tube-patch-steps}}

**The practical way: carry two spare tubes per bike and swap**, then patch the punctured tube at
home in good light. A swap is quicker than a roadside patch and fails less.
Patching still matters: it is what lets each tube survive many flats.

1. **Remove the wheel.** Open the brake's quick release (or unhook the curved cable end on rim
   brakes), then open the axle lever or undo the axle nuts. For the rear wheel, shift to the
   smallest rear gear first so the chain drops off easily.
2. Let out all remaining air. Hook a tire lever under the tire's edge (the "bead"), pry it over
   the rim, and hook the lever onto a spoke.
3. Slide a second lever along under the bead until one whole side of the tire is off the rim.
   Leave the other side on.
4. Pull out the tube, valve last. **Then run your fingers slowly around the inside of the tire.**
   The nail or glass that caused this is usually still there, waiting for the next tube. Check
   that the rim tape covers every spoke hole.
5. **Find the hole:** pump some air in and listen, or pass it slowly near your lips to feel the
   air; best of all, dunk it a section at a time in water and watch for bubbles. Mark the hole.
6. **Patch:** rub the spot with the kit's sandpaper until dull. Spread a thin film of glue wider
   than the patch. **Wait until the glue looks dry (the kit's instructions give the time). This
   is the step everyone rushes, and why patches fail.** Peel the foil off the patch without touching the black rubber
   underneath, and press that black side onto the glue, centered on the hole. **The clear plastic
   film on top stays on.** Press hard, as long as the kit says.
7. **Refit:** put a breath of air in the tube so it holds its shape, push the valve through the
   rim, tuck the tube into the tire all round, then roll the bead back over the rim with your
   palms, finishing opposite the valve. **Use levers only for the last few inches**; levers pinch
   tubes.
8. Before pumping up, squeeze your way round both sides of the tire to check the tube is not caught
   under the bead.
9. Pump to the pressure printed on the tire. Spin the wheel and check the line molded on the tire
   sits evenly above the rim all the way round. Refit the wheel, reconnect the brake, spin it, and
   squeeze the brake once before riding.

**Practice this once at home this month.** Your first flat repair should not be the real one.

## Procedure 2: adjust rim brakes

{{figure fig-rim-brake-adjust}}

1. **The symptom** is weak braking (the lever pulls nearly to the handlebar) or a pad rubbing.
2. **Barrel adjuster first.** This is the small ridged barrel where the cable enters the lever or
   the brake. Unscrewing it tightens the cable. Half a turn at a time; squeeze; repeat. This fixes
   most brake complaints.
3. If the adjuster runs out of thread, screw it most of the way back in, loosen the **cable clamp
   bolt** on the brake arm, pull the cable snug with pliers so the pads almost touch the rim, and
   tighten the bolt firmly.
4. **Test:** spin the wheel; it must turn freely. Squeeze hard; it must stop dead. Release; it
   must spin freely again. If one pad drags, center the brake (most have a small centring screw on
   one arm) or check the wheel is seated straight.
5. **Pads must hit the rim fully**, never the tire (it will cut through) and never below the rim.
   Loosen, line up, tighten. Replace pads worn past their grooves.

Disc brakes work differently; follow the bike maker's manual.

## Procedure 3: replace or rejoin a chain

1. A snapped chain, or a stretched one that skips, is fixed with the **quick link** (a two-piece
   joining link) from the kit, and for a full replacement, a spare chain.
2. **Size a new chain against the old one:** lay them side by side and match them link for link,
   removing extra links with the chain tool (it pushes a pin out). The chain must match your
   gearing's **speed count** (count the rear gears: ____-speed; the chain packet says which it
   fits).
3. **Threading it wrong is the classic failure.** Use your photo:

{{photo derailleur-threading}}

4. Thread the chain to match the photo, bring the two ends together on the bottom run, and join
   them with the quick link: one half on each end, plates together, then press hard on a pedal
   until it clicks shut.
5. **Roadside, with no spare chain:** push out the damaged link's pin with the chain tool, rejoin
   the shorter chain with a quick link, and avoid the biggest gear combinations on the way home.

## How much each method carries

| Method | Load, compared | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Walking with a pack | The least; tiring | The baseline |
| Bicycle with bags | More than a pack | Steering gets twitchy; keep weight low |
| **Bicycle and trailer** | **Much more** | The workhorse; the trailer's label gives its limit |
| Garden cart or wheelbarrow | Heavy loads, short distances | Water, wood, dirt |
| **Sled on snow** | Heavy loads, safely | The winter answer |
| Hand cart | Heavy loads | Its label gives the limit |

Your own limits are the ones that count: weigh a loaded trip once on a bathroom scale and write it
here: ______ lb.

**Water is why this matters** (→ 03-02): a gallon weighs about 8.3 lb (→ 25-02), so five gallons is
over 40 lb and awkward. A cart or sled turns a miserable daily trip into a routine one.

## Children

{{#if has.child}}Children in your household: {{#each children as c}}{{c.label}} ({{c.age_text}}); {{/each}}{{/if}}

- **A bike trailer** suits a child old enough to sit up well. It is enclosed and visible, and it
  also hauls cargo.
- **An infant** is carried, or rides in a trailer only with a proper infant seat or support.
- **In the cold: a sled, not a bike.** A child in a trailer moving at bike speed in freezing air
  feels a serious wind chill and is making no heat (→ 11-02, → 12-10). Check them constantly, or
  do not go.
- **Bright colors and helmets for everyone.**

**Salvage and substitutes:**
- **Bicycles are everywhere:** garages, sheds, curbs (→ 22-02). Most need only tubes, a chain and
  oil. Any bike is a parts bike.
- **Trailer:** two bike wheels, angle iron or conduit, and a plywood box (→ 16-07, → 16-08).
- **Cart:** wheelbarrow, garden wagon, appliance dolly, or a box on lawnmower or stroller wheels.
- **Sled:** plastic toboggan, a plastic barrel cut in half lengthwise, a truck bed liner, a car
  hood.
- **Tube patches:** pieces of old inner tube glued with contact cement (→ 16-05).
- **Tires:** hard to improvise. Stock them.

## When it runs out

- **Tubes and tires are what stop a bicycle completely.** Stock them heavily. Patch kits stretch
  tubes a long way, so patches are the best-value spare.
- **Count tubes and tires per bike** and add them to → 23-02.
- **Make them last:** patch rather than replace; keep pressure right; fit liners; watch for glass.
- **Last resort:** a flat tire stuffed tight with rope, cloth or hose gets you home slowly.
- **The sled and the cart are the options that cannot fail**: no tires, no chain, no spares, just a
  hull and a rope (→ 16-03). In a very long event, they are what is still working.
- **Walking never runs out:** good boots (→ 11-06), a good pack, reasonable loads.

## By phase

- **A, at home:** bike and trailer for water, wood and salvage in good weather; sled and cart in
  winter. Quiet transport is discreet transport (→ 14-03).
- **B, on the move:** a loaded bicycle you push, not ride, still carries far more than your back
  (→ 01-03). In deep winter it is not workable (→ 01-02, → 11-01); a sled is.
- **C, settled:** a bicycle is valuable for anything within a few miles, and a cart or wagon becomes
  daily infrastructure.
