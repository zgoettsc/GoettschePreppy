# Axes, saws and wood tools

**What this page is for.** Cutting and splitting firewood by hand and with a chainsaw without
losing a foot, a leg or a hand. Wood processing is the most frequent dangerous work a household
does without the grid: every day, all winter, often cold and tired, with tools that cut through
bone.

> **CAUTION. This is where serious injuries actually happen.**
> - **An axe wound to the foot or shin, and a chainsaw wound to the leg, are among the most likely
>   severe-bleeding injuries in this whole binder** (→ 12-02). A bleeding-control kit with a
>   tourniquet lives at the woodpile, not in the house (→ 12-08).
> - **Chainsaw kickback** is the bar being thrown back toward the operator's face and body when
>   the tip touches something or the wood pinches the chain. The University of Missouri Extension
>   names kickback, pushback and pull-in as the forces that make operators lose control
>   (Operating a Chain Saw Safely). Most chainsaw injuries are deep cuts to the legs and the left
>   hand. **Chaps, a helmet with face shield, hearing protection and real instruction are not
>   optional.**
> - **Felling a standing tree kills experienced people.** Learn it from a person, not a page. A
>   leaning, dead or wind-thrown tree is more dangerous, not less.
> - **Never refuel a hot saw, and never pour fuel near a flame or running engine.** Fuel is never
>   stored or transferred near an open flame or a running appliance: the vapor ignites (floor,
>   → 30-01).

## Do this now

1. **Buy chainsaw chaps, a helmet with face shield and ear muffs, and cut-resistant gloves before
   you next cut anything.** Chaps are made of long fibers that pull out and jam the chain (University
   of Missouri Extension). They cost little next to a leg.
2. **Hang a bleeding-control kit with a tourniquet at the woodpile** (→ 12-08), and learn to use
   it one-handed on yourself (→ 12-02).
3. **Buy the hand set:** a good axe, a splitting maul, steel wedges, a bow saw with many spare
   blades, and a crosscut saw. These are what you will use in year two (→ 06-04).
4. **Get instruction:** a county Extension class, a forestry course, or an experienced person. One
   afternoon changes your risk for good.
5. **Wear wool, not fleece, near fire and hot work** (→ 11-05), and nothing loose near a saw.

## The tools, by job

| Tool | Job | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Splitting maul** (heavy, blunt wedge-shaped head) | Splitting rounds | The firewood workhorse. Not for chopping |
| **Steel wedges and a sledge** | Knotty or large rounds | Have two wedges |
| **Felling axe** (long handle, thinner head) | Chopping across the grain, removing limbs | Sharp, rounded edge (→ 16-02) |
| **Hatchet** | Kindling | **The most common injury tool**: the short handle puts your leg in its swing |
| **Bow saw** | Cutting logs to length ("bucking") | Fast, cheap; **blades are the consumable** |
| **Crosscut hand saw** | Larger wood, quiet, no fuel | Slower; sharpenable if traditional |
| **Folding saw** | Limbs; carry on the move | Very useful |
| **Chainsaw** | Volume, while fuel lasts | Fastest and most dangerous |
| **Peavey or cant hook** (a lever with a swinging hook) | Rolling logs | Saves backs and fingers |
| **Sawbuck** (an X-shaped stand) | Holds wood at working height while sawing | **Build one**; it prevents many injuries |
| **Chopping block** | **For splitting: low, knee height or less, wide and stable. For kindling with a hatchet: higher**, so hands stay clear | See rule 1 |

## The axe rules that prevent the injury

{{figure fig-chopping-block-height}}

1. **A chopping block at the right height.** This matters most. A round on the bare ground means
   the axe's swing carries on into your shins. **For splitting, a low block (knee height or less)**
   means the head arrives near the bottom of its swing with the handle about level, so a miss
   hits the block or the ground. A waist-high splitting block makes a glancing blow travel forward
   and down into your legs. The higher block is only for hatchet and kindling work.
2. **Feet apart, out of the swing.** Stand so that a glancing blow goes between your feet or into
   the block, not into a leg.
3. **Nothing behind you or overhead.** Check the whole swing path every time.
4. **Sharp** (→ 16-02). A dull axe glances off.
5. **Check the head is tight** every session. A loose head flies off. Re-wedge or re-hang it
   (→ 16-08).
6. **Never chop when exhausted, cold or in poor light.** That is the condition you will most often
   be in, which is why the rule matters. Stop and do it tomorrow.
7. **Children and bystanders well clear**, inside a wide, enforced circle (→ 20-02). Whether older
   children split kindling is a household choice: {{choice DC-24}} (→ 30-25).
8. **Eye protection.** Chips fly; frozen wood shatters.
9. **Never hold wood with your hand while striking it.** Split on a stable surface.
10. **Frozen wood splits more easily but shatters more**, and chips edges. Eye protection
    especially in winter.

## Chainsaws

{{figure fig-chainsaw-kickback-zone}}

**Kickback** happens in a fraction of a second when **the upper tip of the bar** touches wood or
anything else, or when the wood closes and pinches the chain. The bar is thrown up and back toward
you.

**Protective gear** (OSHA, Chain Saw Safety; University of Missouri Extension):
- **Chaps or cut-resistant trousers.** The legs are the most common contact point. Chaps overlap
  the boot tops.
- **Helmet with face shield and ear muffs**: falling limbs, chips, and permanent hearing loss
  (CDC/NIOSH, About Noise and Hearing Loss).
- **Safety glasses** under the shield.
- **Leather gloves.**
- **Steel-toe boots.**
- **No loose clothing.**

**Operating rules:**
- **Both hands on the saw, always, thumb wrapped around the front handle.**
- **Never cut with the upper tip of the bar** (the kickback zone).
- **Never cut above shoulder height.**
- **Firm footing. Never on a ladder. Never in a tree** without specific training.
- **Watch for pinching.** Before cutting, think about which side of the log is being squeezed and
  which stretched, and use wedges. **If the bar is pinched: engine off, then drive a wedge into
  the cut (or lift the log with the peavey) and lift the saw out by hand. Never yank a running saw
  free, and never start it while pinched.**
- **Test the chain brake before every session.** With the engine idling, push the front hand guard
  forward. The chain must stop instantly. If it does not, the saw does not run today.
- **Chain brake on whenever you walk with the saw.**
- **Cut chaps are finished.** Any cut that reaches the fibers means retire them; patched chaps do
  not jam a chain. Replace a cracked helmet too.
- **Let the saw cool before refuelling** (→ 06-06, → 06-07).
- **Do not cut alone if you can avoid it.** With no ambulance coming, cutting alone is a much bigger
  risk than it is today.
- **Sharpen the chain properly** (→ 16-02). A badly sharpened chain kicks back more.

**Chainsaw fuel goes stale.** Gasoline mixed with two-stroke oil does not keep well (→ 29-01).
Whether to use old fuel in a saw is a household choice: {{choice DC-17}} (→ 30-18).

## Felling: the honest version

**Do not learn to fell trees from a book.** Felling means reading lean, tension, rot, wind, trees
hung up in other trees, trunks that split upward ("barber chair"), and escape routes. It kills
professionals every year. Get instruction in person.

**And you mostly do not need to fell.** Most emergency firewood comes from **storm debris, standing
dead trees, downed limbs and wood already on the ground** (→ 06-04), which is safer, already partly
dry, and plentiful after any windstorm. **Cutting to length and splitting downed wood is the job.
Felling is the exception.**

**If you must:** clear two escape paths at 45 degrees behind you, look up for hung-up trees and
loose dead limbs ("widow-makers"), never work under a hung-up tree, and never fell near power
lines.

## Winter

- **Frozen wood is harder on edges and shatters more.** Sharpen more often; wear eye protection.
- **Cold hands are clumsy.** Warm up before precise work (→ 11-04).
- **Short days tempt you to keep working in poor light** (→ 10-01). Do not.
- **Ice underfoot while swinging an axe** is a bad combination. Traction first (→ 11-06).
- **Chainsaws start hard in deep cold**, like any small engine (→ 09-15).
- **This is the work you will do at the worst time, every winter.** Build the safe habits now,
  while it is easy.

**Substitutes:**
- **Axes and mauls** from estate sales and old sheds. Old axe heads are often excellent steel and
  can be re-handled (→ 16-08).
- **Handles:** buy spare hickory or ash handles, or shape one from a straight-grained hardwood
  limb.
- **Wedges:** hardwood wedges ("gluts") made with an axe work.
- **Sawbuck:** scrap lumber or lashed poles, half an hour (→ 16-03).
- **Peavey:** a stout pole as a lever.
- **What you cannot make: chaps, and hardened saw blades.** Buy both now.

## When it runs out

- **Saw blades are the bottleneck of the whole firewood plan** (→ 06-04). Bow-saw blades are
  disposable; stock many. **Chainsaw chains are the opposite**: they can be filed many times
  (→ 16-02), so stock files deep and chains moderately. **Own a traditional crosscut saw** as the
  tool that still works in year five.
- **Files wear out.** Stock several.
- **Chainsaw fuel and bar oil end.** Plan for the chainsaw as a first-year tool and the axe and
  bow saw as the long-term ones.
- **Handles break.** Stock spares and learn to re-hang a head (→ 16-08).
- **Count it:** blades per cord of wood and files per season. Put both on → 23-02.
- **Hand tools cost calories.** The axe and crosscut saw do everything the chainsaw does, slower
  and with far more effort, which matters when food is short (→ 04-18). Budget labor as well as
  tools.
- **The habits outlast the tools.** A person who splits safely, sharpens well and knows when to
  stop still has all their limbs in year three (→ 12-09).

## By phase

- **A, at home:** storm debris and downed limbs are your wood (→ 06-04). Set up the block and the
  sawbuck, keep the bleeding-control kit there, and enforce a child-free circle (→ 20-02).
  Chainsaw while fuel lasts; hand tools after.
- **B, on the move:** a folding saw and a hatchet, no more. Fires for warmth and cooking, not a
  winter supply.
- **C, settled:** the biggest wood workload and the biggest risk. Do not cut alone if you can avoid
  it, get real instruction before you need it, keep the kit at the work site, and build the
  sawbuck, block and wood shed properly in the first month (→ 01-04).
