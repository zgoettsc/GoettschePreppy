# Repairing metal and wood without power

**What this page is for.** Fixing tool handles and wooden structures, cutting, bending and joining
metal by hand, simple work with fire, and knowing what scrap steel is good for. The two skills
that matter most here, fitting a new tool handle and turning scrap steel into a working edge, mean
the tools in this section never truly run out: they get remade.

> **CAUTION. Four hazards on this page.**
> 1. **Never heat galvanized steel** (the dull grey, often spangled zinc-coated kind used on
>    fence posts, buckets and some pipe). The zinc gives off fumes that cause "metal fume fever"
>    and worse. **Galvanized: do not heat, do not weld, do not forge.** Check before anything
>    goes in a fire.
> 2. **Any forge or metalworking fire makes carbon monoxide** (→ 29-03; CDC, Carbon Monoxide
>    Poisoning Basics). **Never run a generator, charcoal, or any engine indoors, in a garage,
>    or near an open window** (floor, → 30-01): a charcoal forge counts. It goes outdoors.
> 3. **Hot metal and hammering:** burns (→ 12-03), flying scale, eye injuries. Eye protection,
>    leather gloves, and natural-fibre clothing; synthetics melt onto skin (→ 11-05).
> 4. **Lead.** Old paint, old solder and some salvaged metals contain lead (CDC, About Lead in
>    the Home). {{#if has.young_child}}**You have a child under 6, the age most harmed by lead.**
>    {{/if}}Do lead-adjacent work outdoors, away from children, and wash hands and clothes
>    afterward.

## Do this now

1. **Set up a vise and a solid work surface.** A bench vise is the item that most expands what you
   can repair (→ 16-01).
2. **Find a heavy block of steel to use as an anvil:** a railroad tie plate, a short piece of
   rail, a sledge head, a thick plate. Free or nearly free.
3. **Start a materials pile:** scrap steel, sheet metal, angle iron, rebar, pipe, vehicle leaf
   springs, hardwood, lumber. Sort it and keep it dry.
4. **Learn to re-hang an axe head** (below). It is the repair you are most likely to need (→ 16-06).

## Wood repair

### Re-hanging an axe or hammer head

{{figure fig-rehang-axe-head}}

1. **Remove the old handle.** Saw it off flush under the head, then drill out the wood left in
   the "eye" (the hole in the head) and punch it through. That is the safer first choice. If you
   must burn it out, **bury the whole cutting edge in damp sand or wet earth so only the eye and
   the wood stub sit in the coals.** Heat at the edge softens the steel and ruins the axe.
2. **Fit a new handle** of hickory or ash, straight-grained, with the grain lines running the same
   way as the blade. Shape the top to fit the eye tightly, taking off a little at a time with a
   rasp and trying it often.
3. **Drive it fully home**, then **wedge it**: a wooden wedge driven into the slot cut in the top of
   the handle, then a small steel wedge across it.
4. **Saw off the excess** and rub the handle with boiled linseed oil. **Linseed-oil rags heat up
   by themselves and can catch fire within hours when bunched.** Spread the rag flat outdoors to
   dry stiff, or drop it in a water-filled metal can with a lid (→ 16-01).
5. **Check it every season.** Wood shrinks in a dry, heated house, and **a loose head is
   dangerous.** Soaking the head in water swells it tight for a while; it is not a repair.

### Other wood repairs

- **Split or cracked piece:** glue and clamp, then **sister a board alongside and bolt through**
  (→ 16-05, → 16-07). A sistered repair is often stronger than the original.
- **Rotted post base:** cut out the rot and splice in new wood, or set a new post beside it. Keep
  wood off the ground; ground contact is what rotted it.
- **Loose joint:** wedges, glue, screws, a mending plate, or a lashing (→ 16-03).
- **Stripped screw hole:** matchsticks or toothpicks and glue, then drive the screw again (→ 16-05).
- **Making a handle from scratch:** a straight-grained hardwood limb, a draw knife and a rasp. A
  learnable and very useful skill.
- **Wooden pegs ("treenails")** in drilled holes, instead of metal fasteners: traditional, strong
  in the right joint, and free.
- **Green or dry wood:** freshly cut ("green") wood is easy to work but shrinks as it dries, which
  can tighten a joint or split a board. Dry wood is stable and harder to work. Plan around it.

## Metal repair without a welder

You can do a great deal with cold work and simple heat.

**Cold work:**
- **Cut** with a hacksaw (stock blades), a cold chisel, or tin snips.
- **Drill** with a hand drill and good bits. Go slowly and use oil.
- **Bend** in a vise, with a hammer, or over a form. Thick steel needs heat.
- **File** to shape. Files do a remarkable amount of work with patience (→ 16-02).
- **Join** with bolts, rivets or wire (→ 16-07). Bolted joints are easier to repair than welds.
- **Rivet:** a pop-rivet gun for sheet metal, or solid rivets hammered over ("peened") for real
  strength.

**With heat** (a fire, a charcoal forge or a torch), **outdoors only**:
- **Hot bending and shaping.** Steel becomes workable at red heat. A wood or charcoal fire with
  forced air (a bellows, a hand-crank blower, or a hair dryer if there is spare power) reaches it.
- **Straightening** bent steel.
- **Basic blacksmithing:** stretching steel thinner ("drawing out") and thickening it
  ("upsetting").
- **Brazing and soft soldering:** joining metal with a melted filler instead of a welder. Torch
  brazing is strong and worth learning.
- **Hardening and tempering** (heat-treating) need you to know the steel, and salvaged steel is
  unknown. Expect to experiment and expect failures. Leaf springs and files are reliable
  high-carbon sources.
- **Quenching** (cooling hot steel fast to harden it): use vegetable oil. **Used motor oil flashes
  into a fireball and its smoke is toxic.** Use a deep metal can with a lid to smother a flare-up,
  nothing flammable within arm's reach, and children nowhere near (→ 12-03).

**A basic forge is achievable:** an old brake drum or a fire pit, charcoal you can make (→ 06-05),
forced air, an anvil block, a hammer and tongs. It is a project for a settled phase, but knowing
it is possible changes what "the tools ran out" means.

## What scrap steel is good for

| Source | Steel | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| **Leaf springs, coil springs** | High-carbon, can be hardened | Knives, chisels, punches. The classic scrap steel |
| **Worn-out files** | Very high carbon | Knives, scrapers, small chisels. **Brittle**: soften by heating and cooling slowly before shaping |
| Rebar | Low carbon, unknown | Stakes, hooks. Will not harden well |
| Angle iron, channel, plate | Mild steel | Brackets, frames |
| Pipe | Mild steel (check it is not galvanized) | Handles, frames, rollers, levers |
| Sheet metal: appliances, ductwork, roofing, cans | Thin mild steel | Patches, flashing, containers |
| Bearings, axles, old tools | Various, often hardenable | Punches, chisels |
| Railroad tie plate or rail | Heavy mild steel | Anvil substitute |
| Copper pipe and wire | Soft | Electrical (→ 09-08), rivets, sheet work |
| Aluminium | Soft and light | Never where steel strength is needed; will not harden |

**The spark test:** touch a piece to a grinding wheel or rough stone. **High-carbon steel throws
bushy, branching sparks; mild steel throws long, straight streaks.** Crude, but it works.

## Rust

- **Prevent:** oil every steel tool each season (→ 16-01); prime and paint anything kept outdoors;
  keep things dry and off the ground.
- **Remove:** wire brush, sandpaper, a vinegar soak (slow but effective), or rust converter.
  Electrolysis with a battery charger works very well if there is spare power.
- **Protect at once.** Bare steel starts to rust within hours in humid air.
- **Rusted bolts:** penetrating oil, patience, and heat (not on galvanized), which breaks the rust
  bond (→ 16-05).

If you may move to another place (→ 01-05), what is already there changes what you carry.

**Only rely on a shop at a place whose people have agreed to have you.** At any place not agreed,
or improvised, assume there is no shop at all: your own vise and anvil block are the only ones.
That is why the vise and anvil are bought regardless (→ 16-01).

**The shop check** (run it on a visit, phone camera in hand, about 20 minutes; or on arrival day):

```
[ ] POWER: grid-down options? 240 V outlets? Generator hookup?
    (09-04, 09-13)
[ ] BENCH & VISE: solid bench? Vise size? Anvil or heavy plate?
[ ] WELDING / CUTTING: welder (type, rod or wire on hand)? Torch?
    WHO there knows how to run them?
[ ] MACHINES: drill press, bench grinder, compressor, chop saw?
[ ] HAND TOOLS: full mechanic's set? Wood tools? What duplicates
    ours, what is missing?
[ ] CONSUMABLES: welding rod, hacksaw blades, files, drill bits,
    fasteners, oil and grease, the things that run out (23-02)
[ ] STEEL & WOOD PILE: scrap steel, lumber, handle stock?
[ ] WHAT IS MISSING that we should bring or leave there ahead of time?
```

**What travels and what waits** (feeds the load plan, → 01-03):
- **Travels:** hand tools (→ 16-01), files, hacksaw blades, drill bits, the sharpening kit
  (→ 16-02), fasteners (→ 16-07), the light, valuable, always-scarce layer. Your vise and anvil
  block if there is any doubt about the shop.
- **Waits** at an agreed place: machines, the welder, the materials pile. Spend weight on the
  consumables no shop ever has enough of.
- **Leave duplicates ahead of time** only at a place that has agreed, a box at a time on visits.

**Substitutes:**
- **Anvil:** tie plate, rail section, a sledge head set in a stump, thick plate.
- **Forge:** a brake drum or a pit with forced air and charcoal (→ 06-05).
- **Tongs:** locking pliers, or tongs forged from rebar.
- **Steel:** leaf springs, files, angle iron, pipe, appliance panels (→ 22-02).
- **Handle wood:** hickory, ash, oak or elm, split and dried (→ 06-04 for the trees near you).
- **What you cannot make: hacksaw blades and files.** They gate all metalwork. Stock them.

## When it runs out

- **The consumables are hacksaw blades, files and drill bits.** Stock all three and add them to
  → 23-02.
- **Everything else is close to permanent:** vise, anvil, hammers, chisels, draw knife. Scrap steel
  and hardwood are plentiful (→ 22-02).
- **Make them last:** do not force a hacksaw (it strips teeth); clean files with a file card; turn a
  worn file into a knife blank instead of throwing it away.
- **Substitute:** cold work for hot; bolts for welds; wood where it will serve; lashings and pegs for
  hardware (→ 16-03, → 16-07).
- **The person who can make a handle and an edge** is valuable in any community (→ 18-03, → 23-05).

## By phase

- **A, at home:** set up the vise and materials pile; learn to re-hang a handle; collect scrap
  steel and hardwood while people still throw it away. Work with fire outdoors, with children well
  clear (→ 20-02).
- **B, on the move:** none of this travels. A file, a hacksaw blade and wire in the repair kit
  (→ 16-05).
- **C, settled:** this is where it becomes real. Run the shop check on arrival day. Where there is a
  shop, fit in and contribute consumables and hands; where there is none, set up your own vise and
  anvil in the first week (→ 01-04). Old farms are rich in salvageable steel. A household that can
  make and repair tools is the household others come to (→ 18-03).
