# Antibiotics by drug: what each covers, dangers, forms and storage

**What this page is for.** One entry for each antibiotic a household is likely to have or find:
what kind of drug it is, what it is used for, who must not take it, the dangers its FDA label
warns about, the forms and liquid strengths it comes in, and how to store it. **Doses are not
here.** The published doses for each infection are on → 12-22, row by row, with their
publishers. Fish and farm-animal products are at the end.

> **SAFETY**
> - **Check every person's allergies and medicines before any antibiotic** (→ 25-09). An
>   allergic reaction can follow any dose, including the first. Swelling of the face or throat,
>   wheezing, hives or collapse: stop the drug and treat as anaphylaxis (→ 02-03).
> - **Every antibiotic can cause C. diff**, a severe diarrhoea. The FDA labels say it "may range
>   in severity from mild diarrhea to fatal colitis" and "has been reported to occur over 2
>   months after" the antibiotic. Watery or bloody diarrhoea during or after a course is a
>   warning sign (→ 12-05).
> - **Antibiotics do not work on viruses** such as colds and flu (CDC, Antibiotic Do's and
>   Don'ts). Using them anyway causes side effects and resistance.
> - **This binder never works out or adjusts a dose**, including for kidney or liver disease.
> - **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products** (floor,
>   → 30-01).

Using antibiotics without a prescriber is a decision card: {{choice DC-16}} (→ 30-17).
Giving one person's prescription to another: {{choice DC-15}} (→ 30-16).

## Words on this page

- **Class**: a family of antibiotics that work the same way. Allergy to one member often means
  allergy to the family.
- **Beta-lactams**: the penicillins (penicillin V, amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate,
  dicloxacillin) and the cephalosporins (cephalexin, cefadroxil, cefdinir, cefuroxime,
  ceftriaxone).
- **Boxed warning**: the FDA's most serious label warning, printed in a box.
- **Contraindicated**: the label says do not use it in this situation.
- **QT prolongation**: a change in the heart's electrical rhythm that can cause a dangerous
  irregular heartbeat.
- **mg/5 mL**: milligrams of medicine in each 5 millilitres (one teaspoon) of a liquid.

## Penicillin allergy: what the sources say

- **Most reported penicillin allergies are not true allergies.** WHO: 5–15% of patients in
  high-income countries report one, but in most cases (> 95%) they "do not have a true
  immunologically mediated allergy" (WHO AWaRe, chapter 3).
- **Low risk** (WHO Table 3.3): past nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea or headache only; a mild rash
  especially more than 5 years ago; a family history of allergy.
- **High risk**: a past severe or life-threatening reaction (anaphylaxis, a blistering rash, or
  rash affecting the mouth or eyes), or reactions to several antibiotics.
- **WHO:** "Patients with a definite history of immediate collapse, breathing difficulties or
  severe facial swelling within a few minutes to 1–2 hours of taking a penicillin class of
  antibiotic are likely to have had a true anaphylactic reaction. If any alternative antibiotics
  are available, they are preferred."
- **Cross-reaction with cephalosporins.** WHO Table 3.2: less than 2%; "Cephalosporins can be
  safely used in most cases of penicillin allergy and vice versa", except after a previous
  life-threatening reaction, when "any use of beta-lactams should be avoided". The US sources are
  stricter: the amoxicillin FDA label is contraindicated after a serious reaction to "other
  β-lactam antibacterial drugs (e.g., penicillins and cephalosporins)", and IDSA advises avoiding
  cephalosporins in people with an immediate, anaphylactic-type penicillin allergy. Both are
  printed; neither is chosen here.
- **Amoxicillin rash with mononucleosis** (glandular fever) is not an allergy: "A high percentage
  of patients with mononucleosis who receive amoxicillin develop an erythematous skin rash.
  Thus, amoxicillin should not be administered to patients with mononucleosis" (amoxicillin
  label).

## Penicillins

### Amoxicillin

- **What it is:** a penicillin. WHO: it "has good activity against *Streptococcus pneumoniae*",
  the most common bacterial cause of ear, sinus and lung infections; higher doses still work
  against most resistant strains.
- **Main uses** (→ 12-22): strep throat (1), ear infection (2), sinusitis (3), pneumonia (4),
  dental infection (10), early Lyme disease (12). Not reliable alone for staph skin infections
  (WHO).
- **Do not use:** after a serious reaction (anaphylaxis or Stevens-Johnson syndrome, a severe
  blistering reaction) to amoxicillin or other beta-lactams (label); with mononucleosis (above).
- **Label warnings:** anaphylaxis; severe skin reactions; drug-induced enterocolitis syndrome
  (protracted vomiting 1 to 4 hours after a dose, mostly in children); C. diff.
- **Babies:** under 12 weeks, the label's "recommended upper dose of amoxicillin is 30
  mg/kg/day divided every 12 hours" because their kidneys are immature.
- **Pregnancy:** the label calls it Pregnancy Category B (no harm in animal studies; no
  adequate human studies) and says to use it only if clearly needed (→ 02-12).
- **Forms:** capsules 250 mg and 500 mg; tablets 500 mg and 875 mg; chewable tablets 125 mg and
  250 mg; oral suspension **200 mg/5 mL, 250 mg/5 mL, 400 mg/5 mL** (Teva amoxicillin label).
- **Storage:** 20° to 25°C (68° to 77°F). Mixed suspension: "Refrigeration is preferable, but
  not required"; "Any unused portion of the reconstituted suspension must be discarded after 14
  days." Take at the start of a meal to reduce stomach upset.

### Amoxicillin-clavulanate (brand Augmentin)

- **What it is:** amoxicillin plus clavulanic acid, which blocks an enzyme some bacteria use to
  destroy amoxicillin. Wider coverage, including the mouth germs in bites.
- **Main uses** (→ 12-22): ear infection (2), sinusitis (3), pneumonia with other illnesses (4),
  bladder infection (5), skin infection (7), wounds and bites (9, WHO's preferred bite drug).
- **Do not use:** after a serious reaction to any beta-lactam; after jaundice or liver problems
  from a previous course of amoxicillin-clavulanate (label).
- **Label warnings:** as amoxicillin, plus liver injury ("Discontinue if signs/symptoms of
  hepatitis occur"). Some liquids and chewables contain aspartame and "should not be used by
  phenylketonurics".
- **Tablets are not interchangeable.** "Two 250 mg/125 mg amoxicillin and clavulanate potassium
  tablets should NOT be substituted for one 500 mg/125 mg" tablet, because both contain the same
  125 mg of clavulanic acid. The 250 mg/125 mg tablet "should NOT be used in pediatric patients
  weighing less than 40 kg" (Teva amoxicillin-clavulanate label). Augmentin ES-600 (600 mg per
  5 mL) "does not contain the same amount of clavulanic acid" as other suspensions (ES-600
  label).
- **Forms:** tablets 500 mg/125 mg and 875 mg/125 mg; suspension **200 mg/28.5 mg per 5 mL and
  400 mg/57 mg per 5 mL**; chewables 200 mg/28.5 mg and 400 mg/57 mg (Teva label); ES-600
  suspension 600 mg per 5 mL.
- **Storage:** dry product 20° to 25°C. Mixed suspension "must be stored under refrigeration and
  discarded after 10 days." WHO adds that clavulanic acid breaks down quickly in heat.

### Penicillin V potassium (phenoxymethylpenicillin)

- **What it is:** the original oral penicillin. Strep remains "universally susceptible to
  penicillin" (WHO).
- **Main uses** (→ 12-22): strep throat (1), pneumonia (4, WHO), dental infection (10).
- **Do not use:** "A previous hypersensitivity reaction to any penicillin is a contraindication"
  (label).
- **Label warnings:** anaphylaxis ("SERIOUS ANAPHYLACTIC REACTIONS REQUIRE IMMEDIATE EMERGENCY
  TREATMENT WITH EPINEPHRINE"); C. diff.
- **Forms:** tablets 250 mg (400,000 units) and 500 mg (800,000 units); oral solution **125 mg
  (200,000 units) per 5 mL and 250 mg (400,000 units) per 5 mL** (Teva label).
- **Storage:** 20° to 25°C, tightly closed. Mixed solution "must be stored in a refrigerator.
  Discard any unused portion after 14 days."

### Dicloxacillin and cloxacillin

Penicillins made to resist staph's enzyme, for skin infections. WHO prints child doses for
cloxacillin and says dicloxacillin or flucloxacillin "could be used" if it is unavailable; IDSA
prints dicloxacillin doses (→ 12-22, sections 7 and 9). Same penicillin allergy rules. The FDA
label for dicloxacillin was not read for this binder.

## Cephalosporins

### Cephalexin (cefalexin)

- **What it is:** a first-generation cephalosporin; covers strep and staph (not MRSA).
- **Main uses** (→ 12-22): strep throat (1), skin infection (7), wounds (9); a penicillin-allergy
  choice in strep throat (IDSA), with the cross-reaction caution above.
- **Do not use:** known allergy to cephalexin or other cephalosporins (label).
- **Label warnings:** allergic reactions; C. diff; seizures, "particularly in patients with renal
  impairment when the dosage was not reduced"; prolonged blood-clotting time, especially with
  kidney or liver disease, poor nutrition or blood thinners (→ 02-10). **Interaction:** raises metformin levels
  (diabetes medicine; "Careful patient monitoring and dose adjustment of metformin is
  recommended").
- **Pregnancy:** the label says decades of use "have not established drug-associated risks of
  major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse maternal or fetal outcomes".
- **Forms:** capsules 250 mg and 500 mg; oral suspension **125 mg/5 mL and 250 mg/5 mL** (Teva
  labels).
- **Storage:** capsules 20° to 25°C. Mixed suspension: "store in refrigerator. May be kept for 14
  days without significant loss of potency."

### Cefdinir

- **What it is:** a third-generation oral cephalosporin.
- **Main uses** (→ 12-22): ear infection in a child with non-severe penicillin allergy (2, FDA
  label rows).
- **Do not use:** "known allergy to the cephalosporin class of antibiotics" (label).
- **Label warnings:** allergic reactions (careful inquiry about penicillin allergy first); C.
  diff. Diarrhoea was more common in children 2 years and under. **Interaction:** aluminium- or
  magnesium-containing antacids cut absorption by about 40%.
- **Forms:** oral suspension **125 mg/5 mL and 250 mg/5 mL** (the label also refers to 300 mg
  capsules).
- **Storage:** dry powder 20° to 25°C; mixed suspension "can be stored at controlled room
  temperature for 10 days" (cefdinir suspension label).

### Cefuroxime axetil

- **What it is:** a second-generation oral cephalosporin.
- **Main uses** (→ 12-22): early Lyme disease (12, CDC); pneumonia with other illnesses (4,
  ATS/IDSA).
- **Do not use:** known serious allergy to cefuroxime or other beta-lactams (label).
- **Forms are not interchangeable:** "Cefuroxime axetil tablets and cefuroxime axetil for oral
  suspension are not bioequivalent and are therefore not substitutable on a milligram-per-
  milligram basis." Tablets must be swallowed whole; crushed, they have "a strong, persistent
  bitter taste" (Aurobindo cefuroxime axetil label).
- **Storage:** 20° to 25°C.

### Cefadroxil and ceftriaxone

Cefadroxil is an oral cephalosporin listed by IDSA for penicillin-allergic strep throat and by
TCCC for battlefield wounds (→ 12-24). Ceftriaxone is an injection (into a vein or muscle) used in
hospitals and by military medics. Their FDA labels were not read for this binder.

## Tetracyclines

### Doxycycline

- **What it is:** a tetracycline; covers many skin, chest and tick-borne infections and MRSA in
  many places.
- **Main uses** (→ 12-22): pneumonia in adults (4), sinusitis with penicillin allergy (3, IDSA),
  MRSA abscess (8), cholera (11), early Lyme disease (12), Rocky Mountain spotted fever (13).
- **Do not use:** "hypersensitivity to any of the tetracyclines" (label).
- **Children under 8 and late pregnancy:** "The use of drugs of the tetracycline class during
  tooth development (last half of pregnancy, infancy and childhood to the age of 8 years) may
  cause permanent discoloration of the teeth (yellow-gray-brown)." The label says to use it
  under 8 years "only when the potential benefits are expected to outweigh the risks in severe or
  life-threatening conditions (e.g., anthrax, Rocky Mountain spotted fever), particularly when
  there are no alternative therapies." CDC lists it for children of all ages for Rocky Mountain
  spotted fever and gives a child dose for Lyme disease (→ 12-22).
- **Other label warnings:** C. diff; severe skin reactions; raised pressure in the skull (headache,
  blurred or double vision; "the possibility for permanent visual loss exists"); severe sunburn
  ("discontinue at the first evidence of skin erythema"); throat ulcers if swallowed without
  water or before bed ("drink fluids liberally").
- **Interactions:** absorption is cut by antacids containing aluminium, calcium or magnesium,
  by iron, and by bismuth subsalicylate (Pepto-Bismol). Can increase the effect of warfarin
  (→ 02-10). May make birth-control pills less effective. Barbiturates, carbamazepine and
  phenytoin shorten its action.
- **Forms:** capsules and tablets (several strengths); oral suspension **25 mg/5 mL** (Chartwell
  doxycycline label).
- **Storage:** 20° to 25°C.

## Macrolides

### Azithromycin (brand Zithromax)

- **What it is:** a macrolide; long-acting, so courses are short.
- **Main uses** (→ 12-22): strep throat with penicillin allergy (1), ear infection with
  penicillin allergy (2), pneumonia (4), traveller's diarrhoea and dysentery (11), cholera (11),
  newborn chlamydia eye infection (14).
- **Do not use:** known allergy to azithromycin, erythromycin or any macrolide; after jaundice or
  liver problems from a previous course (label).
- **Label warnings:** fatal allergic and skin reactions; severe liver injury; **QT prolongation
  and torsades de pointes**, a risk "which can be fatal", especially with known QT prolongation,
  slow heart rhythms, heart failure, low potassium or magnesium, other QT-prolonging drugs, and in
  the elderly; an observational "approximately two-fold increased short-term potential risk of
  acute cardiovascular death in adults"; in newborns up to 42 days, infantile hypertrophic
  pyloric stenosis (projectile vomiting, irritability with feeding); C. diff.
- **Resistance:** strep resistance to azithromycin "is well known and varies by region" (IDSA);
  CDC does not recommend macrolides for sinusitis.
- **Interactions:** warfarin (may increase bleeding time, → 02-10); nelfinavir.
- **Forms:** tablets 250 mg and 500 mg; oral suspension **100 mg/5 mL and 200 mg/5 mL** (Pfizer
  Zithromax label).
- **Storage:** tablets 15° to 30°C (59° to 86°F). Mixed suspension 5° to 30°C; "use within 10
  days. Discard after full dosing is completed."

### Clarithromycin and erythromycin

Other macrolides. WHO and IDSA print clarithromycin doses for strep throat and IDSA/PIDS for
children's pneumonia (→ 12-22). Their FDA labels were not read for this binder; they share the
macrolide QT warning in kind, and the FDA has named erythromycin among unapproved fish
antibiotics (below).

## Sulfonamides

### Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX, co-trimoxazole; brand Bactrim)

- **What it is:** two drugs in one tablet. **Double strength (DS)** = 800 mg sulfamethoxazole +
  160 mg trimethoprim; **single strength** = 400 mg + 80 mg (Bactrim label). Child doses are
  usually written by the trimethoprim part.
- **Main uses** (→ 12-22): bladder infection (5), kidney infection if the germ is known to be
  susceptible (6), MRSA abscess (8), diarrhoea (11, WHO second choice).
- **Do not use** (label): sulfa or trimethoprim allergy; past low platelets from these drugs;
  folate-deficiency anaemia; **babies under 2 months**; marked liver damage; severe kidney
  disease without monitoring; with dofetilide.
- **Not for strep:** "The sulfonamides should not be used for treatment of group A β-hemolytic
  streptococcal infections" (label). Do not use it for strep throat.
- **Label warnings:** fatal skin reactions (Stevens-Johnson, toxic epidermal necrolysis, DRESS),
  liver failure, blood disorders, lung injury and shock. "BACTRIM should be discontinued at the
  first appearance of skin rash." High potassium, especially with kidney problems or blood
  pressure medicines called ACE inhibitors. Drink plenty to prevent crystals in the urine.
- **Pregnancy:** "may be associated with an increased risk of congenital malformations,
  particularly neural tube defects" (label). WHO: "not recommended in the first trimester of
  pregnancy" (→ 02-12).
- **Interactions:** warfarin (longer bleeding time), methotrexate, ACE inhibitors (high
  potassium).
- **Storage:** 20° to 25°C.

## Urinary antiseptic

### Nitrofurantoin (brand Macrobid, Macrodantin)

- **What it is:** works only in the bladder; useless for kidney infection or anything outside the
  urinary tract.
- **Main use** (→ 12-22): bladder infection (5). "Not indicated for the treatment of
  pyelonephritis" (Macrobid label).
- **Do not use** (Macrobid label): significant kidney impairment; pregnant women at term (38 to 42
  weeks), in labour, or when labour is near; babies under one month; past jaundice from
  nitrofurantoin; known allergy. "Safety and effectiveness in pediatric patients below the age
  of twelve years have not been established" for Macrobid capsules (WHO prints child doses for
  nitrofurantoin generally, → 12-22).
- **Label warnings:** acute, subacute or chronic lung reactions, "a contributing cause of death"
  in reports (fever, chills, cough, chest pain, breathlessness in the first week; scarring with
  months of use); liver injury; nerve damage that "may become severe or irreversible"; anaemia
  in people with G6PD deficiency. Urine may turn brown.
- **Interactions:** antacids containing magnesium trisilicate reduce absorption. Take with food.
- **Forms:** Macrobid capsules 100 mg.
- **Storage:** 15° to 30°C.

## Fluoroquinolones

### Ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin

- **What they are:** fluoroquinolones; strong against many gut and urinary germs.
- **Main uses** (→ 12-22): kidney infection (6), dysentery and traveller's diarrhoea (11),
  sinusitis or pneumonia with penicillin allergy (levofloxacin, 3 and 4).
- **Boxed warning (both labels):** "disabling and potentially irreversible serious adverse
  reactions that have occurred together", including tendinitis and tendon rupture, peripheral
  neuropathy (nerve damage) and central nervous system effects; may worsen myasthenia gravis.
  "Discontinue CIPRO immediately" at the first sign. Because of these, the label says to reserve
  ciprofloxacin for people with "no alternative treatment options" for bronchitis flare-ups,
  uncomplicated bladder infection and sinusitis.
- **Tendons:** risk is higher over 60 years, with corticosteroids (prednisone), and after kidney,
  heart or lung transplant; rupture can come "as long as several months after" the course. Stop
  at tendon pain or swelling and rest the limb.
- **Other label warnings:** aortic aneurysm and dissection (a tear in the body's main artery)
  "within two months" of use, especially in the elderly; QT prolongation; severe low blood sugar
  in people on diabetes medicines, including "coma or death"; mental effects; sun sensitivity; C.
  diff; liver injury.
- **Children:** ciprofloxacin is approved under 18 only for complicated urinary infection,
  inhalational anthrax and plague; levofloxacin only for anthrax and plague; both labels report
  more joint problems in children and permanent cartilage damage in young animals. WHO
  nonetheless prints child ciprofloxacin doses (→ 12-22, Table W5); both are printed.
- **Do not use:** quinolone allergy; ciprofloxacin with tizanidine (a muscle relaxant). Avoid
  with a history of myasthenia gravis or of a previous serious fluoroquinolone reaction.
- **Interactions:** take ciprofloxacin "at least 2 hours before or 6 hours after" antacids with
  magnesium or aluminium and products with calcium, iron or zinc; not with milk, yogurt or
  calcium-fortified juice alone. Raises theophylline levels (serious reactions). Levofloxacin:
  2 hours before or after such products.
- **Forms:** CIPRO tablets 250 mg and 500 mg; oral suspension **5% (250 mg/5 mL) and 10% (500
  mg/5 mL)** (Bayer label). Levofloxacin tablets 250 mg, 500 mg and 750 mg.
- **Storage:** 20° to 25°C.

## Lincosamide

### Clindamycin (brand Cleocin)

- **What it is:** the label lists serious infections from anaerobic germs (those that live
  without air) and from streptococci, pneumococci and staphylococci, and says "Its use should be
  reserved for penicillin-allergic patients or other patients for whom ... a penicillin is
  inappropriate". IDSA lists it for MRSA skin infection.
- **Main uses** (→ 12-22): strep throat with penicillin allergy (1), skin infection and MRSA
  abscess (7, 8), sinusitis in children with penicillin allergy (3, with a cephalosporin).
- **Boxed warning:** C. diff. "Because CLEOCIN HCl therapy has been associated with severe colitis
  which may end fatally, it should be reserved for serious infections where less toxic
  antimicrobial agents are inappropriate"; not for "most upper respiratory tract infections".
- **Do not use:** allergy to clindamycin or lincomycin (label).
- **Swallowing:** take capsules "with a full glass of water (6 to 8 ounces, approximately 200 to
  250 mL) and at least 30 minutes before lying down" to avoid throat ulcers. The 75 mg and 150 mg
  capsules contain tartrazine, which can cause allergic-type reactions.
- **Forms:** capsules 75 mg, 150 mg, 300 mg (Cleocin HCl label); Cleocin Pediatric granules,
  **75 mg per 5 mL** when mixed.
- **Storage:** 20° to 25°C. Mixed Cleocin Pediatric: "Do NOT refrigerate the reconstituted
  solution"; "stable for 2 weeks at room temperature."

## Nitroimidazole

### Metronidazole (brand Flagyl)

- **What it is:** the label lists trichomoniasis, amoebic dysentery and amoebic liver abscess,
  and serious infections from anaerobic germs (those that live without air), with surgery where
  needed.
- **Main uses:** gum and soft-tissue dental infections (→ 12-22, section 10, WHO footnote). C.
  diff infection and abdominal infections are hospital uses in WHO's dosing tables.
- **Boxed warning:** "Metronidazole has been shown to be carcinogenic in mice and rats.
  Unnecessary use of the drug should be avoided" (Flagyl label).
- **Do not use** (label): allergy to metronidazole or similar drugs; **with alcohol** during and
  for at least three days after the course (cramps, vomiting, headache, flushing); disulfiram
  within the last two weeks; Cockayne syndrome; for trichomoniasis in the first trimester of
  pregnancy (→ 02-12).
- **Label warnings:** brain and nerve effects (unsteady walking, dizziness, slurred speech,
  seizures, numbness or tingling); the label says their appearance "demands the prompt evaluation
  of the benefit/risk ratio of the continuation of therapy".
- **Interactions:** increases warfarin's effect (→ 02-10); raises lithium levels; busulfan.
- **Storage:** follow the bottle's label.

## Forms you will find, and what to check on every bottle

- **The name and the strength.** Many antibiotics come in several strengths and in liquids of
  different mg per 5 mL (above). Write the strength in your log with every dose (→ 25-05).
  Reading a label: → 29-14.
- **Combination products differ.** Two half-strength amoxicillin-clavulanate tablets are not one
  full-strength tablet; cefuroxime tablets and liquid are not swapped milligram for milligram.
- **Measure liquids with a dosing syringe**, not a kitchen spoon. The amoxicillin label: "use a
  calibrated oral syringe."
- **Shake suspensions** before each dose (labels).
- **Eye drops and ointments** (gentamicin, ofloxacin, tetracycline) are for eyes only (→ 12-22,
  section 14).

## Storage and expiry

- **Dry tablets and capsules:** the labels above say 20° to 25°C (68° to 77°F) for most, in the
  closed original container; azithromycin tablets and Macrobid 15° to 30°C. Keeping medicines
  inside their label range when the power is out: → 12-07.
- **Mixed liquids have short lives,** fixed by their labels: amoxicillin 14 days; penicillin V 14
  days in the fridge; cephalexin 14 days in the fridge; amoxicillin-clavulanate and ES-600 10 days
  in the fridge; cefdinir 10 days at room temperature; azithromycin 10 days; Cleocin Pediatric 2
  weeks at room temperature, not refrigerated. Mix a bottle only when it will be used.
- **Past the printed date.** In a federal testing programme many properly stored medicines kept
  their strength past the printed date, but it varied from lot to lot and could only be confirmed
  by testing (Lyon and others, 2006). Whether your household uses expired medicine when nothing
  else exists is a decision card: {{choice DC-04}} (→ 30-05).

## Fish, bird and farm-animal antibiotics: what is known and unknown

People who stockpile often buy antibiotics sold for aquarium fish, pet birds or livestock,
because they are sold without a prescription and the capsules can look like human ones. This
binder does not recommend them. Here is what the FDA has said, and what nobody can know.

**What the FDA has said** (FDA, "FDA Warns Nine Manufacturers, Distributors of Unapproved
Antimicrobials for Animals", 14 December 2023):
- The products were "marketed and labeled for minor species such as aquarium fish and pet birds"
  and are "unapproved and misbranded antimicrobial animal drugs".
- They contained antimicrobials important in human medicine, "such as amoxicillin, penicillin,
  tetracycline, and erythromycin".
- The FDA "has not verified their safety or effectiveness". FDA's Center for Veterinary Medicine
  director: "we don't know whether they're safe or effective, or even contain what the label
  states."
- "Using unapproved animal drugs in humans can be especially dangerous." "Self-diagnosis and
  treatment with unapproved animal antimicrobials could delay effective treatment, allowing
  infections to become more severe and/or more resistant to antimicrobials."
- FDA advice: dispose of these products safely; animal owners should consult a veterinarian and
  people a licensed health care provider.

**What is unknown about any such bottle:** whether it contains the drug named, at the strength
named; what else is in it (fillers, contaminants); how it was made and stored; and whether its
date means anything. Nothing on an animal label is a human dose, and no human dose in this
binder (→ 12-22) can be assumed to match an animal product. Antibiotics approved for farm animals
or pets are made and labelled for those animals; this binder found no FDA guidance on their use
in people.
