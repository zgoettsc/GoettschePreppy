# Kit tiers: everyday, trauma and long-haul

**What this page is for.** Where each medical kit lives and why, so the right supplies are
within reach in the time each emergency allows. What goes in the kits is on → 12-01.

> **Put each kit where its emergency happens, and match the reach time to the injury's
> clock.** Severe bleeding is a **minutes** problem: that kit must be at the woodpile, not in
> a closet. A cut finger is a **whenever** problem: that kit lives in the kitchen, where it is
> used weekly.
>
> **Life-critical medicines are not in any of these kits.** Epinephrine, insulin, glucagon,
> rescue inhalers and seizure medicines have their own known, instantly reachable place and
> travel with their person (→ 12-11). A life-saving drug buried in a general bag is one you
> cannot find in ninety seconds.

## Do this now

1. **Count what you own** and write it on → 22-04. Then build the first trauma kit from the
   "buy first" list on → 12-01. Everything else here assumes that kit exists.
2. **Draw a map of the house and yard and mark where each kit lives.** Photograph it and add it
   below. Every adult must know it by heart.
3. **Build the trauma kit for the work area first** (where wood is cut). That is where the
   worst likely injury happens (→ 16-06).
4. **Label every kit on the outside with its contents**, so someone who is not you can find
   things under stress.
5. **Set a twice-a-year check** on the calendar: expiry dates, used-up items, batteries
   (→ 22-04, → 25-10).

## The five kits

**1. Everyday kit: kitchen, used weekly.**
The one that actually gets opened. Adhesive bandages in every size, antiseptic wipes,
tweezers, small scissors, antibiotic ointment, blister care, OTC pain relief, a thermometer,
gloves{{#if has.child}}, children's bandages{{/if}}. **Restock it constantly**: the everyday kit
is always the one that is empty when you need it. Keep it in a visible, obvious place.

**2. Trauma kits: where injuries happen. Several, small, fast.**
For serious bleeding and nothing else. **Small, bright, obvious, reachable in seconds.**
- Tourniquet, pressure bandage, hemostatic and compressed gauze, gloves, trauma shears, a
  marker for writing the tourniquet time. **Never loosen a tourniquet once placed while help can
  still come; mark the time. If no help will ever come, the conversion decision is DC-25
  (→ 30-26).** That is a floor (→ 30-01); how to use the kit is → 12-02.
- **Locations: the work area or woodpile, the vehicle, the go-bag, and one in the house.**
- **Do not make them big.** A trauma kit you have to dig through fails at its one job.
- **Everyone who could be the responder has trained** on it (Stop the Bleed).

**3. Vehicle kit: everything at once, because the vehicle is where you get stranded.**
Trauma kit plus everyday supplies plus warmth.{{#if is.cold_winters}} In your winters, the
vehicle kit is as much a cold-survival kit as a medical one (→ 11-01, → 17-01): blankets, hats,
mittens, hand warmers, water, food, a light.{{else}} Add blankets, water, food and a light
(→ 11-01).{{/if}} Check it every season, and remember that medicines in a vehicle face both
heat and cold (→ 12-07).

**4. Go-bag medical module: light, compact, life-critical first** (→ 22-03).
**The priority is each person's life-critical medicines and supplies** (→ 12-11, → 01-07).
Then a compact general kit: pain relief, ORS packets, antihistamine, anti-diarrhoea medicine,
antibiotic ointment, blister care, gauze, tape, gloves and a tourniquet. **Weight discipline
matters**: everything competes with water.

**5. Long-haul stock: the bulk supply, stored and counted.**
Boxes of gauze, rolls of tape, cases of ORS, bulk OTC medicines, splint material, soap, gloves.
**This depth is what carries you through months, and it is the difference between a household
with a first-aid kit and one with a medical plan.**
- Cool, dark, dry, above freezing (→ 12-07).
- **Counted on paper** with expiry dates (→ 22-04).
- Rotated first in, first out.

## Placement by emergency

| Emergency | How fast it kills | The kit must be |
|---|---|---|
| **Severe bleeding** | **Minutes** | At the point of injury: work area, vehicle, go-bag |
| **Severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)** | **Minutes** | Epinephrine in its own known place, travelling with its person (→ 02-03; CDC, Recognizing and Responding to Anaphylaxis) |
| **Burn** | Minutes to hours | Kitchen and near the stove; **cooling water first** (→ 12-03) |
| Cut or scrape | Whenever | Kitchen everyday kit |
| Illness | Hours to days | Long-haul stock |
| Long-term medicine | Continuous | → 12-07, → 12-11 |

## Usable by someone other than you

In an emergency, the person who built the kits may be the injured one.
- **Label the outside** of every kit with a contents list.
- **Put a laminated card inside each trauma kit** with the basic sequence from → 12-02. It is a
  prompt under stress, not a substitute for training.
{{#if has.child}}- **Teach the children where the kits are and how to get an adult** (→ 20-04).
{{/if}}- **Every adult trains, and every adult knows the locations.**
- **Bright colours and matching containers** so each kit is recognisable at a glance.

## Common failures

- **One giant bag with everything in it.** Too heavy to move, too slow to search, never where
  the injury is.
- **The trauma kit in a closet.** It might as well not exist.
- **Never restocking the everyday kit**, so it is empty in the real emergency.
- **Nothing in the vehicle.**
- **No inventory**, so expired items sit for years and shortages go unnoticed.
- **Life-critical medicines buried in a general kit.** They need instant, known access.
- **Kits nobody but you can use.**

## Scavenge or substitute

- **Containers:** metal ammunition cans (waterproof, tough, obvious), tool boxes, dry bags,
  tackle boxes, clear plastic bins, gallon zip bags inside a backpack.
- **Labels:** tape and a marker. **Do it now**; unlabelled kits are half-useless.
- **Vehicle kit container:** a small duffel you can carry away if you end up walking.
- **Prompt cards:** index cards in a zip bag.

## When it runs out

- **The everyday kit runs out constantly.** That is normal and it is the signal to draw from the
  long-haul stock. Track it: "the bandages are gone" is easy to notice and easy to keep not
  fixing.
- **The long-haul stock decides how long you can manage injury and illness.** Estimate in
  **dressing changes and sick-days**, not in items (→ 12-02, → 12-05, → 23-02).
- **Ration:** as the long-haul stock falls, move the everyday kit toward washable cloth
  dressings (→ 12-02) and keep sterile commercial materials for fresh, deep or dirty wounds.
  **Never rob the trauma kit to restock the everyday kit.** That is the discipline that matters.
- **Resupply:** scavenging (→ 22-02), trade (→ 18-02), cloth from the household's fabric
  (→ 11-05).
- **Organisation itself is free.** Even with almost no supplies, the right things in the right
  places, and people who know where they are, is most of the value.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** all five kits placed, mapped, labelled and counted. Restock the everyday kit
  weekly from the long-haul stock.
- **B (moving):** the go-bag module and vehicle kit are what you have. **Life-critical medicines
  are the core**, and everything else competes with water and warmth for weight. Keep a
  tourniquet reachable one-handed, not at the bottom of a pack.
- **C (resettled):** rebuild the same structure: a trauma kit at every work area, an everyday
  kit in the living space, long-haul stock dry and above freezing. **Map and label again**; a
  kit nobody can find in an unfamiliar building is a kit you do not have.
