# Wound closure, drainage, and when not to close

**What this page is for.** Deciding whether a wound should be closed at all (usually not), the
safest ways to close the few that should be, packing an open wound, and why draining an abscess
is a procedure, not a home trick.

> **SAFETY**
> - **The most important thing on this page is a negative: most wounds in a long emergency
>   should not be closed.**
> - Suture kits are popular with preppers and are **one of the items most likely to cause harm
>   in untrained hands.** **Closing a dirty wound traps germs inside**, turning a wound that would
>   have healed slowly into an abscess, a deep infection or sepsis (→ 12-04), with no surgeon and
>   perhaps no antibiotics.
> - **The axe wound, the chainsaw wound, the animal bite, the puncture: the wounds a long
>   emergency produces are almost all wounds to leave open.**
> - **This page teaches no stitching or cutting technique.** Get *Where There Is No Doctor*, and
>   if you want real skill, get real training before you need it (→ 24-01).

## Do this now: should I close this wound?

1. **Clean it first, thoroughly** (→ 12-02). Always.
2. **Run the "do not close" list below.** Any one item: **leave it open**, pack it loosely, dress
   it.
3. **Clean, fresh, shallow, straight edges that meet without pulling, nothing on the list?**
   Closure strips or skin glue may be reasonable.
4. **Unsure? Leave it open.** A bigger scar is acceptable; a trapped infection is not.
5. **Check tetanus** (→ 12-04).

## Do this now: preparation

- **Learn the "do not close" list.** It is the part that saves people, and it is free.
- **Buy wound-closure strips and skin glue, not a suture kit**, as your first purchase. They
  handle the wounds a household should actually close, and they trap infection far less.
- **Buy far more gauze and flushing supplies than closure supplies** (→ 12-02). Cleaning and
  packing is what you will actually do.
- **Bring everyone's tetanus vaccination up to date** (→ 12-04; CDC, About Tetanus).

## Why an open wound is often the safer wound

A wound heals in one of two ways:
- **Closed (primary closure):** edges brought together; heals fast with a small scar. **Only
  right for clean, fresh, well-cleaned wounds.**
- **Left open (healing by secondary intention):** packed and allowed to fill in from the bottom.
  **Slower and uglier, and far safer for anything dirty.**

**With no surgeon and few antibiotics, leaving it open is your default.**

## The do-not-close list: learn it by heart

**Do not close a wound that is:**
- **Dirty**: soil, manure, rust, wood, bark, gravel, cloth fibres
- **A puncture**: deep, narrow, impossible to clean; the classic tetanus and abscess risk
- **An animal or human bite**: **always** dirty, always high infection risk (→ 20-09, → 05-15)
- **More than several hours old**: germs have multiplied
- **Already showing signs of infection** (→ 12-04)
- **Deep, with damage you cannot see the bottom of**
- **From crushing**, with damaged tissue at the edges
- **Over a joint, or where the edges will not line up**
- **Somewhere you are not sure what lies underneath** (tendons, nerves, arteries)
- **On the face, hands or genitals**: these need proper care for function and appearance; seek
  real care if it exists

**In other words: nearly everything an axe, a chainsaw, an animal or a fall on rough ground
produces.**

## The narrow case where closing is reasonable

- **Clean and fresh** (within a few hours) and thoroughly flushed (→ 12-02)
- **Straight edges** that meet easily without pulling
- **Not deep**, nothing left in it, no sign of dirt
- Somewhere a gaping wound would cause real trouble
- **You are sure what is underneath**

**Even then, closure strips or skin glue usually do the job**, and they have a big safety
advantage: **they come off instantly** if the wound starts to look infected. Removing stitches
from a swollen infected wound is far worse.

## Closure options, safest first

1. **Nothing**: clean, dress and let it heal open. Right for most wounds in a long emergency.
2. **Closure strips** (thin adhesive strips laid across the cut): no needle, easy to remove, good
   for shallow clean cuts. **Your main closure tool.**
3. **Skin glue (medical adhesive):** good for clean wounds that close without pulling. **Never
   inside a wound**: it goes on the surface across the closed edges.
4. **Stitches or staples:** real capability, real risk, **real training required.** Not from a
   book. If nobody in your household or community is trained, this option does not exist for
   you; the first three still do.

## Packing an open wound

What you will do far more often than closing.
- **Flush thoroughly first** (→ 12-02): volume and pressure.
- **Pack loosely** with clean gauze so the wound stays open at the surface and heals from the
  bottom up. **A wound that seals over at the skin while still infected underneath is how
  abscesses form.**
- **Change the packing regularly and look at the wound every time.**
- **Watch for infection signs** at every change (→ 12-04).
- A significant wound needs this for **weeks**. That is a lot of dressing material (→ 12-02).

## Abscesses and drainage

A closed pocket of pus does not clear with antibiotics alone; **it needs to drain.** Draining it
is a historically standard treatment, and it is also a **procedure**:
- **Location matters enormously.** An abscess on the face, neck, hand or groin, or near large
  blood vessels, is dangerous to open and can go badly wrong.
- **Some swellings are not abscesses**, and cutting into them causes serious harm.
- **After draining, the cavity is packed and heals from the bottom**, as above.
- **Warm compresses** may bring a shallow abscess to a head so it drains by itself. Try that
  first.
- **This binder gives no cutting procedure.** Read a real reference in advance and get hands-on
  training if you want the skill. Tooth abscesses: → 12-20.

## Aftercare: where the outcome is decided

- **Clean hands and gloves at every dressing change** (CDC, About Hygiene). This is the whole
  game.
- **Look at the wound every time.** Less redness and pain is normal; more is a warning (→ 12-04).
- **Keep it clean and slightly moist.** Modern practice favours moist healing over letting a wound
  dry into a hard scab.
- **Honey** on suitable clean wounds has traditional and evidence-supported use. **Never for a
  baby under 12 months** (infant botulism).
- **Support the person:** food, protein, fluids, warmth, sleep. A well-fed, warm, rested person
  heals much better.
- **Keep wounds over joints still** so they are not reopened again and again.
- **Tetanus** (CDC, About Tetanus): no home treatment once it starts. **The booster decision is
  the part you can act on: → 12-04 prints the CDC rule** (clean minor wound: booster if 10 or
  more years since the last dose; any other wound: if 5 or more years, within 48 to 72 hours;
  fewer than 3 lifetime doses plus a dirty wound: tetanus immune globulin too).

## The realistic picture

With clean water, careful flushing, clean dressings and patience, **a household can manage a
great many wounds well.** People did it for centuries. What they could not do, and you cannot
either, is rescue a wound closed over dirt, or an infection that has spread through the body.
**The discipline of not closing is the most valuable thing on this page, and it costs nothing.**

## Scavenge or substitute

- **Closure strips:** thin strips of strong medical tape (cloth or paper) laid across the wound.
  **Works surprisingly well** on shallow clean cuts. Never duct tape directly on skin.
- **Packing:** clean cotton cloth, boiled, dried and torn into strips (→ 12-02).
- **Flushing:** any squeeze bottle, a bag with a pinhole, a turkey baster, plus clean drinkable
  water.
- **Honey and sugar** for suitable wounds: both have real traditional use.
- **Instruments:** sterilised by boiling for a sustained time.
- **What you cannot improvise:** the judgement about **whether** to close, and the training to do
  it. That is why the negative rule at the top is the real content of this page.

## When it runs out

- **Packing gauze is the consumable that matters.** One significant wound over weeks uses an
  enormous amount. Count in dressing changes (→ 12-02, → 23-02).
- **Ration:** boil and reuse cloth for packing and outer layers; save sterile commercial material
  for the first days and the deepest, dirtiest wounds.
- **Resupply:** cloth is plentiful (→ 11-05); scavenged medical supplies (→ 22-02); trade
  (→ 18-02).
- **Substitute:** **clean water, clean cloth and clean hands stand in for nearly everything
  here.** They do not stand in for surgery, or for the judgement about when a wound is beyond you
  (→ 12-16).
- **The permanent answer is prevention.** Sharp tools (→ 16-02), correct technique (→ 16-06),
  chaps and gloves, and no dangerous work when cold, tired or rushed. The wound you do not get
  needs no dressings.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** clean water is plentiful; **flush generously.** This is the time to read the
  reference and, if you want the skill, get real training while it exists.
- **B (moving):** water for flushing is scarce exactly when dirt is worst. **Clean as well as you
  can, close nothing, dress it, and clean it properly at the next real water source.**
- **C (resettled):** more wounds from axes, animals and building, further from help. **Stock
  packing deeply**, keep the flushing kit near the work areas, and hold the do-not-close line even
  when a wound looks tempting.
