# Common illness, diarrhoea and dehydration

**What this page is for.** Recognising dehydration, treating it with oral rehydration
solution, knowing when it is beyond home care, and the other common illnesses of a long
emergency.

> **SAFETY**
> - **Diarrhoeal illness is one of the most likely and most dangerous things that will happen
>   to a household in a long emergency.** Worse sanitation, doubtful water, food kept without
>   a fridge and less handwashing all arrive at once. **Worldwide, diarrhoeal disease is the
>   third leading cause of death in children under 5**, and WHO's treatment is **oral
>   rehydration solution (ORS), a solution of clean water, sugar and salt** (WHO, Diarrhoeal
>   disease fact sheet). A baby or toddler can become dangerously dehydrated quickly.
> - **Some conditions make it worse.** Vomiting and diarrhoea in someone with diabetes is a
>   sick-day emergency risk (CDC, Managing Sick Days; → 02-01, → 02-05). Kidney disease, adrenal
>   insufficiency and some heart and blood-pressure medicines also change the rules (→ 12-11).
> - **This page prints one recipe: the World Health Organization's home oral rehydration
>   solution.** Commercial packets, mixed as their label says, come first. The printed recipe is
>   the backstop, and its safety rules (level spoons, measured litre, taste check) are part of
>   the recipe.
> - **Nothing by mouth** to anyone unconscious, too drowsy to swallow safely, or unable to
>   swallow.

## Do this now: someone has diarrhoea or vomiting

1. **Start oral rehydration solution (ORS)** from a packet, mixed with safe water exactly as the
   label says. No packets? Use the printed recipe below.
2. **Small sips every few minutes, for hours.** Keep going through vomiting.
3. **Keep feeding** if they can eat. Babies keep nursing or taking formula.
4. **Check for dehydration signs** (below), and write down what goes in and comes out
   (→ 25-05).
5. **Anyone with diabetes or another condition on → 12-11:** open that condition's page now.
6. **Blood in the stool, a drowsy child, nothing staying down for hours:** that is the line for
   moving toward care (→ 12-16).
7. **Keep the sick person's waste, cloths and dishes apart** and wash hands every time
   (→ 13-11).

## Do this now: preparation

- **Buy commercial oral rehydration salts or children's electrolyte solution in quantity, now,
  and rotate it.** Packets are light, cheap and keep for years, and for a household with small
  children they may be the most life-saving item in this section. Stock far more than seems
  reasonable. Write your count on → 22-04.
- **Tape one used ORS packet label to this page.** The label carries its own mixing ratio.
- **Mark a 1-litre container** and keep it with level measuring spoons in the medical kit.
- **Fix the prevention chain first:** water treatment (→ 03-04), handwashing and soap
  (→ 13-02), and human waste handled well away from water and food (→ 13-01, → 13-07). **This
  is a sanitation problem before it is a medical one.**

> **Before the collapse:** for anyone with a long-term condition, ask their prescriber now for
> written sick-day instructions (what to do with each medicine during vomiting, diarrhoea or
> fever) and put them on that person's condition page and in the "my meds" table (→ 12-07).

## Why this is the illness to plan for

Diarrhoeal illness spreads when traces of stool reach the mouth: through dirty water, dirty
food and unwashed hands. Every one of those gets worse when the grid goes down: toilets that do
not flush (→ 13-01), untreated water (→ 03-04), food kept without a fridge (→ 04-17), less
washing because water is rationed{{#if has.toddler}}, and a toddler who puts everything in their
mouth{{/if}}.

**One outbreak can put the whole household down at once.** That is dangerous when someone still
has to haul water, keep the fire going and look after the sickest person.

## Recognising dehydration

**Adults and older children:** thirst, dry mouth, dark or reduced urine, tiredness, dizziness on
standing, headache, muscle cramps.

**Babies and small children: check these, do not ask:**
- **Fewer wet diapers** than normal, or no urine for many hours
- **No tears when crying**
- Dry mouth and tongue
- **Sunken eyes**; in a baby, a sunken soft spot on the top of the head
- Unusual sleepiness, floppiness, or hard to wake
- Skin that stays "tented" when gently pinched
- **Refusing to drink**

**In a baby or toddler, sleepiness plus less urine is a serious warning sign.**

## Oral rehydration: the treatment that matters

**This is the treatment for dehydration from diarrhoea, and it works.** WHO: "Diarrhoea should
be treated with oral rehydration solution (ORS), a solution of clean water, sugar and salt"
(WHO, Diarrhoeal disease fact sheet).

**How it works:** ORS mixes sugar and salts in a set ratio that lets the gut absorb water even
while it is inflamed. **The ratio is the medicine.** That is why the strength matters and why
guessing is dangerous.

- **Small, frequent sips, continuously.** Big drinks trigger vomiting. Sips every few minutes,
  patiently, for hours, is how it is done.
- **Keep giving it through vomiting**, in small amounts, often. Some is absorbed.
- **Keep feeding** where the person can eat. Withholding food is outdated advice.
- **Sports drinks are not ORS.** CDC: for severe fluid loss, replacement is best done with ORS;
  in mild cases any preferred drink, sports drinks included, can keep someone hydrated (CDC
  Yellow Book, Travelers' Diarrhea). WHO lists drinks sweetened with sugar, such as
  commercial soft drinks and fruit juices, as unsuitable during diarrhoea (WHO, The Treatment of
  Diarrhoea).
- **Plain water alone is not enough** for big losses. You lose salts as well as water, and
  replacing only water causes its own problems.
- **The water used to mix ORS must be safe** (→ 03-04). ORS made with dirty water keeps the
  illness going.

### The printed recipe: home-made ORS when there are no packets

From the World Health Organization (WHO, Cholera outbreaks Q&A: ORS "can be bought or
obtained from health workers or even made at home by mixing 1 litre of safe water, 6 teaspoons
of sugar and half a teaspoon of salt"). WHO's manual says the salt is measured as a **level**
teaspoon (WHO, The Treatment of Diarrhoea):

```
  6  LEVEL teaspoons of sugar
+ ½  LEVEL teaspoon of salt
+ 1  litre of clean, safe water  (about 4¼ cups, treated first: 03-04)
Stir until fully dissolved. Mix only what you will use soon, and mix it fresh.
```

- **LEVEL spoons, never heaped. This is the safety-critical word.** The danger with home ORS is
  **too much salt.** A heaped half-teaspoon, or "a bit extra for strength", can push a
  dehydrated toddler into dangerous salt overload. Scrape the spoon flat with a knife. If in
  doubt, slightly weak is safer than slightly strong.
- **The taste-of-tears check:** correctly mixed ORS tastes **no saltier than tears.** If it
  tastes like sea water, it is wrong. Pour it out and mix again. Taste every batch before giving
  it to a child.
- **Measure the water, not just the spoons.** "A big jar" is not a litre. Use the marked
  container.
- **Nothing else goes in.** No extra salt "for cramps", no baking soda, no extra sugar for taste.
- **Read the recipe; do not mix from memory**, above all for a baby.

**How much to give after each loose stool** (WHO, The Treatment of Diarrhoea, as published; on
top of normal drinking and continued feeding):

| Age | After each loose stool |
|---|---|
| Children under 2 years of age | **50-100 ml** (a quarter to half a large cup) of fluid |
| Children aged 2 up to 10 years | **100-200 ml** (a half to one large cup) |
| Older children and adults | As much fluid as they want |

### For someone already dehydrated: WHO's first-4-hours amounts, as published

WHO calls this **"some dehydration"** when a person has **two or more** of these signs:
restless or irritable; sunken eyes; thirsty, drinks eagerly; a skin pinch that goes back slowly
(WHO, The Treatment of Diarrhoea, 4th rev., 2005, Table 1). For them WHO uses Treatment Plan B,
ORS solution over 4 hours. WHO's "Table 2: Guidelines for treating children and adults with
some dehydration", **"APPROXIMATE AMOUNT OF ORS SOLUTION TO GIVE IN THE FIRST 4 HOURS"**,
printed as published:

| Age (a) | Less than 4 months | 4 – 11 months | 12 – 23 months | 2 – 4 years | 5 – 14 years | 15 years or older |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weight | Less than 5 kg | 5–7.9 kg | 8-10.9 kg | 11-15.9kg | 16-29.9kg | 30 kg or more |
| In ml | 200-400 | 400-600 | 600-800 | 800-1200 | 1200-2200 | 2200-4000 |
| in local measure | ____ | ____ | ____ | ____ | ____ | ____ |

WHO's notes to the table, as published:
- (a) "Use the patient's age only when you do not know the weight. The approximate amount of ORS
  required (in ml) can also be calculated by multiplying the patient's weight in kg by 75."
- "If the patient wants more ORS than shown, give more."
- "Encourage the mother to continue breastfeeding her child."
- "For infants under 6 months who are not breastfed, if using the old WHO ORS solution containing
  90 mmol/L of sodium, also give 100-200ml clean water during this period. However, if using the
  new reduced (low) osmolarity ORS solution containing 75mmol/L of sodium, this is not
  necessary."
- "NOTE: During the initial stages of therapy, while still dehydrated, adults can consume up to
  750 ml per hour, if necessary, and children up to 20 ml per kg body weight per hour."

And from the same section of the manual: "Children under 2 years of age should be offered a
teaspoonful every 1-2 minutes; older children (and adults) may take frequent sips directly from
the cup." "If the child vomits, wait 5-10 minutes and then start giving ORS solution again, but
more slowly (e.g. a spoonful every 2-3 minutes)." "Oedematous (puffy) eyelids are a sign of
over-hydration. If this occurs, stop giving ORS solution, but give breastmilk or plain water,
and food." "After four hours, reassess
the child fully." WHO counts rehydration as complete when "the skin pinch is normal; thirst has
subsided; urine is passed; the child becomes quiet, is no longer irritable and often falls
asleep."

This binder prints WHO's table; it does not work out an amount for anyone. **Two or more of
these signs is WHO's "severe dehydration"**: lethargic or unconscious; sunken eyes; drinks poorly
or not able to drink; skin pinch goes back very slowly. WHO treats that with fluids into a vein
(Plan C), which a household cannot give: that is the line for moving toward care (→ 12-16).

**When to stop giving fluids by mouth: a rule, not a feeling.** **Nothing by mouth to anyone who
is unconscious, too drowsy to swallow safely, or cannot swallow** (it goes into the lungs). Stop
if the belly is swollen and tight, or if every sip for hours comes straight back. **A drowsy
child who cannot be woken to drink is not a "try harder" case. It is the line for moving toward
care (→ 12-16), now.** Lay them on their side (the recovery position, → 12-17) while you move.

**When ORS beats plain water:** any diarrhoea beyond a stray loose stool, any vomiting illness,
and any time dehydration signs appear, because the losses are salt and water. Plain safe water
alongside is fine; **plain water alone, in volume, to a dehydrated toddler is not.** For ordinary
thirst, sweat and heat, plain water is right. ORS is a treatment, not a drink.

## Warning signs that mean this is beyond home care

- **Blood in the stool**
- **High fever** with the illness
- **Dehydration that gets worse** despite rehydration
- **Unable to keep any fluid down** at all
- **Sleepiness, confusion, hard to wake**, above all in a child
- **Signs of sepsis** (→ 12-04)
- **Illness lasting well beyond the usual course**, or getting worse after first improving
- **Diabetes with vomiting, ketones (a warning chemical measured with a urine or blood strip),
  or fluids not staying down:** that condition's emergency (→ 02-01, → 02-05)

## Conditions that change the rules

Any vomiting or diarrhoea illness is a sick-day event for several conditions (CDC, Managing Sick
Days). For diabetes in particular:
- Illness raises blood sugar even without eating.
- **Insulin is still needed during illness**, often more. Stopping it is dangerous.
- Dehydration plus high blood sugar plus ketones leads to **diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)**, which
  is life-threatening and, without a hospital, one of the most serious situations in this
  binder.
- **Ketone test strips** belong on the condition page's stock list.

Other conditions with sick-day rules: adrenal insufficiency (→ 02-20), kidney disease (→ 02-15),

## Other common illnesses

- **Colds and flu** spread easily when everyone lives in one heated room (→ 08-01). Mostly
  viral; **antibiotics do nothing** (→ 12-04). Rest, fluids, warmth and comfort.
- **Skin infections** from minor wounds and less washing (→ 12-04).
- **Head lice, scabies and similar**: real in crowded conditions and miserable. They need
  specific treatments worth stocking.
- **Food poisoning** (→ 04-17, → 05-16). Prevention is temperature control and clean handling.
- **Waterborne illness**, especially giardia and cryptosporidium (gut parasites) from surface
  water. **Not every treatment method handles every germ** (→ 03-04, → 29-06).
- **Keeping the sick apart** helps, and is hard in one heated room. Do what you can (→ 13-11).

## Scavenge or substitute

- **ORS ingredients:** the printed recipe uses ordinary salt and sugar with safe water. Level
  spoons, measured litre, taste check. Salt and sugar are on your stock list (→ 04-13).
- **Extra fluids:** WHO lists plain water, unsalted rice water, unsalted soup, weak unsweetened
  tea and unsweetened fresh fruit juice as suitable home fluids, alongside ORS (WHO, The
  Treatment of Diarrhoea). Avoid sugary drinks.
- **Zinc** is part of WHO's standard treatment of childhood diarrhoea (WHO, The Treatment of
  Diarrhoea). Ask a clinician about it before the collapse; it is cheap.
- **Cleaning:** diluted bleach for surfaces and for cleaning up vomit and stool (→ 29-05);
  boiling for cloths and utensils.
- **What you cannot substitute:** safe water. Rehydrating with dirty water keeps the illness
  going.

## When it runs out

- **ORS and safe water are the consumables here**, both on → 23-02. **An illness multiplies
  water use at exactly the moment someone is too sick to haul it.** Keep more water stored than
  the daily figure suggests (→ 03-02). Your household's planning figure is
  {{ derived.water.plan_gal_day }} gallons a day before anyone is sick.
- **Count packets in litres, then in days of a sick child.** One packet usually makes one litre
  (check your label). A dehydrated toddler can need a lot over a day or two.
- **Ration:** give ORS first to **the smallest and the sickest**: babies and toddlers, then
  older children, then anyone whose condition makes dehydration dangerous (→ 12-11), then
  healthy adults, who can often manage on salted fluids and broths.
- **Resupply:** salt and sugar (→ 04-13), safe water and the printed recipe. As long as you have
  those three, ORS is renewable. That is why the recipe is printed here.
- **Substitute:** rice water, soup and the other home fluids above help, but **correct-ratio
  ORS is better.**
- **The permanent answer is prevention**: water treatment, soap, latrine placement and food
  handling. Every hour spent there prevents an illness that would cost far more.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** the whole prevention chain can be built here: treated water, working
  sanitation, soap, food safety. **Get it right in the first week.**
- **B (moving):** the highest-risk phase. Unfamiliar water, worse hygiene, exhaustion and fluid
  you cannot easily replace. **Treat all water** (→ 03-04), carry ORS packets (they weigh almost
  nothing) and hand sanitiser for when washing is not possible. **A vomiting person with
  diabetes or another listed condition on the move means stop or turn around** (→ 01-02).
- **C (resettled):** latrine placement is the whole game (→ 13-07): waste, water and animals
  separated on day one. Surface water needs real treatment (→ 03-04), and animals add new routes
  for illness (→ 05-04).
