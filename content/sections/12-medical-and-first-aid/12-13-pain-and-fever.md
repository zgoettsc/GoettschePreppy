# Pain, fever and over-the-counter medicine reference

**What this page is for.** Using over-the-counter (OTC) pain and fever medicines safely, the
American Academy of Pediatrics' published children's dosing charts, the fever warning signs in
children, and pain relief that needs no medicine.

> **SAFETY**
> - **Read the package. Every time.** For adults, the label's directions govern. For children,
>   the only doses in this binder are the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) charts below,
>   printed as published. **This binder never calculates, adjusts or suggests a dose.**
> - **The three ways households hurt themselves with OTC pain relievers:**
>   1. **Overdose.** Above the daily maximum, acetaminophen can cause **liver failure**;
>      ibuprofen and similar drugs cause stomach bleeding and kidney damage. **The daily
>      maximum is a ceiling, not a suggestion.**
>   2. **Doubling up without knowing.** Cold, flu and sinus products **often contain pain
>      relievers.** Taking one with a separate pain reliever is a very common accidental
>      overdose. **Read every ingredient list.**
>   3. **Children.** Children's doses go **by weight**, measured with a **dosing syringe**, never
>      a kitchen spoon. Some medicines are **not for children at all.**
> - **Check every liquid, chewable and suspension against everyone's allergies** (→ 25-09):
>   flavourings and suspension bases can contain food allergens (FDA, Food Allergies).
> - **Never aspirin or other salicylates for children or teenagers** (Reye's syndrome, → 12-07).

## Do this now: a child has a fever

1. **Check the red flags below first.** A baby under 3 months with a fever of 100.4 °F (38 °C)
   or higher is an emergency, full stop.
2. **Weigh the child** (or use the weight on → 25-09 if it is recent).
3. **Read the bottle's strength** (mg per mL). It must match the chart column exactly.
4. **Find the weight row**, measure with a dosing syringe, and **write the time down** (→ 25-05)
   so two tired adults never double-dose.
5. **Keep them drinking** (→ 12-05). Comfort is the goal, not the thermometer number.

## Do this now: preparation

- **Buy accurate dosing syringes and cups** and keep them with the medicines.
- **Read the label of every OTC product you own** and mark the ones with a hidden pain reliever.
- **Write everyone's weight down** and update children's weights every few months (→ 25-09).
- **Check every product against the household allergy list.**

> **Before the collapse:** ask your pharmacist which OTC products suit each person in your
> household, and how to combine or alternate them for significant pain. Ask your child's doctor
> to confirm the chart row for each child's weight and the bottle you own, and write the answer
> on → 25-09 with the date. For anyone nursing, ask which products are fine (→ 12-07). These are
> free, five-minute conversations that make this page safe to act on.

## Children's dosing charts (AAP, as published)

Below are the American Academy of Pediatrics' two printable charts, transcribed in full: every
weight band, every product column, the dose line and the note printed on each chart (AAP,
HealthyChildren: Acetaminophen pictographic dosing chart, 2026 edition; Ibuprofen dosing chart,

column that matches the bottle in your hand, then the weight row. Use weight; use age only if

  than 6 years of age."

### Rules that go with both charts

- **The label on the bottle you own governs if it differs.** Check the strength (mg per mL) every
  time; it must match the column you read.
- **Never aspirin for children** (Reye's syndrome, → 12-07).
- **Do not alternate the two drugs on an improvised schedule.** If a clinician gave you a written
  alternating plan, follow it exactly.
- **Write every dose down with the time** (→ 25-05).

## Fever in children: the red flags and two scary nights

From the AAP (When to Call the Pediatrician: Fever). **The AAP says to call the doctor right away
if a child has a fever and any of these.** With no doctor to call, treat each one as the line
for moving toward care:
- **Younger than 3 months (12 weeks) with a temperature of 100.4 °F (38.0 °C) or higher.**
- Looks very ill, is unusually drowsy, or is very fussy
- Has been somewhere very hot, such as an overheated car
- Other symptoms such as a stiff neck, severe headache, severe sore throat or severe ear pain
- An immune system problem (such as sickle cell disease or cancer, or medicines such as steroids
  that weaken the immune system), or a heart problem
- Has had a seizure
- Temperature rises above 104 °F (40 °C) repeatedly, at any age

**Also call** (AAP): still "acts sick" once the fever is brought down; seems to be getting worse;
fever lasting more than 24 hours under 2 years, or more than 3 days (72 hours) at 2 years and
older. **Not drinking, or few wet diapers, is dehydration** (→ 12-05).

If care exists, go. If not, → 12-16. **Medicine treats comfort; it never replaces this check.**

**Febrile seizure** (a seizure brought on by fever; most often in healthy children between 6
months and 5 years; MedlinePlus, Febrile seizures): terrifying, and a simple one stops by itself
within a few seconds to 10 minutes. **Turn the child on the side, protect from injury, nothing
forced into the mouth, time it.** MedlinePlus: a seizure that lasts several minutes needs an
ambulance; one lasting longer than 15 minutes, in just one part of the body, or happening again
in the same illness is not a normal febrile seizure. With no ambulance, those are the line for
moving toward whatever care exists (→ 01-02).

**Croup** (children from about 3 months to 5 years; MedlinePlus, Croup): a barking, seal-like
cough with a noise on breathing in, often worse at night. Home comfort is **calm plus cool or
warm moist air** (a steamy bathroom or cool night air); MedlinePlus says this may help the child
feel better, though no studies show it reduces symptoms. **MedlinePlus says call 911 for bluish
lips or skin, drooling, trouble swallowing, a breathing-in noise at rest or getting worse, the
muscles between the ribs tugging in, or struggling to breathe.** With no 911, that is the line
for moving toward care now.

## What pain and fever medicine is actually for

With no clinic, these medicines do three valuable things:
1. **Let an injured or sick person sleep**, which is part of recovery.
2. **Let a feverish person drink and eat**, which is what actually prevents dehydration (→ 12-05).
3. **Keep people functional.** Someone in severe pain makes bad decisions.

**They do not treat the cause.** Masking pain can hide a worsening infection or injury, **so keep
watching for the warning signs** (→ 12-04) even when someone feels better. **Feeling better on
medicine is not the same as getting better.**

## The categories (no doses, no brands)

| Category | What it does | Key cautions: **read the label** |
|---|---|---|
| **Acetaminophen** | Pain, fever | **Liver damage above the daily maximum.** Hidden in many combination products. Alcohol raises the risk |
| **NSAIDs** (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs: ibuprofen, naproxen) | Pain, fever, **swelling** | Stomach bleeding, kidney effects; take with food; caution with dehydration, kidney disease and some conditions (→ 12-11) |
| **Aspirin** | Pain, fever, specific uses | **Not for children or teenagers** (Reye's syndrome). Bleeding risk |
| **Rub-on pain relievers** | Local muscle and joint pain | Not with heat; away from eyes and broken skin |
| **Antihistamines** | Allergy symptoms, itching; some cause drowsiness | **Not a substitute for epinephrine in anaphylaxis** (→ 02-03). Children's diphenhydramine Drug Facts label: **"Do not use to make a child sleepy"**; **under 2 years, do not use; 2 to 5 years, do not use unless directed by a doctor** (Children's Benadryl Allergy, Drug Facts label). FDA warns that more than the label dose can cause serious heart problems, seizures, coma or death (FDA, Drug Safety Communication on diphenhydramine). Non-drowsy antihistamines made for children exist; the label gives the age and dose. Drowsy types dull adult judgement too |
| **Combination cold and flu products** | Several symptoms | **Contain pain relievers: the doubling-up trap** |

**Practical points:**
- **The two main pain relievers work differently**, which is why a clinician may advise using
  them in a pattern for significant pain. Ask your pharmacist before the collapse and write it
  down.
- **NSAIDs reduce swelling and acetaminophen does not**, which matters for sprains and tooth
  pain.
- **After a head injury (→ 12-19), use acetaminophen, not ibuprofen or another NSAID, during the
  watch.** The AAP's head-injury advice is no pain medicine except acetaminophen unless a doctor
  says otherwise (AAP, Head Injury).

## Fever: the judgement, not the number

Fever is the body fighting infection. **You are treating the person's comfort and ability to
function, not the thermometer.**
- **Write temperatures down with the time** (→ 25-05). The trend matters more than any reading.
- **A comfortable, drinking, resting person with a fever is usually fine.** One who cannot
  drink, cannot rest, or is getting worse is not.
- **Fever plus confusion, fast breathing or a fast heartbeat may be sepsis** (→ 12-04; CDC,
  About Sepsis): an emergency whatever the number.
- **Fever in someone with a long-term condition can be a sick-day event** (CDC, Managing Sick
  Days). Diabetes, adrenal insufficiency and a weakened immune system each have their own rules
  (→ 12-11).
- **Most fevers come from viruses, where antibiotics do nothing** (CDC, Antibiotic Use; → 12-04).

## Pain relief without medicine: more important as supplies fall

Free, unlimited and effective:
- **Rest and keeping the injured part still**: for injuries, most of the treatment (→ 12-09).
- **Cold** early (swelling, fresh injury); **heat** later (muscle pain, stiffness). **No heat on
  a spreading infection.**
- **Raising** a swollen limb.
- **Positioning**: find the position that hurts least and support it with pillows and rolled
  blankets.
- **Distraction, slow breathing and calm.** Effective, especially for children (→ 20-08). Fear
  makes pain worse (→ 21-01).
- **Warmth and comfort.** A cold, frightened person in pain is worse off in every way.
- **Salt-water rinses** for tooth and throat pain (→ 12-06).
- **Sleep.** Protecting an injured person's sleep is real treatment (→ 21-07).

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Doubling up** through combination products: the most common real-world overdose.
- **Going over the daily maximum** because the pain is not controlled. **If the maximum is not
  working, the answer is not more; it is reassessment** (→ 12-16).
- **Dosing a child by guessing.** By weight, with a syringe, from the label or the AAP chart.
- **Aspirin for a child.**
- **Masking a worsening problem.** Keep checking the wound, the temperature and the trend.
- **Ignoring interactions** with prescriptions. Ask the pharmacist while you can.
- **An allergen hidden in a medicine.** Check every label.

## Scavenge or substitute

- **Medicine cabinets (→ 22-02):** OTC pain relievers are among the most common and useful finds.
  **Original labelled containers only**; never unidentified pills.
- **Pain relief without medicine** (above) is the real substitute, and it does not run out.
- **Willow bark** contains a salicylate related to aspirin and has a long history. **It carries
  the same risks as aspirin, including not for children, and its strength is unreliable.** If
  you use it, learn it properly in advance.
- **Honey** for cough and sore throat has real support. **Never under 12 months** (infant
  botulism).
- **What you cannot improvise:** a reliable dose. That is why the no-medicine methods deserve
  more attention than they get.

## When it runs out

- **Pain relievers are among the fastest-used medicines**: headaches, sore backs, injuries,
  fevers, tooth pain, {{ household.size }} people, months. **Stock far more than a normal
  household** and list it on → 23-02.
- **Use them for function, not comfort alone, as supply falls**: letting a feverish child drink,
  letting an injured adult sleep, controlling tooth pain enough to eat. Use the no-medicine
  methods for ordinary aches.
- **Put the smallest, the sickest and the people the household depends on first.**
- **Expiry:** liquids and children's suspensions expire faster than tablets (→ 12-07). **Rotate
  the children's products most actively.** Splitting an adult tablet for a child is never done
  without a clinician's written instruction, and for some medicines not at all. Ask your
  pharmacist before the collapse.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** stock deep while pharmacies work, have the pharmacist and doctor
  conversations, start the logging habit. Nobody gives a child medicine without the label, the
  chart and the syringe.
- **B (moving):** a compact supply of both medicines, adult and children's, plus the dosing
  syringe, in the medical module (→ 22-03). Pain control on the move is about keeping people
  able to keep going.
- **C (resettled):** no resupply and more injuries. **Pain relief without medicine becomes the
  main tool**, and the stocked medicine a reserve for real need. Build the habit early.
