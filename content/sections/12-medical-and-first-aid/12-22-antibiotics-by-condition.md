# Antibiotics by condition: published adult and child doses

**What this page is for.** When there is no doctor and no pharmacy, this page shows, for the
infections a household is most likely to meet, whether antibiotics help at all, which antibiotic
the published guidance names first, what to use with a penicillin allergy, and the adult and
child doses **exactly as each publisher printed them**, with the publisher named on every row.
It also lists the signs that mean the infection is beyond home treatment. The drugs themselves
(dangers, allergies, pregnancy, liquids, storage, animal products) are on → 12-23. The military
antibiotic section is on → 12-24.

> **SAFETY**
> - **This binder never works out a dose for a person.** Every dose below is printed as its
>   publisher printed it. Where publishers differ, both are printed. Nothing has been adjusted,
>   averaged or rounded.
> - **Child doses in mg/kg must be checked against the published maximum.** "mg/kg" means
>   milligrams of medicine for each kilogram the child weighs. A heavy child can reach a dose
>   above the adult dose; the published maximum, or the weight band that says "use adult dose",
>   is the ceiling. Weigh the child; do not guess.
> - **Liquids come in different strengths.** The same medicine is sold as several liquids with
>   a different number of milligrams in each 5 mL. The FDA label for amoxicillin lists oral
>   suspensions of **200 mg/5 mL, 250 mg/5 mL and 400 mg/5 mL**, and tells you to measure a
>   child's dose with a calibrated oral syringe (Teva amoxicillin label, DailyMed). The same
>   number of millilitres gives twice the medicine in the 400 mg/5 mL bottle as in the
>   200 mg/5 mL bottle. Read the strength on every bottle (→ 29-14).
> - **Doses are for normal kidney and liver function.** WHO states this under every table. This
>   binder prints no kidney- or liver-adjusted dose. A person with kidney or liver disease
>   (→ 02-15) needs a prescriber's dose.
> - **Check allergies first** (→ 25-09). A severe reaction to a penicillin (collapse, breathing
>   trouble or facial swelling within minutes to 1 to 2 hours) is a reason to avoid the whole
>   penicillin group (WHO). → 12-23 has the allergy rules.
> - **Sepsis is an emergency** (the body's runaway reaction to infection): confusion, fast
>   breathing, fast heartbeat, clammy skin or extreme pain with an infection (CDC, About Sepsis).
>   If any care exists, go now. If not, → 12-16.
> - **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products** (floor,
>   → 30-01). Antibiotic liquids are flavoured to taste good.

Whether your household uses antibiotics without a prescriber at all, and whether that includes
children, is a decision card: it weighs a possible cure against allergy, the wrong drug and
resistance. Your household's choice: {{choice DC-16}} (→ 30-17)

## How to read the rows

- **Who wrote each row.** **WHO** is the World Health Organization's *AWaRe Antibiotic Book*
  (2022), written for clinics with few resources; it gives adult and child doses for each
  infection. **IDSA** is the Infectious Diseases Society of America; **ATS** the American
  Thoracic Society; **PIDS** the Pediatric Infectious Diseases Society; **CDC** the US Centers
  for Disease Control and Prevention. **FDA label** means the prescribing information the US
  Food and Drug Administration approved for that product, as posted on DailyMed.
- **WHO does not print penicillin-allergy alternatives.** It says true severe allergy is rare
  and over-reported, and that avoiding the best drug exposes the patient to weaker treatment
  (WHO, chapter 3). Allergy rows below come from US guidelines and FDA labels.
- **Words used in the rows.** *Oral* or *po*: by mouth. *IV*: into a vein. *IM*: into a muscle.
  *bid*: twice a day. *tid*: three times a day. *qid*: four times a day. *qd*: once a day.
  *"Given every 8 hours"*: three times in 24 hours. *"In 2 divided doses"*: the day's total
  split into two equal doses. *"Weight band"*: a fixed dose for a range of body weights.
  *"Max"*: the most that may be given per dose or per day, as stated.
- **WHO lists choices in alphabetical order** and calls them "equal treatment options, unless
  otherwise indicated". The order here is WHO's, not a ranking.
- **Duration** means the total number of days, as published.

## When antibiotics do not help

Antibiotics kill bacteria. They do nothing for viruses. The published guidance is plain:

- **Colds and flu:** "Antibiotics DO NOT work on viruses, such as those that cause colds and
  flu" (CDC, Antibiotic Do's and Don'ts). Rest, fluids and fever care: → 12-05, → 12-13.
- **Bronchitis (chest cold, a cough that lingers):** "Antibiotics are not needed for most
  cases." Almost all cases are viral; the cough can last several weeks; yellow or green phlegm
  "does not indicate bacterial infection and the need for antibiotics" (WHO, chapter 4). CDC:
  routine antibiotic treatment of uncomplicated acute bronchitis "is not recommended,
  regardless of cough duration" (CDC, Outpatient Clinical Care for Adults).
- **Most sore throats:** "Antibiotics are not needed for most cases since most cases are
  self-limiting and of viral origin" (WHO, chapter 6).
- **Most ear infections:** "Antibiotics are not needed for most cases" (WHO, chapter 5).
- **Most sinus infections:** "Antibiotics are not needed in the great majority of cases."
  Coloured nasal discharge alone is not a sign of bacterial infection (WHO, chapter 7).
- **Most diarrhoea:** "Antibiotics are not needed in the great majority of cases of watery
  diarrhoea with or without a fever" (WHO, chapter 14). Fluids are the treatment (→ 12-05).
- **Uninfected wounds:** "In general, uninfected wounds do not require antibiotic treatment
  except in very select cases" (WHO, chapter 18). Cleaning is the treatment (→ 12-02).

**Where WHO and the FDA label differ for small babies.** The FDA amoxicillin label says that in
babies under 12 weeks (3 months) "the recommended upper dose of amoxicillin is 30 mg/kg/day
divided every 12 hours", because their kidneys are not fully developed (Teva amoxicillin label,
DailyMed). WHO's bands above start at 3 kg and do not separate babies under 3 months. Both are
printed; a baby under 3 months with a fever is an emergency in its own right (→ 12-13).

## 1. Strep throat (sore throat likely caused by streptococcus)

**Does it need antibiotics?** Usually not. WHO: "The only clear indication for antibiotic
treatment of pharyngitis is to reduce the probability of developing rheumatic fever in endemic
settings"; antibiotics shorten sore-throat pain by about one day. WHO uses the Centor score,
one point each for fever above 38.0 °C, no cough, tender swollen glands at the front of the neck,
and white patches (pus) on the tonsils: with 0 to 2 points strep is unlikely and treatment is for
symptoms only; with 3 to 4 points antibiotics are recommended where rheumatic fever is common
and "can be withheld" where it is rare (WHO, chapter 6).

**WHO AWaRe, Table 6.5**
- **Adults, first choice:** Amoxicillin (oral): 500 mg given every 8 hours, OR
  Phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin V) (oral): 500 mg (800 000 IU) given every 6 hours.
- **Children, first choice:** Amoxicillin (oral): 80–90 mg/kg/day, weight bands in Table W1, OR
  Phenoxymethylpenicillin (oral): 10–15 mg/kg/dose (16 000–24 000 IU/kg/dose) given every 6 to
  8 hours.
- **Adults, second choice:** Cefalexin (oral): 500 mg given every 8 hours, OR Clarithromycin
  (oral): 500 mg given every 12 hours.
- **Children, second choice:** Cefalexin (oral): 25 mg/kg/dose given every 12 hours (Table W3),
  OR Clarithromycin (oral): 7.5 mg/kg/dose given every 12 hours.
- **Duration:** 5 days where rheumatic fever is rare, 10 days where it is common or the person
  has had rheumatic fever; always 5 days for cefalexin or clarithromycin.

**IDSA 2012 group A strep guideline, Table 2** (doses with maximums; adults and children)

| Drug, route | Dose as published | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Penicillin V, oral | Children: 250 mg twice daily or 3 times daily; adolescents and adults: 250 mg 4 times daily or 500 mg twice daily | 10 d |
| Amoxicillin, oral | 50 mg/kg once daily (max = 1000 mg); alternate: 25 mg/kg (max = 500 mg) twice daily | 10 d |
| Benzathine penicillin G, intramuscular injection | < 27 kg: 600 000 U; ≥ 27 kg: 1 200 000 U | 1 dose |
| **Penicillin allergy:** Cephalexin, oral | 20 mg/kg/dose twice daily (max = 500 mg/dose) | 10 d |
| **Penicillin allergy:** Cefadroxil, oral | 30 mg/kg once daily (max = 1 g) | 10 d |
| **Penicillin allergy:** Clindamycin, oral | 7 mg/kg/dose 3 times daily (max = 300 mg/dose) | 10 d |
| **Penicillin allergy:** Azithromycin, oral | 12 mg/kg once daily (max = 500 mg) | 5 d |
| **Penicillin allergy:** Clarithromycin, oral | 7.5 mg/kg/dose twice daily (max = 250 mg/dose) | 10 d |

IDSA notes: avoid cephalosporins (cephalexin, cefadroxil) in people with an immediate,
anaphylactic-type penicillin allergy; strep resistance to azithromycin and clarithromycin is
well known and varies by region. CDC: "Recommended treatment course for all oral beta-lactams
[penicillins and cephalosporins] is 10 days" (CDC, Outpatient Clinical Care for Adults).

**FDA label, penicillin V potassium** (adults and children 12 years and over): streptococcal
infections, mild to moderately severe, of the upper respiratory tract: "125 to 250 mg (200,000
to 400,000 units) every 6 to 8 hours for 10 days" (Teva penicillin V potassium label, DailyMed).

**Beyond home treatment.** WHO names the rare complications: a collection of pus beside the
tonsil (quinsy) and rheumatic fever, which can damage the heart. Any sore throat with the sepsis
signs in the safety box is an emergency.

## 2. Ear infection (acute otitis media)

**Does it need antibiotics?** Usually not. WHO: most non-severe cases can be managed with pain
and fever relief and close watching, "especially in children > 2 years of age". Antibiotics
should be considered if symptoms are severe ("systemically very unwell, ear pain despite
analgesics, fever ≥39.0 °C"), in children with a weak immune system, and for infection in both
ears in children under 2 years. Reassess if not improving over 3 days (WHO, chapter 5). Pain and
fever relief for children: → 12-13.

**WHO AWaRe, Table 5.3** (duration 5 days)
- **Adults, first choice:** Amoxicillin (oral): 500 mg given every 8 hours.
- **Adults, second choice:** Amoxicillin+clavulanic acid (oral): 500 mg + 125 mg given every
  8 hours.
- **Children, first choice:** Amoxicillin (oral): 80–90 mg/kg/day (Table W1).
- **Children, second choice:** Amoxicillin+clavulanic acid (oral): 80–90 mg/kg/day of
  amoxicillin component (Table W2).

**FDA labels (United States).** The American Academy of Pediatrics otitis guideline could not be
read for this binder, so the FDA-approved label doses are printed instead. They differ from WHO.
- **Amoxicillin label**, children 3 months and older under 40 kg, ear/nose/throat infections:
  mild/moderate "25 mg/kg/day in divided doses every 12 hours or 20 mg/kg/day in divided doses
  every 8 hours"; severe "45 mg/kg/day in divided doses every 12 hours or 40 mg/kg/day in divided
  doses every 8 hours". Adults and children over 40 kg: mild/moderate "500 mg every 12 hours or
  250 mg every 8 hours"; severe "875 mg every 12 hours or 500 mg every 8 hours" (Teva
  amoxicillin label).
- **Amoxicillin-clavulanate label**, children 3 months and older under 40 kg, otitis media:
  "45 mg/kg/day every 12 hours" using the 200 mg/28.5 mg per 5 mL or 400 mg/57 mg per 5 mL
  suspension; "Duration of therapy studied and recommended for acute otitis media is 10 days"
  (Teva amoxicillin-clavulanate label).
- **Augmentin ES-600 label** (a different, stronger liquid: 600 mg amoxicillin per 5 mL), for
  recurrent or persistent ear infection in children 3 months to 12 years weighing 40 kg or less:
  "90 mg/kg/day divided every 12 hours, administered for 10 days". The label warns that ES-600
  "does not contain the same amount of clavulanic acid" as the other suspensions, so they are
  not swapped for each other.
- **Penicillin allergy, cefdinir label** (a cephalosporin), children 6 months through 12 years,
  acute bacterial otitis media: "7 mg/kg q12h" for 5 to 10 days, or "14 mg/kg q24h" for 10 days;
  "the total daily dose for all infections is 14 mg/kg, up to a maximum dose of 600 mg per day"
  (cefdinir for oral suspension label). Not for anyone allergic to cephalosporins.
- **Penicillin allergy, azithromycin label** (Zithromax), children 6 months and older, acute
  otitis media: "30 mg/kg as a single dose or 10 mg/kg once daily for 3 days or 10 mg/kg as a
  single dose on Day 1 followed by 5 mg/kg/day on Days 2 through 5" (Pfizer Zithromax label).

**Beyond home treatment.** WHO names the complications that need imaging and hospital care:
"mastoiditis" (infection of the bone behind the ear) and brain abscess. An ear infection that
gets worse instead of better after 3 days, or comes with the sepsis signs, is beyond home care.

## 3. Sinus infection (acute sinusitis)

**Does it need antibiotics?** Usually not. Symptoms can last up to 4 weeks. WHO: watchful
waiting without antibiotics when symptoms have lasted under 10 days and are improving.
Antibiotics should be considered for a severe start (fever ≥ 39.0 °C with pus-like nasal
discharge or facial pain for at least 3 to 4 days in a row), symptoms lasting 10 days or more
without improvement, or clear worsening after an early mild phase (WHO, chapter 7).

**WHO AWaRe, Table 7.3** (duration 5 days)
- **Adults:** Amoxicillin (oral): 1 g given every 8 hours, OR Amoxicillin+clavulanic acid (oral):
  500 mg + 125 mg given every 8 hours.
- **Children:** Amoxicillin (oral): 80–90 mg/kg/day (Table W1), OR Amoxicillin+clavulanic acid
  (oral): 80–90 mg/kg/day of amoxicillin component (Table W2).

**IDSA 2012 acute bacterial rhinosinusitis guideline, Tables 9 and 10**

| Who | Choice | As published |
|---|---|---|
| Adults | First line | Amoxicillin-clavulanate (500 mg/125 mg PO tid, or 875 mg/125 mg PO bid) |
| Adults | Second line | Amoxicillin-clavulanate (2000 mg/125 mg PO bid) |
| Adults | β-lactam (penicillin) allergy | Doxycycline (100 mg PO bid or 200 mg PO qd) |
| Adults | β-lactam allergy | Levofloxacin (500 mg PO qd) |
| Adults | β-lactam allergy | Moxifloxacin (400 mg PO qd) |
| Children | First line | Amoxicillin-clavulanate (45 mg/kg/day PO bid) |
| Children | Second line | Amoxicillin-clavulanate (90 mg/kg/day PO bid) |
| Children | β-lactam allergy, type I (immediate, severe) | Levofloxacin (10–20 mg/kg/day PO every 12–24 h) |
| Children | β-lactam allergy, not type I | Clindamycin (30–40 mg/kg/day PO tid) plus cefixime (8 mg/kg/day PO bid) or cefpodoxime (10 mg/kg/day PO bid) |

IDSA duration: "The recommended duration of therapy for uncomplicated ABRS in adults is 5–7
days"; "In children with ABRS, the longer treatment duration of 10–14 days is still
recommended". IDSA states doxycycline is "not suitable for children". CDC adds that
azithromycin and other macrolides are not recommended for sinusitis because of resistance (CDC,
Outpatient Clinical Care for Adults). These child rows print no maximum; the adult dose is the
ceiling for a heavy child.

**Beyond home treatment.** WHO lists the signs of a complicated infection: "systemic toxicity,
persistent fever ≥ 39.0 °C, periorbital redness and swelling [red, swollen skin around the eye],
severe headache and altered mental status".

## 4. Pneumonia caught in the community (not in hospital)

**Does it need antibiotics?** Yes when it is pneumonia. WHO describes it as a new (under 2
weeks) or worsening cough with fever (≥ 38.0 °C), phlegm, shortness of breath and fast breathing;
in children also grunting, flaring nostrils, blue lips or pallor. Many viral chest infections look
the same. WHO: "Clinical improvement should be evident within 48–72 hours of starting
antibiotic therapy"; if not, think of a complication such as pus around the lung. Cough and
tiredness often take 2 to 3 weeks to clear (WHO, chapter 12).

**WHO AWaRe, Table 12.4, adults, mild pneumonia** (duration 5 days)
- **First choice:** Amoxicillin (oral): 1 g given every 8 hours, OR Phenoxymethylpenicillin
  (oral): 500 mg (800 000 IU) given every 6 hours.
- **Second choice:** Amoxicillin+clavulanic acid (oral): 875 mg + 125 mg given every 8 hours, OR
  Doxycycline (oral): 100 mg given every 12 hours.

**WHO AWaRe, Table 12.5, children, pneumonia (fast breathing and/or chest indrawing) treated at
home:** Amoxicillin (oral): 80–90 mg/kg/day (Table W1). Duration: "3 days for children in areas
of low HIV prevalence and no chest indrawing"; "5 days if the child has chest indrawing or lives
in region of higher HIV prevalence". *Chest indrawing* means the lower chest sucks in when the
child breathes in.

**ATS/IDSA 2019 adult pneumonia guideline (United States), outpatients**
- **Healthy adults without the conditions below:** "amoxicillin 1 g three times daily", or
  "doxycycline 100 mg twice daily", or "a macrolide (azithromycin 500 mg on first day then 250 mg
  daily or clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily or clarithromycin extended release 1,000 mg daily)
  only in areas with pneumococcal resistance to macrolides <25%".
- **Adults with chronic heart, lung, liver or kidney disease, diabetes, alcoholism, cancer or no
  spleen:** combination therapy, "amoxicillin/clavulanate 500 mg/125 mg three times daily, or
  amoxicillin/clavulanate 875 mg/125 mg twice daily, or 2,000 mg/125 mg twice daily, or a
  cephalosporin (cefpodoxime 200 mg twice daily or cefuroxime 500 mg twice daily); AND"
  a macrolide (azithromycin 500 mg on first day then 250 mg daily, clarithromycin 500 mg twice
  daily or extended release 1,000 mg once daily) or doxycycline 100 mg twice daily; or a
  respiratory fluoroquinolone alone: "Levofloxacin 750 mg daily, moxifloxacin 400 mg daily, or
  gemifloxacin 320 mg daily".
- **Duration:** "we recommend treating for a minimum of 5 days".
- **Penicillin allergy:** doxycycline and the macrolides above are not penicillins; the
  fluoroquinolones carry serious FDA warnings (→ 12-23).

**PIDS/IDSA 2011 children's pneumonia guideline, Table 7, outpatients**
- **Presumed bacterial pneumonia, under 5 years:** "Amoxicillin, oral (90 mg/kg/day in 2
  doses)"; alternative "oral amoxicillin clavulanate (amoxicillin component, 90 mg/kg/day in 2
  doses)".
- **Presumed bacterial pneumonia, 5 years and over:** "Oral amoxicillin (90 mg/kg/day in 2 doses
  to a maximum of 4 g/day)"; alternative "oral amoxicillin clavulanate (amoxicillin component,
  90 mg/kg/day in 2 doses to a maximum dose of 4000 mg/day, eg, one 2000-mg tablet twice daily)".
  The guideline's footnote says the 4 g/day maximum was "Not evaluated prospectively for
  safety."
- **Presumed "atypical" pneumonia (walking pneumonia), 5 years and over:** "Oral azithromycin
  (10 mg/kg on day 1, followed by 5 mg/kg/day once daily on days 2–5 to a maximum of 500 mg on
  day 1, followed by 250 mg on days 2–5)"; alternatives "oral clarithromycin (15 mg/kg/day in 2
  doses to a maximum of 1 g/day)", erythromycin, "doxycycline for children >7 years old".
- **Under 5 years, atypical:** "Azithromycin oral (10 mg/kg on day 1, followed by 5 mg/kg/day
  once daily on days 2–5)". The guideline also says antibiotics are "not routinely required for
  preschool-aged children with CAP", because most is viral.

**Beyond home treatment.** WHO's adult score (CRB-65), one point each: new confusion; breathing
faster than 30 breaths a minute; blood pressure below 90 systolic or 60 or less diastolic; age
65 or over. Score 0 to 1: candidate for home treatment. Score 2: "Consider inpatient treatment".
3 or more: hospital. In children, WHO calls pneumonia severe when fast breathing comes with "a
general danger sign, such as inability to breastfeed or drink, convulsions, lethargy or a reduced
level of consciousness"; a child who does not respond to amoxicillin needs second-line care.

## 5. Bladder infection (uncomplicated cystitis)

**What it is:** burning when passing urine, needing to go often and urgently, low belly pain, no
fever. WHO: "Oral nitrofurantoin for 5 days is the main recommended treatment for lower UTIs";
cloudy or smelly urine alone is not a reliable sign (WHO, chapter 23).

**WHO AWaRe, Table 23.5**
- **Amoxicillin+clavulanic acid (oral):** adults 500 mg + 125 mg given every 8 hours; children
  80–90 mg/kg/day of amoxicillin component (Table W2). 3–5 days.
- **Nitrofurantoin (oral):** adults "100 mg given every 12 hours (modified-release formulation)"
  or "50 mg given every 6 hours (immediate-release formulation)"; children "2 mg/kg/dose given
  every 12 hours" or "1 mg/kg/dose given every 6 hours (immediate release formulation)". 5 days.
- **Sulfamethoxazole+trimethoprim (oral):** adults 800 mg + 160 mg given every 12 hours;
  children 4 mg/kg (of trimethoprim component), every 12 hours. 3 days. Child weight bands (mg of
  sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim): 3–< 6 kg: 100 mg/20 mg; 6–< 10 kg: 200 mg/40 mg; 10–< 30 kg:
  400 mg/80 mg, each given every 12 hours; ≥ 30 kg: use adult dose.
- **Trimethoprim (oral):** adults 200 mg given every 12 hours; children 4 mg/kg, every 12 hours
  (3–< 6 kg: 20 mg; 6–< 10 kg: 40 mg; 10–< 30 kg: 80 mg, each every 12 hours; ≥ 30 kg: use adult
  dose). 3 days.
- WHO notes: "shorter treatments are indicated for children or non-pregnant women (3–5 days
  depending on the antibiotic), while longer treatments are indicated for pregnant women
  (usually 5 days) or men (usually 7 days)". Sulfamethoxazole+trimethoprim "is not recommended in
  the first trimester of pregnancy".

**IDSA/ESCMID 2011 guideline (women, uncomplicated cystitis)**
- "Nitrofurantoin monohydrate/macrocrystals (100 mg twice daily for 5 days)".
- "Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (160/800 mg [1 double-strength tablet] twice-daily for 3
  days)", where local resistance does not exceed 20%.
- "Fosfomycin trometamol (3 g in a single dose)" where available.
- Fluoroquinolones (such as ciprofloxacin) "should be reserved for important uses other than
  acute cystitis". Amoxicillin alone is not recommended for this.

**FDA label, Macrobid (nitrofurantoin):** "Adults and Pediatric Patients Over 12 Years: One 100 mg
capsule every 12 hours for seven days." Macrobid "is indicated only for the treatment of acute
uncomplicated urinary tract infections (acute cystitis)" and "is not indicated for the treatment
of pyelonephritis" (Macrobid label). This 7-day label course differs from WHO's and IDSA's 5 days;
both are printed.

**Children.** CDC: for urinary tract infection in children 2 to 24 months, "Duration of therapy
should be 7-14 days" (CDC, Outpatient Clinical Care for Pediatric Populations). A urinary
infection in a baby or young child often shows only as fever, vomiting or irritability (WHO).

**Beyond home treatment.** Fever, pain in the side or back (flank), nausea and vomiting, or
feeling generally ill mean the infection may have reached the kidney (section 6). Pregnancy,
men, kidney stones or other urinary-tract problems make it a "complicated" infection (WHO).

## 6. Kidney infection (pyelonephritis)

**What it is:** fever (≥ 38.0 °C), flank pain, tenderness over the back of the lower ribs,
nausea and vomiting, with or without bladder symptoms (WHO, chapter 34).

**WHO AWaRe, Table 34.5, mild to moderate cases** (duration 7 days)
- **Adults:** Ciprofloxacin (oral): 500 mg given every 12 hours.
- **Children:** Ciprofloxacin (oral): 15 mg/kg/dose given every 12 hours (Table W5).
- WHO notes that in men "prostatitis can also be present and longer treatment may therefore be
  warranted". Severe cases need injected antibiotics (ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, gentamicin or
  amikacin), which is hospital care.

**IDSA/ESCMID 2011 guideline (women)**
- "Oral ciprofloxacin (500 mg twice daily) for 7 days", where fluoroquinolone resistance is not
  known to exceed 10%.
- "levofloxacin (750 mg for 5 days)" once daily, under the same condition.
- "Oral trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (160/800 mg [1 double-strength tablet] twice-daily for 14
  days)" if the germ is known to be susceptible.
- Oral beta-lactams (penicillins and cephalosporins) are less effective; IDSA keeps "a duration of
  therapy of 10–14 days" for them and recommends a first injected dose such as ceftriaxone.

**Beyond home treatment.** A kidney infection with vomiting (pills will not stay down), sepsis
signs, pregnancy, or no improvement needs injected antibiotics and fluids. Without care, → 12-16.

## 7. Skin infection: cellulitis, erysipelas and impetigo

**What it is:** spreading redness, warmth, swelling and pain of the skin, often on a leg or the
face, sometimes with fever. *Impetigo* is a crusty surface infection, common in children.

**WHO AWaRe, Table 16.4, mild skin infections** (duration 5 days; WHO: "The optimal duration of
antibiotic treatment is not known")
- **Adults:** Amoxicillin+clavulanic acid (oral): 500 mg + 125 mg given every 8 hours, OR
  Cefalexin (oral): 500 mg given every 8 hours, OR Cloxacillin (oral): 500 mg given every 6 hours.
- **Children:** Amoxicillin+clavulanic acid (Table W2), OR Cefalexin (Table W3), OR Cloxacillin
  (Table W4).
- WHO prefers cloxacillin and cefalexin (narrower) "whenever possible". For a small patch of
  impetigo, WHO prefers an antibiotic ointment, "for example, a 5 day course with mupirocin 2%
  ointment", over pills.

**IDSA 2014 skin and soft tissue guideline, Table 2** (oral rows; adult dose / child dose)

| Drug | Adult | Child |
|---|---|---|
| Cephalexin | 500 mg qid po | 25–50 mg/kg/d 4 divided doses po |
| Dicloxacillin | 500 mg qid po | 25–50 mg/kg/d in 4 divided doses po |
| Clindamycin (staph, not MRSA) | 300–450 mg qid po | 25–30 mg/kg/d in 3 divided doses po |
| Penicillin VK (strep skin infections) | 250–500 mg every 6 h po | no oral child dose printed |

IDSA: typical cellulitis "should receive an antimicrobial agent that is active against
streptococci"; "The recommended duration of antimicrobial therapy is 5 days", longer if not
improved. Clindamycin is the usual penicillin-allergy choice in this table. Raise the limb.

**Beyond home treatment.** IDSA uses the signs of a body-wide reaction (SIRS): temperature above
38 °C or below 36 °C, breathing faster than 24 a minute, heart rate above 90 a minute. IDSA
advises hospital for concern about deeper infection, severe immune weakness, or failure of
treatment by mouth. WHO: watch for "change in colour of the wound due to necrosis" (dying,
dark tissue) and signs of sepsis. Dying tissue can mean necrotizing fasciitis, a deep infection
for which IDSA advises prompt surgery.

## 8. Abscess (boil or pocket of pus) after it has been drained

**What it is:** a painful, swollen lump with pus inside. IDSA: "Incision and drainage is
the recommended treatment for inflamed epidermoid cysts, carbuncles, abscesses". Draining is the
treatment; pills alone do not empty a pocket of pus. Drainage: → 12-14 and → 12-04.

**When antibiotics are added (IDSA 2014).** The decision "should be made based on the presence or
absence of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS)" (the signs in section 7). "An
antibiotic active against MRSA is recommended for patients with carbuncles or abscesses" who
have failed initial treatment. MRSA is staph that resists penicillin-type drugs.

**IDSA 2014, Table 2, MRSA rows** (adult dose / child dose)

| Drug | Adult | Child |
|---|---|---|
| Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole | 1–2 double-strength tablets bid po | 8–12 mg/kg/d (based on trimethoprim component) in either 4 divided doses IV or 2 divided doses po |
| Doxycycline, minocycline | 100 mg bid po | Not recommended for age < 8 y |
| Clindamycin | 300–450 mg qid po | 30–40 mg/kg/d in 3 divided doses po |

IDSA does not print a duration for a drained abscess. For recurrent abscesses it advises "a 5- to
10-day course of an antibiotic active against the pathogen isolated". These child rows print no
maximum; the adult dose is the ceiling.

## 9. Infected wounds and animal or human bites

**Clean first.** WHO: "Adequate cleaning and debridement are the cornerstone of initial
treatment": wash and flush "for about 15 minutes with soap or detergent and a lot of clean
water" (→ 12-02). Check tetanus for every wound (→ 12-04). After an animal bite, rabies must also
be considered; after a human bite, hepatitis B and C and HIV (WHO, chapter 18). WHO says the
need for rabies "post-exposure prophylaxis" (vaccine given after the bite) must always be
evaluated; antibiotics do nothing against rabies. WHO rabies exposure categories: touching or feeding animals or licks on intact skin,
no exposure; nibbling of uncovered skin or minor scratches without bleeding, exposure; bites or
scratches through the skin, saliva on broken skin or mucous membranes, or contact with bats,
severe exposure.

**Preventive antibiotics (wound not yet infected).** WHO: not required in most cases. They "may
be considered" for wounds of the face, hands or near a joint, and in severely immunocompromised
people. IDSA 2014: "Preemptive early antimicrobial therapy for 3–5 days is recommended" for bite
patients who are immunocompromised, have no spleen or advanced liver disease, have swelling of
the area, moderate to severe injuries (especially hand or face), or injuries that may reach bone
or a joint.

**WHO AWaRe, Table 18.4, mild infections from wounds and bites**
- **Adults:** Amoxicillin+clavulanic acid (oral): 500 mg + 125 mg given every 8 hours, OR
  Cefalexin (oral): 500 mg given every 8 hours, OR Cloxacillin (oral): 500 mg given every 6 hours.
- **Children:** Amoxicillin+clavulanic acid (Table W2), OR Cefalexin (Table W3), OR Cloxacillin
  (Table W4).
- **Duration:** "3 days (preventive treatment of wounds at high risk of infection)"; "5 days
  (treatment of infected wounds)".
- WHO: "Amoxicillin+clavulanic acid is the preferred treatment option for bite wound infections
  because of its activity against anaerobic bacteria" (germs that live without air, common in
  mouths).

**IDSA 2014, animal bites (adults, oral):** amoxicillin-clavulanate "875/125 mg bid";
doxycycline "100 mg bid" is among the other oral choices in the bite table. The table prints no
child doses.

**Beyond home treatment.** WHO: watch for signs of invasive infection, "change in colour of the
wound due to necrosis and signs of sepsis". Spreading redness and red streaks are on → 12-04.

## 10. Dental abscess and severe tooth infection

**Treatment is a procedure, not a pill.** WHO: "Dental procedures (e.g. drainage of an abscess
and tooth extraction) are the main treatment"; antibiotics "are not appropriate" for toothache,
inflamed pulp or dry socket, and should only complement drainage or extraction (WHO, chapter 8).
Field dentistry: → 12-06, → 12-20.

**When antibiotics are needed (WHO):** "severe, spreading dental infections", with signs such as
"facial swelling, inability to open the mouth, fever ≥ 38.0 °C and tachycardia" (fast heart
rate).

**WHO AWaRe, Table 8.4, severe dental infections**
- **Adults:** Amoxicillin (oral): 500 mg given every 8 hours, OR Phenoxymethylpenicillin (oral):
  500 mg (800 000 IU) given every 6 hours.
- **Children:** Amoxicillin (oral): 80–90 mg/kg/day (Table W1), OR Phenoxymethylpenicillin
  (oral): 10–15 mg/kg/dose (16 000–24 000 IU/kg/dose) given every 6 to 8 hours; ≥ 30 kg: use adult
  dose.
- **Duration:** "3 days, if adequate source control is achieved; otherwise 5 days". Reassess
  before the end.
- WHO footnote: for infections of the gum and soft tissue around a tooth (pericoronitis,
  necrotizing gum disease), "metronidazole is an option". WHO prints no allergy alternative.

**Beyond home treatment.** The emergency signs on → 12-06: swelling of the face, jaw or neck,
especially spreading; swelling that closes the eye; trouble swallowing, opening the mouth or
breathing, which is an airway emergency.

## 11. Diarrhoea when antibiotics apply (bloody diarrhoea, traveller's diarrhoea, cholera)

**Fluids first, always** (→ 12-05). WHO: antibiotics "should only be used in patients with severe
bloody diarrhoea (dysentery) or in immunocompromised patients" (WHO, chapter 14).

**WHO AWaRe, Table 14.6, selected cases of acute infectious diarrhoea**
- **First choice, adults:** Ciprofloxacin (oral): 500 mg given every 12 hours. **Children:**
  Ciprofloxacin (oral): 15 mg/kg/dose given every 12 hours (Table W5). 3 days.
- **Second choice, adults:** Azithromycin (oral): 500 mg given once a day (on day 1) followed by
  250 mg given once a day for 3 days, OR Cefixime (oral): 400 mg given once a day, OR
  Sulfamethoxazole+trimethoprim (oral): 800 mg + 160 mg given every 12 hours. Injection option:
  Ceftriaxone (IV/IM): 1 g given once a day.
- **Second choice, children:** Azithromycin (oral): 10 mg/kg/dose given once a day, OR Cefixime
  (oral): 10 mg/kg/dose given once a day, OR Sulfamethoxazole+trimethoprim (oral): 20 mg/kg +
  4 mg/kg given every 12 hours (3–< 6 kg: 100 mg/20 mg; 6–< 10 kg: 200 mg/40 mg; 10–< 15 kg,
  15–< 20 kg and 20–< 30 kg: 400 mg/80 mg; each given every 12 hours; ≥ 30 kg: use adult dose).
  Injection option: Ceftriaxone (IV/IM): 80 mg/kg/dose given once a day.
- **Second-choice durations:** azithromycin 4 days; cefixime 3 days (adults), 5 days
  (children); sulfamethoxazole+trimethoprim 5 days; ceftriaxone 3 days.
- WHO notes: azithromycin is preferred where ciprofloxacin resistance is common; cefixime,
  ceftriaxone and sulfamethoxazole+trimethoprim do not work against *Campylobacter*;
  sulfamethoxazole+trimethoprim should not be used empirically when shigellosis is suspected. "If
  symptoms do not resolve within 24–48 hours of treatment, consider *Entamoeba histolytica* or
  *Giardia intestinalis*" (parasites, which need a different medicine).

**WHO AWaRe, Table 14.7, cholera** (profuse watery diarrhoea in an outbreak; rehydration is the
most important treatment)
- **Adults, first choice:** Azithromycin (oral): 1 g, single dose, OR Doxycycline (oral): 300 mg,
  single dose (or 100 mg given every 12 hours for 3 days if single dose is not tolerated).
  **Second choice:** Ciprofloxacin (oral): 1 g, single dose.
- **Children, first choice:** Azithromycin (oral): 20 mg/kg, single dose. **Second choice:**
  Ciprofloxacin (oral): 15 mg/kg, single dose, OR Doxycycline (oral): < 45 kg (< 12 years): 2 to
  4 mg/kg, single dose; > 45 kg (> 12 years): 300 mg, single dose.

**CDC Yellow Book 2026, Table 1.6.1, travelers' diarrhea (adults)**

| Drug | Dose | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Azithromycin | 1,000 mg | Single or divided dose |
| Azithromycin | 500 mg QD | 3 days |
| Ciprofloxacin | 750 mg | Single dose |
| Ciprofloxacin | 500 mg BID | 3 days |
| Levofloxacin | 500 mg QD | 1–3 days |
| Rifamycin SV | 388 mg BID | 3 days |
| Rifaximin | 200 mg TID | 3 days |

Yellow Book notes: azithromycin is the "Preferred treatment for dysentery or febrile diarrhea";
for single-dose regimens, "If symptoms are not resolved after 24 hours, continue daily dosing for
up to 3 days"; rifamycin SV and rifaximin are not for suspected *Campylobacter*, *Salmonella*,
*Shigella* or other invasive diarrhoea. Antibiotic treatment is "not recommended" for mild
diarrhoea. In children, "The main treatment for TD in children is ORS" (oral rehydration
solution) and "macrolides (e.g., azithromycin) are considered first-line antibiotic therapy";
the Yellow Book prints no child dose (WHO's child rows are above). It warns that antibiotics for
children "must be weighed against the risk of hemolytic uremic syndrome" (a kidney-failing
complication of some *E. coli* infections).

**Beyond home treatment.** The dehydration warning signs on → 12-05, blood in the stool, and a
baby or young child who cannot keep fluids down.

## 12. Early Lyme disease (the expanding "bull's-eye" rash after a tick bite)

**CDC, Clinical Treatment of Erythema Migrans Rash (2026)**, "consistent with guidance published
by the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA), American Academy of Neurology (AAN), and
American College of Rheumatology (ACR)"

| Who | Drug | Dose | Maximum | Days |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adults | Doxycycline | 100 mg, twice per day orally | N/A | 10–14 |
| Adults | Amoxicillin | 500 mg, three times per day orally | N/A | 14 |
| Adults | Cefuroxime | 500 mg, twice per day orally | N/A | 14 |
| Children | Doxycycline | 4.4 mg/kg per day orally, divided into 2 doses | 100 mg per dose | 10–14 |
| Children | Amoxicillin | 50 mg/kg per day orally, divided into 3 doses | 500 mg per dose | 14 |
| Children | Cefuroxime | 30 mg/kg per day orally, divided into 2 doses | 500 mg per dose | 14 |

CDC notes: where different durations work, "the shorter duration is preferred". "For people
intolerant of amoxicillin, doxycycline, and cefuroxime, the macrolide azithromycin may be used",
"although it is less effective", and they "should be closely monitored to ensure that symptoms
resolve" (the page prints no azithromycin dose). Regimens "may need to be adjusted depending on a
person's age, medical history, underlying health conditions, pregnancy status, or allergies".
After any tick bite, CDC says: "If you develop a rash or fever within several weeks of removing a
tick, see your doctor" (CDC, What to Do After a Tick Bite).

## 13. Rocky Mountain spotted fever and other tick-borne rickettsial disease

Fever and illness in the days or weeks after a tick bite can be a rickettsial disease such as
Rocky Mountain spotted fever.

**CDC, Clinical Care of Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever (2025)**
- "Doxycycline is the first-line treatment for adults and children of all ages", including
  "presumptive treatment in patients of all ages, including those under 8 years old".
- "Adults: 100 mg every 12 hours".
- "Children under 45 kg (100 lbs): 2.2 mg/kg body weight given twice a day". Children 45 kg and
  over take the adult dose (doxycycline FDA label).
- Treat "with doxycycline for at least 3 days after the fever subsides"; "Minimum course of
  treatment is 5 to 7 days".

CDC's Lyme table (section 12) also gives doxycycline a child dose with no age limit. The FDA
doxycycline label warns of permanent tooth staining under 8 years and says to use it then "only
when the potential benefits are expected to outweigh the risks in severe or life-threatening
conditions (e.g., anthrax, Rocky Mountain spotted fever)" (→ 12-23).

## 14. Pink eye (bacterial conjunctivitis)

**Usually no antibiotic.** WHO: most infectious conjunctivitis is viral and clears without
treatment in 7 to 10 days. Bacterial conjunctivitis (red eye with thick pus and normal vision)
may be treated with drops or ointment "to shorten the duration of symptoms" (WHO, chapter 10).
Wash hands; do not share towels.

**WHO AWaRe, Table 10.3, bacterial conjunctivitis (children and adults)** (duration 5 days)
- Gentamicin (eye drops): 0.3%, 1 drop in the affected eye every 6 hours, OR
- Ofloxacin (eye drops): 0.3%, 1 drop in the affected eye every 6 hours, OR
- Tetracycline (eye ointment): 1%, 1 cm in the affected eye every 6 hours.

WHO: steroid eye drops are "not usually needed" and "might even make the condition worse" if the
cause is herpes or the cornea is affected.

**Beyond home treatment.** Eye pain (WHO: pain "usually indicates corneal involvement"), blurred
or reduced vision, or very heavy pus with a swollen lid (WHO: "hyperacute" conjunctivitis, mostly
gonorrhoea, needs urgent eye care because the cornea can perforate). **A newborn with red,
discharging eyes** needs whole-body treatment, not drops: WHO prints ceftriaxone injection for
gonococcal infection (first 5 days of life) and oral azithromycin 20 mg/kg given once a day for 3
days for chlamydia (after 5 days). Red, swollen skin around the eye with fever: see section 3.

## When any infection is beyond home treatment

- The sepsis signs in the safety box, at any point.
- No improvement on the antibiotic, or getting worse on it. For pneumonia WHO says improvement
  "should be evident within 48–72 hours".
- Vomiting that stops pills staying down.
- A baby under 3 months with fever (→ 12-13), pregnancy (→ 02-12), a weak immune system or no
  spleen, kidney or liver disease (→ 02-15).
- A new rash, hives, swelling of the face or trouble breathing after a dose: stop the antibiotic;
  this is an allergic reaction (→ 02-03 for anaphylaxis).
- Severe or bloody diarrhoea during an antibiotic or after it: the FDA labels warn that C. diff
  diarrhoea "has been reported to occur over 2 months after" the antibiotic (→ 12-23).

If any care exists, take the person there with this page and the log of what was given, when and
how much. If none exists, → 12-16.

## Keep a record

Write down for every course: the person, their weight and the date weighed, the drug, the
strength on the bottle (for liquids, mg per 5 mL), the source row used, each dose time, and how
they responded (→ 25-05). The family's allergies and medicines are on → 25-09.
