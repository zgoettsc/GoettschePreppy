# Building a real first-aid and trauma kit

**What this page is for.** It lists what to buy and store so your household can handle the
injuries and illnesses that actually happen when no clinic is open. It is a supply list only.
How to use each item is on the treatment pages it points to.

> **Supplies are the smaller half of this.** A kit you do not know how to use is a bag of
> expensive props. **Take a Stop the Bleed course and a hands-on first-aid and CPR course
> now**, while classes still exist. They are short and inexpensive, and they are worth more
> than anything on this list. Nobody in your household needs to be a doctor; this binder is
> written for people who are not.

> **Life-critical medicines do not go in this kit.** Epinephrine auto-injectors (a pen that
> injects adrenaline into the thigh for a severe allergic reaction, → 02-03), insulin
> (→ 02-01, → 02-02) and any other medicine someone cannot go without belong in their own
> known place, reachable in seconds, never buried in a general first-aid bag.
> {{#if has.epinephrine}}Your household carries epinephrine: everyone, including older
> children, should know exactly where it is.{{/if}}

## Do this now

1. **Take the courses.** Stop the Bleed (about an hour) and a Red Cross or equivalent first
   aid and CPR class. Every adult who could be the one responding.
2. **Count what you already own** against the "buy first" table below and write the counts on
   → 22-04. Assume you do not have a trauma kit until those counts are written down.
3. **Check everyone's tetanus vaccination** while clinics are open. A puncture wound with no
   clinic and an out-of-date tetanus shot is a dangerous combination, and it is easy to fix
   now (CDC, About Tetanus; → 12-04).
4. **Build the everyday kit first, then the trauma kit.** You will use adhesive bandages,
   tweezers and pain relief a hundred times for every time you need a tourniquet.
5. **Put a trauma kit where wood is cut and fires are lit.** Axes, saws, chainsaws and stoves
   are where serious injuries happen (→ 16-06, → 06-01).
6. **Stock quantities, not a boxed kit.** Commercial kits hold about six of everything. For
   {{ household.size }} people in a long event you want **boxes** of gauze, not packets.
7. **Photograph where each kit lives** and add the photo below and on → 12-08.

## Design the kit around what will actually happen

Forget television. In a long emergency at home, the realistic list of injuries and illness
looks like this:

| How often | What | Where it is covered |
|---|---|---|
| **Constant** | Cuts, scrapes, splinters, blisters, minor burns | → 12-02, → 12-03 |
| **Very likely** | Axe, saw and knife wounds from processing wood and food | → 12-02 |
| **Very likely** | Burns from stoves, open fires and hot liquids{{#if has.child}}, especially children{{/if}} | → 12-03 |
| **Very likely** | Diarrhoea and vomiting; dehydration (losing more water than you take in) | → 12-05 |
| **Likely** | Sprains, falls{{#if is.cold_winters}} on ice{{/if}}, broken bones | → 12-09, → 11-06 |
| **Likely** | Infected wounds, the thing that kills when there are no antibiotics | → 12-04 |
| **Likely** | Cold injuries | → 12-10, → 08-05 |
| **Likely** | Dental problems | → 12-06 |
| **Ongoing** | Long-term conditions and the medicines they depend on | → 12-11 |
| Less likely | Massive bleeding; this is what the trauma kit is for | → 12-02 |

**Design for the top of that list.** Most of your supplies should be gauze, tape, wound
cleaning and burn care, not exotic gear.

## The three tiers (detail on → 12-08)

1. **Everyday kit**: in the kitchen, used weekly. Bandages, antiseptic, tweezers, pain
   relief{{#if has.child}}, children's supplies{{/if}}.
2. **Trauma kit**: for serious bleeding. Small, fast to reach, in the places injuries happen:
   the woodpile or work area, the vehicle, the go-bag.
3. **Long-haul supplies**: the bulk stock. Boxes of gauze, tape, saline, splint material and
   over-the-counter (OTC, no prescription needed) medicines in quantity. Stored, rotated,
   counted.

## The core list

**Bleeding and wounds** (→ 12-02)
- **Tourniquet ×2 to 3** (a strap with a stick-like handle, the windlass, that you twist to
  stop blood flow to a limb), and **training in how to use one.** Buy a genuine
  windlass-type tourniquet of a model the Stop the Bleed course teaches. Cheap copies can snap
  or slip under load.
- **Hemostatic gauze ×2 to 4** (gauze treated with a substance that speeds clotting) and plain
  **compressed gauze** in quantity.
- **Pressure bandages ×2 to 4** (a roll bandage with a built-in pad and a clip you can put on
  with one hand; often sold as "emergency" or "Israeli-type" bandages).
- **Sterile gauze pads** in many sizes, **by the box**. This is your highest-volume item.
- Roller gauze, self-adherent wrap, medical tape (cloth and paper).
- Non-adherent dressings (pads that do not stick to a wound) and large abdominal pads.
- **Nitrile gloves by the box.** Not latex: latex allergy is common. Gloves protect the
  person giving care, which matters when there is no clinic for them either.
- Trauma shears, fine-point tweezers for splinters, an irrigation syringe (20 to 60 mL) for
  washing wounds.
- Sterile saline or clean drinkable water for washing wounds (→ 03-04).
- Wound-closure strips and skin adhesive. **Read → 12-14 before closing any wound.** Most
  wounds in a long emergency should not be closed.
- Antiseptics: povidone-iodine, chlorhexidine, alcohol wipes.
- Antibiotic ointment (→ 12-07).

**Burns** (→ 12-03)
- Sterile non-adherent burn dressings, clean cling film (a recommended burn covering), and
  plenty of clean water for cooling.

**Splints and support** (→ 12-09)
- **Moldable padded splint ×2** (a flexible aluminium-and-foam strip you bend to shape),
  elastic bandages, triangular bandages, athletic tape, a sling.

**Checking a patient**
- Thermometers (**several**, plus batteries), a blood pressure cuff, a pulse oximeter (a
  fingertip clip that reads oxygen level and pulse; needs batteries), a good headlamp
  (→ 10-01), a notepad and pen for a treatment log, and a watch with a second hand.

**Medicines**: see → 12-07 and → 12-13. Follow the label. For children, use only the
published dosing tables on → 12-13 and the label; never guess.

**Reference books on paper**
- A real first-aid manual. *Where There Is No Doctor* and *Where There Is No Dentist*
  (Hesperian) are widely used for exactly this situation. Buy physical copies.
- Your **family medical summary** (→ 25-09): conditions, medicines, allergies, blood types,
  vaccination dates. Written down, in this binder.

## The mistakes to avoid

- **Buying a boxed kit and calling it done.** A "300-piece" kit is mostly adhesive bandages. It
  is a restock source, not a plan.
- **Skipping the training.** Supplies without skills is the most common failure.
- **Burying the trauma kit in a closet.** Serious bleeding is a minutes problem. The kit has
  to be where the injury happens.
- **Nothing in the vehicle or the go-bag.**
- **Ignoring expiry dates.** Sterile packaging degrades; medicines lose strength. Count
  everything twice a year (→ 22-04).
{{#if has.child}}- **No children's sizes.** Small bandages, a child-suitable thermometer,
  children's medicine strengths and an oral dosing syringe. A one-year-old and a four-year-old
  need different everything.
{{else}}- **No children's sizes.** Even without children at home, you may be caring for
  someone else's child. Keep a few small bandages and children's medicine strengths.
{{/if}}- **Latex.** Use nitrile.

## Two things that prevent more harm than any kit treats

1. **Handwashing and clean water** (CDC, About Hygiene; → 13-02). Preventing infection is far
   cheaper than treating it when there are no antibiotics (→ 12-04).
2. **Not getting hurt.** Sharp tools used carefully (→ 16-02), traction on ice (→ 11-06), fire
   discipline (→ 10-02){{#if has.child}} and children kept away from the stove (→ 20-02){{/if}}
   prevent more harm than any amount of gauze fixes.

## Scavenge or substitute

- **Dressings:** clean cotton cloth, boiled and dried; clean sheets torn into strips.
  Sanitary pads and disposable diapers make good absorbent dressings: they come sealed and soak
  up a lot.
- **Washing wounds:** a clean squeeze bottle, a plastic bag with a pinhole, any syringe, and
  clean drinkable water (→ 03-04). Pressure and volume matter more than sterility.
- **Splints:** cardboard, magazines, sticks, foam pads, rolled newspaper (→ 12-09).
- **Tape:** duct tape or electrical tape over gauze, not directly on skin.
- **Slings and triangular bandages:** a T-shirt, a pillowcase, a scarf.
- **Cold packs:** snow, ice, a bag of anything frozen.
- **Tourniquet:** improvised tourniquets often fail. Buy real ones. If you must improvise, it
  needs a wide strap and a rigid stick to twist. Read → 12-02 first.
- **From other houses (→ 22-02):** medicine cabinets, veterinary supplies, first-aid stations
  in workplaces and vehicles, school and church kits.

## When it runs out

- **Gauze, tape and gloves run out fastest.** One significant wound with dressing changes for
  two weeks uses an astonishing amount. Stock in bulk and list it on → 23-02.
- **Count dressing changes, not packets.** One wound, changed twice a day for ten days, is
  twenty dressings, for one injury.
- **Ration:** wash and reuse cloth dressings, boiled, dried and stored clean. This is how it
  was done for most of history and it works. Keep sterile commercial dressings for fresh, deep
  or dirty wounds.
- **Resupply:** scavenging (above) and trade. Medical supplies are among the most valuable
  trade goods there are (→ 18-02). That cuts both ways: you can get them, and people will want
  yours. Think about what you tell others (→ 14-03).
- **Substitute:** clean cloth for gauze, boiled water for saline, honey as a wound dressing
  (→ 12-02), soap and water for antiseptic. **Cleanliness substitutes for almost everything;
  nothing substitutes for cleanliness.**
- **What cannot be substituted:** epinephrine, insulin and the other life-critical medicines
  on the condition pages (→ 12-11). Everything else on this page has a workaround.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** all three tiers set up. Everyday kit in the kitchen, trauma kits at the
  work area and in the vehicle, bulk stock stored and counted. Take the courses now.
- **B (moving):** a compact kit per adult: tourniquet, pressure bandage, gauze, tape, gloves,
  blister care, pain relief, **and each person's life-critical medicines first** (→ 22-03,
  → 01-07). Blisters and cold injury are the realistic problems on the move.
- **C (resettled):** further from help, more dangerous work (axes, animals, building), so you
  need more capability, not less. A trauma kit at every work site, a real supply store, and
  ideally someone in the group with real medical training (→ 18-03).
