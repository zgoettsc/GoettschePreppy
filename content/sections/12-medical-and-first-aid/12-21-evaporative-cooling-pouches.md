# Evaporative cooling pouches for medicine: how to use them

**What this page is for.** Using an evaporative cooling pouch (a fabric wallet with crystals you
soak in water) to keep insulin or another heat-sensitive medicine out of the heat with no power.
What these pouches do, the mistakes that ruin them, and where they fit in a cold chain (the
unbroken chain of cool storage a medicine needs; → 29-07).

> **SAFETY**
> - **This is not a refrigerator.** An evaporative pouch keeps medicine **out of the heat**; it
>   does not reach fridge temperatures and it is **not** a way to store an unopened supply long
>   term (→ 29-07, → 12-07).
> - **Two mistakes destroy it completely: sealing it in a plastic bag, and using it in humid air
>   with no airflow.** Both stop evaporation, which is the entire mechanism.
> - **It gives no protection against freezing.** In cold weather the danger runs the other way.
> - **A frozen-then-thawed insulin vial or pen is discarded.** It loses strength with no way to
>   tell, and the published guidance is unanimous. This is a floor (→ 30-01), not a choice.
> - **The published storage windows come from the label and the FDA, not from this page**
>   (FDA, Information Regarding Insulin Storage and Switching Between Products in an Emergency).

## Do this now

1. **Read the instructions for your own pouch and keep them with it.** Soak time, reactivation
   interval and the stated temperature range differ by product. **This page teaches the method;
   your product's instructions govern the numbers.**
2. **Activate one now and run it for three days with a thermometer inside and no medicine.** Find
   out what it really does in your conditions before you rely on it.
3. **Buy the size that fits what you carry** (a pen, a vial, a box of pump supplies) **and a
   second one**, because one is always drying or being re-soaked.
4. **Put it in the medical kit with a note of its reactivation interval** (→ 22-06), and
   photograph the set:

## How it works, in one paragraph

The pouch holds crystals that soak up water and turn into gel. **As that water evaporates through
the fabric, it carries heat away**, keeping the contents below the surrounding air temperature.
That is the whole thing. **Anything that stops evaporation stops the cooling**, which is why every
failure below is a version of "you sealed it."

{{figure fig-evaporative-pouch}}

## How to use it

```
[ ] SOAK the inner pouch in cold water for the time your instructions give.
    The crystals swell into a gel. Under-soaking is the usual beginner
    mistake; over-soaking usually just means more to pat off.
[ ] PAT THE OUTSIDE DRY with a towel. Wet gel inside, not dripping fabric.
[ ] PUT IN the medicine, in its own packaging, and close the pouch.
[ ] LEAVE IT OPEN TO THE AIR. On a belt, in an open bag, on a counter.
    ** NOT sealed in a zip bag. NOT inside an airtight cooler. NOT in the fridge. **
[ ] RE-SOAK on your product's schedule, or sooner in dry heat.
    Planning figure: one maker (Frio) states a soak lasts on the order of
    TWO DAYS. That is the manufacturer's figure; your own insert governs,
    and heat and dry air shorten it.
[ ] CHECK IT WITH A THERMOMETER, especially the first few times and
    whenever conditions change.
```

## The five ways people ruin it

| Mistake | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Sealing it in a plastic bag** | Evaporation stops. It becomes an insulated pouch holding whatever temperature it started at, often worse than nothing |
| **Putting it in a cooler or the fridge** | Same. And it wets everything else |
| **Using it in humid, still air** | Evaporation works poorly when the air is already full of moisture. **Know this before you count on it in a humid summer** |
| **Not patting the outside dry** | Wet packaging, ruined labels, eventually mould |
| **Forgetting to re-soak** | It quietly stops working. It looks the same either way |

> **The pattern:** every one of these is invisible. **The thermometer is what makes this device
> trustworthy**, not the pouch.

## What it is good for, and what it is not

| Good for | Not for |
|---|---|
| **In-use insulin or other medicine at room temperature**, kept out of summer heat | **Long-term storage of an unopened supply**, which needs proper refrigeration (→ 29-07) |
| **Travel and moving** (Phase B): no power, no ice, nothing to charge | Reaching refrigerator temperatures |
| **A hot vehicle, a hot day, a summer power cut** | **Any protection against freezing** |
| Other heat-sensitive medicines (check each label) | A substitute for counting days of supply (→ 12-07) |

**Where it fits in a cold chain:** it is the **no-power, warm-weather layer.** Powered cooling (a
12-volt fridge, a power station) is on → 09-10; the cold-weather problem is the opposite and is on
→ 29-07. **This pouch solves exactly one of those three problems**, and solves it well.

## Medicine that got too warm anyway

Every insulin and refrigerated medicine has a published window it may spend unrefrigerated, on its
label or package insert; the FDA's emergency guidance collects the insulin windows (FDA,
Information Regarding Insulin Storage). The American Diabetes Association adds handling advice for
disasters (ADA, Insulin Storage and Syringe Safety; Switching Between Insulin Products in Disaster
Response Situations).

**What to do with medicine that has gone past its window is a trade-off.** Throwing it out at the
end of the published window is the safe rule. Some households, with nothing else available, would
keep using it and watch more closely, accepting that it may be weaker. Card DC-08 explains the
risk and the options.
Your household's choice: {{choice DC-08}} (→ 30-09)

**Frozen insulin is not part of that choice.** Frozen and thawed means discard (→ 30-01).

## Cold weather: the reversal

{{#if is.cold_winters}}In your climate, for part of the year the risk is not heat but
freezing.{{else}}Even where winters are mild, a cold night or a cold car can freeze medicine.{{/if}}
**This pouch does not help at all with that**: a wet pouch in freezing air is worse than a dry
insulated one.
- **In cold weather, carry insulin and other liquid medicines against your body**, inside the coat.
- **Do not activate a pouch and leave it in a cold vehicle.** You have made a cold, wet object.
- **Medicine does not live in an unattended car in either season** (→ 22-05).
- Epinephrine auto-injectors must not freeze either (→ 02-03).

## Care and maintenance

- **Rinse and dry it fully between uses**, then store it dry and flat. Crystals stored damp grow
  mould.
- **Wash the outer fabric** as the instructions say; do not machine-wash the crystal pouch.
- **They wear out.** The crystals eventually stop swelling properly. **If soaking takes longer
  than it used to, or it dries out much faster, replace it** and note it on → 26-08.
- **Keep two**, so one can dry while the other is in use.
- **Never leave medicine in a pouch you have not re-soaked**, assuming it still works.

## The honest limit

A pouch extends how long medicine survives warm conditions (→ 23-04). It does not make cold, it
does not add to the supply, and **it does nothing about the number that actually matters: days of
medicine on hand** (→ 12-07, → 23-01). Own one, understand it, check it with a thermometer, and do
not let it distract from resupply.

## Scavenge or substitute

- **A damp cloth wrapped round a container in moving air** is the same physics, crudely.
- **A clay-pot cooler** (a pot inside a larger pot with wet sand between them, called a zeer) is the
  same idea at household scale (→ 29-07).
- **Neither replaces checking the temperature, and neither protects against freezing.**

## When it runs out

- **A pouch lasts years with care; the crystals are the part that ages.**
- **Re-soaking is a habit, and the habit is what fails.** Put it on the daily list in hot weather
  (→ 25-05).
- **Resupply:** replaceable while shops exist; improvised afterwards with cloth and airflow.
- **Substitute:** cloth and airflow, a clay-pot cooler, a cool basement, night-time air. **What has
  no substitute is the medicine itself** (→ 23-04).

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** the summer layer of the cold chain; the fridge and powered cooling (→ 09-10) do
  the heavy lifting.
- **B (moving):** **this is its moment.** No power, no ice, nothing to charge. It travels in the
  medical kit in the cabin, and it works while you move (→ 22-06, → 01-07).
- **C (resettled):** combine it with a cool cellar or shaded ground for the main supply, and keep
  the pouch for what is in use.
