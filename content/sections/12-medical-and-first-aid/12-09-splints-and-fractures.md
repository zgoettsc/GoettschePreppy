# Splints, sprains and broken bones

**What this page is for.** Recognising a broken bone (fracture), splinting it, caring for
sprains, and planning for the weeks when an injured adult cannot work.

> **SAFETY**
> - **An open fracture, with bone through the skin, is a surgical emergency** with a very high
>   infection risk (→ 12-04). It is beyond household care.
> - **Do not try to straighten (realign) a broken bone from this page.** Realigning is a
>   procedure with real risk to nerves and blood vessels. It is taught hands-on in wilderness
>   first-aid courses; take one now (→ 24-01). The one exception, a limb with no pulse below the
>   break when no care is coming, is a decision card (→ 30-30).
> - **After any high-energy fall, check the head, neck, back, pelvis and belly before the
>   obvious limb.**
> - **A person lying still on cold ground is becoming hypothermic** (dangerously cold; CDC,
>   Preventing Hypothermia). Insulate them from the ground at once.

## Do this now: suspected broken bone

1. **Check the whole person first** after a big fall: head (→ 12-19), neck and back, pelvis,
   belly. Suspected neck, back, pelvis or hip injury: **do not move them** unless they are in
   immediate danger where they lie.
2. **Stop any bleeding** (→ 12-02). Bone showing? Cover it with a clean dressing; do not push it
   back.
3. **Check beyond the injury: colour, warmth, pulse, feeling, wiggle.** Write it down.
4. **Splint it as you found it.** Joint above and joint below. Pad every gap.
5. **Check colour, warmth, pulse and feeling again.** Worse? Loosen the splint at once.
6. **Get them off the cold ground, warm, and give pain relief** (→ 12-13).
7. **Re-check the splint every hour or so.** Swelling keeps building for hours.

## Do this now: preparation

- **Buy traction cleats for boots and keep sand or ash for paths** (→ 11-06). Prevention is
  cheap compared to the injury.
- **Buy two moldable padded splints and elastic bandages**: light, reusable, and they make any
  fracture manageable.
- **Take a hands-on first-aid course that covers splinting.** Having done it once beats reading
  about it.
- **Plan for "one adult is down" now**: who does what, what gets dropped{{#if has.child}}, what
  the children can safely help with (→ 20-07){{/if}}, and how the household runs short-handed.
  Write it on → 00-07.

## Recognising what you are dealing with

You often cannot tell a bad sprain from a broken bone without an X-ray. **When in doubt, treat
it as broken.** Splinting a sprain costs a few days of inconvenience; walking on a fracture
costs far more.

**Signs of a broken bone:**
- **Deformity**: an angle, shortening, a limb that looks wrong
- **A crack or snap** heard or felt at the time
- **Cannot bear weight or use the limb at all**
- **One spot that is extremely painful to press**
- **Grating** when it moves
- **Fast, heavy swelling and bruising**
- **A wound over the suspected break**: assume it is an open fracture

**Signs it is an emergency beyond splinting:**
- **Bone showing, or a wound over the break** (open fracture)
- **The hand or foot beyond the injury is pale, cold, blue, numb or has no pulse.** Blood flow
  may be cut off, and the limb can be lost within hours. If any care exists, go now (→ 12-16).
  When no care is coming, published first-aid guidance says to try to bring the limb back toward
  its normal position, and wilderness first aid teaches gentle traction for it (MedlinePlus,
  Broken Bone; Wilderness First Aid Scope of Practice); splinting as found risks the limb, one
  gentle pull risks pain and added injury. Your household's choice: {{choice DC-29}} → 30-30.
  It must be learned hands-on, not from a page.
- **Suspected spine, neck, pelvis or hip fracture**: do not move the person except to save them
  from an immediate hazard
- **Suspected skull fracture or significant head injury** (→ 12-19)
- **Thigh bone (femur) fracture**: major internal bleeding risk
- **Several fractures, or a fracture with shock** (pale, cold, clammy, fast weak pulse,
  confused)

## Splinting: the principles

**The goal is to stop the injured part moving.** A splint reduces pain, prevents more damage and
lets the person be moved.

1. **Check circulation, feeling and movement beyond the injury before and after splinting**:
   colour, warmth, pulse, feeling, wiggle. **If splinting makes any of these worse, loosen it at
   once.** This is the most important step and the most often skipped.
2. **Splint it in the position found** where possible. Do not force a limb straight.
3. **Hold still the joint above and the joint below** the break.
4. **Pad everything**: bony points, gaps, anywhere the splint touches. Unpadded splints cause
   pressure sores over hours and days.
5. **Secure firmly but not tightly, and check again as swelling builds.** A splint that was fine
   at noon can cut off blood by evening. **Re-check repeatedly.**
6. **Raise the limb** where you can, and use cold early to limit swelling. Wrap ice or snow in
   cloth; never put it straight on skin.
7. **Treat the person, not just the limb**: pain relief (→ 12-13), fluids, warmth.
   {{#if is.cold_winters}}In your winters, a person lying still outdoors is a hypothermia case in
   progress (→ 08-05).{{/if}} Get insulation under them first.

## Sprains and strains

Most soft-tissue injuries are managed with **rest, cold early on, compression and raising the
limb**, then a gradual return to use.
- **If they cannot bear weight at all, treat it as a fracture** until shown otherwise.
- **Compression wraps must not be so tight they block circulation.** Same check as above.
- **Return to use gradually.** Keeping it still too long causes stiffness and weakness, which
  means a slower return to being useful.

## What a household can manage, and what it cannot

| Injury | Household capability |
|---|---|
| Finger or toe | Usually manageable: tape it to its neighbour (buddy-taping) or splint |
| Wrist or forearm | Splint and hold still; **may heal crooked without realignment** |
| Ankle | Splint, no weight on it; long recovery; **a major loss of working hands** |
| Collarbone | Sling and support; usually heals |
| Ribs | Pain relief and **deep-breathing exercises**: the danger is pneumonia from shallow breathing, not the bone. **Never tape, strap or bind the chest**: it stops the deep breaths that prevent pneumonia |
| **Open fracture** | **Beyond household care.** Clean dressing, splint, do not push bone back in, treat as a life-threatening infection risk (→ 12-04) |
| **Thigh bone** | Beyond household care; major bleeding risk |
| **Spine, pelvis, hip** | **Do not move.** Beyond household care |
| **Skull or significant head injury** | Beyond household care; watch for worsening (→ 12-19) |

**→ 12-16 covers what to do when something is beyond you and there is no hospital.** Think it
through before it happens.

## The part people do not plan for: losing a pair of hands

A broken bone takes an adult out of the work for weeks or months.
- **An adult on crutches cannot haul water, split wood{{#if has.young_child}} or carry a small
  child{{/if}}.**
- The other adults now do **everything**, including caring for the injured one.
- **If the injured person is the one who manages someone's medicines or medical devices**,
  someone else must already know how (→ 12-11). Every adult in the household should be able to
  do every life-critical medical routine.
- **Plan it in advance** (→ 00-07): what gets dropped, who can help, what neighbours could do
  (→ 18-01), and how long the household can run short-handed.
- **An injured adult may make leaving impossible** (→ 01-02).

## Prevention: where the leverage is

- **Traction on ice; sand and light the paths** (→ 11-06, → 10-01).{{#if is.cold_winters}}
  Falls on ice are the most likely source of a broken bone in your climate.{{/if}}
{{#if has.young_child}}- **Do not carry a small child across ice.** Clear the path or use a sled.
{{/if}}- **Ladder discipline.** Most household falls from height involve ladders, and there is
  no emergency room.
- **No sharp or heavy tool work when exhausted, cold or in poor light** (→ 16-06).
- **Sleds and carts instead of carrying** heavy loads on uneven or icy ground.
- **Handrails and lit stairs** in a dark house (→ 10-01).

## Scavenge or substitute

- **Splint material:** cardboard (excellent: stiff, light, bendable when scored), rolled
  magazines or newspaper, sticks, a rolled foam sleeping pad, wooden slats, a broom handle,
  metal flashing, a book.
- **Padding:** clothing, towels, blankets, foam, moss, dry grass. **Use plenty.**
- **Securing:** torn cloth strips, cord, duct tape (**not directly on skin**), belts, elastic
  from clothing.
- **Sling:** a T-shirt, pillowcase, scarf, or the person's own shirt front pinned up.
- **Crutch:** a forked branch padded at the top, a hoe or rake handle, ski poles, a chair pushed
  as a walker.
- **Cold:** snow in a bag, ice, cold water, anything frozen. **Wrap it; never ice straight on
  skin**, especially when frostbite is a risk.

## When it runs out

- **Splint supplies barely run down.** Splints are reusable, elastic bandages wash, and
  cardboard is free. Supply is not the limit here.
- **What runs down is working hands, for weeks.** Put "one adult unavailable for 6 to 12 weeks"
  on the planning list and work out what it means for water, wood, food and medical care.
- **Ration work, not supplies.** Drop everything non-essential. Water, warmth, food and medical
  care come first; the garden and projects stop.
- **Substitute:**{{#if has.child}} children can genuinely help with small tasks, and it is good
  for them (→ 20-07);{{/if}} neighbours are a real resource, and this is the kind of concrete,
  bounded request that builds mutual aid (→ 18-01).
- **The permanent answer is prevention.** Cleats, sand, light, sleds, handrails, and no
  dangerous work when tired. All cheap, and the injury they prevent could halve the household's
  working strength for two months.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):**{{#if is.cold_winters}} ice is the hazard.{{/if}} Sand and light the path from
  the door to the woodpile and the water, keep cleats by the door, and use a sled for anything
  heavy.
- **B (moving):** **an injury on the move is one of the worst situations in the binder.** It
  can strand the household in the open. Move deliberately, do not carry more than you can
  manage, and treat an injured adult as a reason to stop and shelter (→ 01-02, → 01-03).
- **C (resettled):** more heavy work, more ladders, more animals, further from help. **Build the
  safety in early**: good steps, handrails, lit paths, carts. Keep splint material and crutches
  where they can be found.
