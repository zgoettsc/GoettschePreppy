# Poisoning and swallowed objects: the offline protocol

**What this page is for.** What to do when someone swallows, breathes in, or is splashed by
something poisonous, and Poison Control cannot be reached. Prevention comes first, because once
care is out of reach it is almost the whole defence.

> **SAFETY**
> - **While phones work, Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 is the first call, every time.** Free,
>   24/7, and better than any page. **This page is for when that number cannot be reached.**
> - **Do not make anyone vomit. Do not try to neutralise. Nothing by mouth to anyone drowsy,
>   seizing or unconscious.**
> - **A swallowed button battery is a surgical emergency.**
> - **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medicines or chlorine products.** This is a
>   floor (→ 30-01). A house stocked for a long emergency, with medicines, fuels, bleach, lye and
>   pool chemicals, plus a curious toddler, is the most predictable poisoning in this binder.
> - **Never mix bleach or pool shock with ammonia, acids or other cleaners.** It makes chlorine
>   gas. Also a floor (→ 30-01).

## Do this now: someone swallowed something

1. **Get it away from them.** Have a child spit out what remains. Sweep the mouth with a finger
   only if you can see the object.
2. **Grab the container and keep it.** What, and how much?
3. **Call Poison Control, 1-800-222-1222**, on any phone that works.
4. **No phone? Read the label's first-aid statement and follow it.**
5. **Do not make them vomit.**
6. **Awake: sit them up. Drowsy or vomiting but breathing: on their side** (recovery position).
   **Not breathing: CPR** (→ 12-18).
7. **Button battery, lamp oil or fuel, a corrosive, iron, or a lot of any medicine?** Read the
   special cases below now.
8. **Write down the time, the substance and the worst-case amount. Watch for hours.**

## Do this now: prevention

- **Lock it up tonight:** all medicines (every OTC bottle, glucagon, stomach-relief chewables,
  iron and prenatal tablets), lamp oil and fuels, bleach and cleaners, lye and soap-making
  supplies, pool chemicals, button batteries and the devices that hold them.
  {{#if has.refrigerated_meds}}**Refrigerated medicines cannot go "up high"**: they live in the
  fridge, which a climbing child can open. A lockable box inside the fridge, or a fridge latch,
  solves it.{{/if}}
- **Never put a chemical into a food or drink container.** This one rule prevents the classic
  fatal accident. Original containers only, labels intact.
- **Keep the labels.** A product's label carries a first-aid statement required by law. **The
  label is your offline Poison Control.** A jug with no label is a jug you cannot respond to.
- **Post this page's do and do-not list** where the medicines live.
- **Count your button batteries** (remotes, thermometers, key fobs, watches, hearing aids, toys).
  Tape battery compartments shut.

## The do and do-not list (every poisoning)

From MedlinePlus (Poisoning first aid) and the American Academy of Pediatrics (Poison
Prevention).

**Do:**
1. **Get the poison away from the person**: out of their hands, out of the mouth; have a child
   spit out what is left.
2. **Identify it: grab the container and keep it.** Everything after depends on knowing what and
   roughly how much.
3. **Read the label's first-aid statement and follow it.** It is written for this moment, and it
   is on hand and offline.
4. **Call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222) if any phone works**: a landline, a neighbour's, a cell
   with one bar. Rely on this page alone only when no call is possible.
5. **Position them safely:** awake and alert, sitting up and calm. Drowsy, vomiting or
   unconscious but breathing, **on their side (recovery position)** so vomit cannot choke them.
   Not breathing, **CPR (→ 12-18).**
6. **Watch for hours, not minutes**: drowsiness, vomiting, breathing changes, burns around the
   mouth. Write down the time, substance and amount; you will not remember.

**Do not:**
- **Do not make them vomit.** Not salt water, not fingers, not syrup of ipecac (no longer
  recommended by the AAP). Corrosives burn twice on the way back up, and fuels and oils (**lamp
  oil, kerosene, gasoline**) can be breathed into the lungs while vomiting. That is what kills
  in fuel swallowing, not the swallow itself.
- **Do not give activated charcoal on your own.** It is not a home remedy, it is wrong for many
  poisons, and it is dangerous in a drowsy child. (Charcoal from a fire is not activated charcoal
  and does nothing.)
- **Do not try to neutralise**: no vinegar on lye, no baking soda on acid. The reaction adds heat
  to a chemical burn.
- **Nothing by mouth to someone drowsy, seizing or unconscious.**
- **Do not let "they seem fine" end the watch.** Several common poisons (acetaminophen above all)
  feel fine for a day while doing deadly damage. "Fine now" is information, not a verdict.

## By route

| Route | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Swallowed** | Spit out or remove what remains. Do not cause vomiting. Follow the label. A few sips of water to clear the mouth **only if fully awake and able to swallow** |
| **On the skin** | **Dry powder or granules (pool shock, lye, garden lime): brush off every visible grain first** with a gloved hand or dry cloth, **take off the dusted clothing, then flush for 20 minutes**; a little water on a pile of dry chemical concentrates the heat on the skin (MedlinePlus, Chemical burn or reaction). **Liquids: flush at once.** Remove contaminated clothing, protecting your own hands. **Rinse under running water for 15 to 20 minutes**, timed, not a splash. Then treat any burn (→ 12-03) |
| **In the eye** | **Flush with clean lukewarm water for 15 to 20 minutes**, eyelid held open, water running from the nose side outward so it does not wash into the other eye. Do not rub; do not neutralise (→ 12-03) |
| **Breathed in** | **Fresh air at once.** Get them out and get yourself out; do not become the second casualty (the carbon monoxide rule, → 08-02, → 29-03). Loosen clothing; recovery position if drowsy |

## Special cases

- **Button batteries: the true emergency.** A swallowed button battery can burn through the food
  pipe **in as little as 2 hours.** It is a surgical emergency and there is no home treatment.
  The National Capital Poison Center's guideline (Button Battery Ingestion Triage and Treatment
  Guideline) adds one measure while you move toward care: **for a child 12 months or older, 2
  teaspoons of honey every 10 minutes, up to 6 doses.** It slows the burn; it does not stop it.
  **Not under 12 months** (infant botulism), nothing else by mouth, do not cause vomiting. With
  no surgery reachable, prevention is the **entire** defence.
- **Fuels and oils (lamp oil, kerosene, gasoline, torch fuel).** The classic toddler poisoning in
  an off-grid house: lamp oil looks like juice. Never cause vomiting. Watch breathing for 24
  hours; coughing, choking or fast breathing right after the swallow means it went into the
  lungs. Store lamp fuel like the poison it is (→ 10-02, → 06-06, → 29-01).
- **Corrosives (lye from soap-making, drain cleaner, pool chemicals).** Burn on contact. Wipe out
  or spit out what remains, small sips of water only if fully alert, **no vomiting, no
  neutralising.** Skin and eyes: the 15 to 20 minute flush. Pool chemicals mixed with other
  products make dangerous gas: the "breathed in" row (→ 29-05, → 06-06).
- **Medicines.** A stocked medicine cabinet (→ 12-07) is the biggest target in the house.
  **Acetaminophen is the deadliest common one**, precisely because the child looks fine
  afterwards. Note the substance, strength and worst-case count ("the bottle held up to ___"),
  keep the bottle, and treat any uncertain swallow as the real thing.
- **Iron tablets and prenatal vitamins.** **Treat any swallow as serious and move toward care.**
  Adult iron tablets look like sweets, a small number can kill a toddler, and iron was for years
  a leading cause of childhood poisoning deaths in the US (AAP, Poison Prevention). **The trap:
  vomiting and diarrhoea, then an apparent recovery, then collapse a day later** (MedlinePlus,
  Iron overdose). Count the tablets missing; keep the bottle.
- **Bismuth subsalicylate (pink stomach-relief chewables).** They are a **salicylate**, aspirin's
  family: a toddler who eats a handful has an aspirin overdose (fast breathing, vomiting, ringing
  ears, drowsiness). Count the tablets missing, keep the bottle, move toward care if the count is
  uncertain. Store them locked, never in the children's medicine bin (→ 12-07).
- **Clove oil (eugenol)** from a dental kit: seriously toxic to small children if swallowed
  (→ 12-06). Treat as a serious swallow.
- **Plants.** Children eat **nothing** wild without an adult confirming it (→ 05-06, → 20-02). If
  it happens anyway: remove plant material from the mouth, **keep a sample** to identify against
  the plates (→ 05-06), do not cause vomiting, watch closely.
- **Someone else's insulin or diabetes tablets.** If a child gets insulin (a licked vial top is
  nothing; an actual injection is an emergency) or swallows diabetes tablets: **give sugar and
  keep giving carbohydrate**, watch as for a severe low blood sugar (→ 02-01, → 02-05), and treat
  unconsciousness as severe low blood sugar. Move toward care.

## With no Poison Control and no emergency room: honest decision rules

- **The label plus the do and do-not list is the whole home treatment.** Most childhood swallows
  are small and end well with watching. The protocol is mostly about not adding a second injury
  (vomiting, neutralising, charcoal).
- **What still justifies moving toward any care that exists** (→ 01-02): a button battery; any
  corrosive actually swallowed; fuel swallowed with coughing or breathing changes; any medicine
  swallow where the possible amount is large; iron; burns in the mouth or throat; worsening
  drowsiness or breathing; seizures.
- **Decide by the worst case, not the best.** "The bottle might have been nearly empty" is how the
  fatal ones start. Count what is left against what the container held.

| Marker and tape | Label everything; tape battery doors | Never food containers for chemicals |

## Scavenge or substitute

- Any lockable tote, footlocker or high cabinet works. Height plus a latch beats a brand.
- **Never** improvise vomiting agents or "antidotes" from folklore: milk, salt water, oil, or
  charcoal from a fire.

## When it runs out

- **Nothing here is consumed, but locks, labels and habits decay.** Re-walk the house at every
  seasonal check (→ 26-01) asking "what can the now-taller child reach?"
- **Prevention costs nothing** and is the entire defence once care is out of reach.
- **Call Poison Control the moment phones return**, even about something that happened days ago
  and "seems fine."

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** phones may still work: the number first, always. Lock-up discipline starts now.
- **B (moving):** medicines and fuel travel together in vehicles. Keep the medicine bag latched
  and up front, fuel sealed and at the back (→ 06-07, → 22-05). Strange houses and shelters are
  not childproofed.
- **C (resettled):** more fuel, more chemicals, more improvised containers, no care. The "never in
  a food container" rule is the one that saves a life here.
