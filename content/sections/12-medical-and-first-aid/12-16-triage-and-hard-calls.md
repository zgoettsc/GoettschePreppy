# Triage and hard decisions with no hospital

**What this page is for.** Thinking through the hardest medical situations once, calmly, in
advance: checking whether help really is unavailable, sorting what to do first, recognising a
stroke or heart attack fast, knowing what is beyond a household, and what to do when someone
dies.

> **SAFETY**
> - **"There is no hospital" is almost always less true than it feels.** Emergency care degrades
>   unevenly in real disasters. Hospitals run on generators, clinics open in odd places,
>   neighbours turn out to be nurses. **Before deciding care is unavailable, actually check**:
>   try 911, the phone, the radio (→ 15-02), the neighbours (→ 18-01), 988. **The biggest error
>   households make is deciding too early that they are alone.**
> - **Stroke signs: check blood sugar first** in anyone with diabetes. A low sugar looks exactly
>   like a stroke.
> - **No aspirin for stroke symptoms.**
> - This page is a framework for decisions. **It cannot make them for you, and it is not ethical
>   instruction.**

## Do this now: something serious just happened

1. **Make yourself safe.** A second casualty helps nobody.
2. **Life threats first:** severe bleeding (→ 12-02), not breathing (→ 12-18), blocked airway.
3. **Try to reach care**: 911, phone, radio, neighbours. Keep trying as conditions change.
4. **Then the things that kill in hours**: worsening infection, dehydration, a condition
   emergency (→ 12-11).
5. **Write it down as you go** (→ 25-05): times, what you saw, what you gave.
6. **Take thirty seconds, say the plan out loud to another adult, then commit.**

## Do this now: preparation

- **Have the conversation.** All the adults, calmly, before anything happens. What do we do if
  one of us is seriously injured? If a child is? If a life-critical medicine runs out? Who
  decides? What are our limits? **Hard, and far better had now.**
- **Write down decision thresholds** (→ 01-02, → 12-11): at what supply clock do we move? At what
  point do we seek outside help even at some risk? Your household's choice on when to leave:
  {{choice DC-19}} (→ 30-20)
- **Map the medical resources that might survive** (→ 25-03, → 25-04): hospitals, clinics, fire
  stations, pharmacies, veterinarians, and neighbours with training. Know this before you need
  it.
- **Fill the training gap.** The honest answer to most of this page is "get trained": Stop the
  Bleed, first aid and CPR, and if possible a wilderness first responder course (→ 24-01).
  **Every hour of training moves the line of what is beyond you.**

## Step zero: check that you really are alone

- **Try to reach care.** Phone, radio (→ 15-02), 911, 988, driving to a hospital.
- **Ask around** (→ 18-01). Nurses, paramedics, veterinarians, dentists and doctors live
  everywhere, and in a crisis most will help. **Knowing who they are before the crisis is worth
  an enormous amount.** Have that conversation this year.
- **Reassess as things change.** Care unreachable during a blizzard may be reachable in three
  days.
- **The risk changes when someone is dying.** A trip too dangerous for supplies may be worth it
  for a life.

## Triage: what it actually means

Triage is sorting when you cannot help everyone at once. In a household it is rarely about
choosing between people; it is usually about **ordering your own attention** under stress:
1. **Immediate threats to life**: severe bleeding, not breathing, blocked airway.
2. **Things that kill in hours**: worsening infection, dehydration, a condition emergency such as
   a diabetic crisis (→ 12-11).
3. **Things that cause lasting harm**: broken bones, deep wounds, cold injuries.
4. **Comfort and function.**

**And explicitly:**
- **The rescuer's safety first.**
- **The person who can be saved with what you have gets your attention first.**
- **Protecting the adults who are still functioning is not selfishness. It is what keeps
  everyone alive.** An exhausted, injured carer is a second patient.

## Knowing what is beyond you

Think about this list in advance so you are not discovering it in the moment. **With no surgery,
no X-ray or scans, and few or no antibiotics, these are generally beyond household care:**
- Major bleeding inside the body; deep wounds to the chest or belly
- An airway blockage that basic first aid does not clear
- A major head injury with worsening consciousness (→ 12-19)
- Broken thigh bone, pelvis or spine
- Large burns (→ 12-03)
- Labour that cannot progress, and heavy bleeding after birth (→ 12-12)
- Sepsis that has spread through the body (→ 12-04)
- Heart attack and stroke: **but recognising them fast still changes the outcome** (below)
- A life-critical medicine that has run out (→ 12-11), which is exactly why the supply clock
  drives → 01-02

**Recognising this is not giving up.** It means: (1) **try harder to reach real care**, the
intervention that matters; (2) **give comfort, warmth, pain relief, fluids and presence**, which
are real and valuable; (3) **do not destroy your remaining capability** on something with no
realistic chance while others depend on you.

## Stroke and heart attack: recognise fast, decide fast

"Beyond household care" does not mean "nothing to do." Recognising it within minutes turns a
mystery collapse into a decision.

**Stroke: the F.A.S.T. check** (American Heart Association / American Stroke Association, Stroke
Symptoms). **Any one sign means act.**
- **Before anything else, check blood sugar** if the person has diabetes and you have a meter or
  sensor. **Low sugar looks exactly like a stroke**: weakness on one side, a drooping face,
  slurred speech, confusion. Treat the low (→ 02-01, → 02-05) and check F.A.S.T. again at
  the 15-minute recheck CDC uses for a low (CDC, Treatment of Low Blood Sugar). **Signs that clear with sugar were the low, not a stroke.** Stroke guidelines require
  a sugar check before calling a stroke (AHA/ASA, Guidelines for the Early Management of Acute
  Ischemic Stroke, 2019). Driving someone for an hour instead of treating a low is the fatal error
  this line prevents.
- **F, Face drooping:** ask for a smile; one side sags or is numb.
- **A, Arm weakness:** ask them to raise both arms; one drifts down.
- **S, Speech difficulty:** slurred, wrong words, cannot repeat a simple sentence.
- **T, Time:** note the time symptoms started and go. Stroke treatment depends on time, and "when
  did it start?" is the first thing any working facility will ask.
- Also watch for: sudden severe headache, sudden loss of sight, sudden confusion, sudden trouble
  walking. **Sudden and one-sided is the pattern.**

**Heart attack: the pattern** (American Heart Association, Warning Signs of a Heart Attack):
chest pressure, squeezing or fullness lasting more than a few minutes, or going and coming back;
pain spreading to the jaw, neck, back or one or both arms; shortness of breath; cold sweat;
nausea; light-headedness.
- **Women more often have** shortness of breath, nausea or vomiting, and back or jaw pain
  **without** crushing chest pain.
- **People with long-standing diabetes can have a heart attack with little or no chest pain.**
  Unexplained crushing tiredness, breathlessness, nausea, or jaw or arm discomfort is a
  heart-attack question until answered, not indigestion.

**Aspirin.** The American Heart Association says that during a suspected heart attack a 911
dispatcher may tell you to chew aspirin, and that aspirin is not right for everyone (American
Heart Association, Aspirin and Heart Disease). **Not with an aspirin allergy or a bleeding
problem. Never for stroke symptoms**: bleeding in the brain cannot be ruled out without a scan.

> **Before the collapse:** ask each adult's doctor whether chewing aspirin in a suspected heart
> attack is right for them, and how much. Write the answer on → 25-09 with the doctor's name and
> the date. This binder does not set that amount.

**With no 911:** this is the decision to go for care (→ 01-02), made at recognition speed: the
nearest working facility from your map (→ 25-03, → 25-04). On a journey, head for the nearest
facility, never "press on to the destination" (→ 01-07). CPR (→ 12-18) if they collapse; the
honest odds written there apply.

## Decisions worth making in advance

Write your answers down. **Deciding in advance greatly reduces the paralysis.**

**1. When do we leave to seek care?** (→ 01-02) A life-critical medicine's supply clock is the
clearest trigger: **set the number early enough that you can still act on it.** Also a serious
injury beyond household care, a pregnancy nearing its due date (→ 12-12), an illness getting
worse.

**2. Who decides?** (→ 00-07) When one adult is injured or panicking, another decides. If every
adult is compromised, what then? Is there someone outside the household who would step in?

**3. How much risk do we accept to get help?** Travelling in a storm, approaching strangers,
leaving the house unattended, spending fuel you cannot replace. These trade one risk for another
and there is no formula, but deciding in advance beats improvising at 3 a.m.

**4. What do we do for someone we cannot save?** Comfort, pain relief, warmth, cleanliness, and
**presence: not being alone.** These are real acts of care. **The dying deserve company.** Say it
out loud in advance, because under stress the instinct is to keep frantically doing things.

**5. What do we tell the children?** (→ 20-08, → 21-03) Honesty suited to their age. Children
usually know something is wrong, and being shut out is frightening in its own way.

## Deciding under stress: the practical part

- **Stop for thirty seconds.** Almost nothing is so urgent that thirty seconds of thought makes it
  worse, and panicked action often does.
- **Say it out loud to another adult.** Saying a plan exposes bad reasoning fast.
- **Ask: what is the actual threat to life right now, and what do I have that addresses it?**
- **Ask: what will I regret?** Both acting and not acting.
- **Write things down as you go** (→ 25-05): times, temperatures, what you gave, what you saw.
  Memory fails under stress, and the record matters if you reach care.
- **Then commit and reassess.** Constant second-guessing is worse than a decent decision followed
  through and revisited.

## If someone dies: the practical part

The human side (grief, ritual, what to tell the children) is on → 21-03 and matters more than
anything below. The practical side is short, and knowing it removes one fear (PAHO/WHO with ICRC
and IFRC, Management of Dead Bodies after Disasters):
- **A body after a natural death is a low infection risk.** The idea that corpses cause epidemics
  is a myth the field manual exists to correct. Ordinary care (gloves, handwashing, → 13-02) is
  enough. Fear should not rush or degrade what you do next.
- **Dignity and records.** Wash and wrap the body. Write down **name, date and time of death, and
  a witness's name**; keep one copy with the household documents (→ 19-01) and one with the
  burial site. If authorities ever work again, this record matters.
- **Bury away from water.** Use the same distances from water that the latrine rules set
  (→ 13-07): downhill of nothing you drink, well away from wells and surface water, and deep
  enough to stay undisturbed.
- **In winter, frozen ground may make burial impossible for months.**{{#if is.cold_winters}} That
  is normal in your climate's history.{{/if}} The interim answer is **protected cold storage**:
  wrapped, in a cold outbuilding or shaded protected place, secured against animals, until the
  ground opens. Naming this now makes it a plan instead of a horror.

## Afterwards

- **Grief, guilt and second-guessing are normal**, whatever the outcome (→ 21-03).
- **"Would a hospital have saved them?" has no answer**, and it can consume someone. Name that in
  advance.
- **Talk about it**: with each other, with anyone available, with 988 if it works. Isolation makes
  it worse (→ 12-15).
- **The household still has to function.** Giving everyone a role and a routine is part of how
  people get through (→ 21-04).

## What this page is really arguing for

Almost everything here gets easier if you do three things **now**:
1. **Get trained.** Every course moves the line of what you can handle.
2. **Know your neighbours, and know who has medical skills** (→ 18-01, → 18-03). **The most
   valuable medical resource in a crisis is a person, not a supply.**
3. **Do not let a life-critical medicine's supply clock run short before you act** (→ 01-02,
   → 12-11). The hardest decisions on this page mostly follow from that one clock, and that one
   you can see coming.

## Scavenge or substitute

- **People.** The most valuable find is someone with real medical training (→ 18-01, → 18-03). Ask
  around before the crisis.
- **Any working care, however degraded**: a fire station, a veterinarian, a retired nurse, a
  clinic on a generator.
- **Comfort, warmth, cleanliness, presence**: always available, always worth something.
- **What cannot be substituted:** surgery, scans, blood and intensive care. That is the whole
  argument for reaching real care and for not letting the supply clock run out.

## When it runs out

- **What runs out here is capability, and it shrinks over time**: supplies fall, people tire,
  small injuries add up. **Expect your medical capability to be lower in month six than in month
  one**, and let that shape how much risk you take in everything else (→ 16-06, → 11-06, → 12-03).
- **Training and community make up for it**, and both **grow** over time if you invest in them.
- **The hardest clocks are the life-critical medicines** (→ 12-11). They turn medical decisions
  into decisions about staying or leaving. Watch them weekly and act early.
- **The last substitution is real:** when you cannot fix something, **you can still care for the
  person.** Warmth, water, cleanliness, pain relief, honesty and not being alone. That is genuine
  medicine, it never runs out, and it is worth doing well.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** 911 first, always. This page is for what happens while help is coming, or if it
  does not come. Use the calm to get the training and the doctor's answers done.
- **B (moving):** **any serious medical event on the road means heading for the nearest working
  facility; never press on to the destination** (→ 01-07). Decide that rule now so nobody debates
  it at highway speed.
- **C (resettled):** on arrival, the first medical task is finding the nearest working facility
  and writing it on → 25-03.
