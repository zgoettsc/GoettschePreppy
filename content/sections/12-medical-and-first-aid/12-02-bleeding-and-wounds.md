# Bleeding control and wound care

**What this page is for.** Stopping serious bleeding in the first minutes, then cleaning and
caring for a wound over the following weeks so it does not become infected. It is a reminder
card for people who have taken a hands-on course, not the course itself.

> **SAFETY**
> - **Reading this page does not make you able to do this.** Bleeding control is a physical
>   skill. **Take a Stop the Bleed course now**, while classes exist. A class takes about an
>   hour (Stop the Bleed, US Department of Defense).
> - **Never loosen a tourniquet once placed while help can still come; mark the time. If no help
>   will ever come, the conversion decision is DC-25 (→ 30-26).** Re-bleeding and clot release
>   kill. While help can come, this is one of the binder's floors (→ 30-01): it is not offered
>   as a choice.
> - **Severe bleeding kills in minutes.** That is why the trauma kit lives where the work
>   happens (→ 12-01, → 12-08).
> - **Infection is the bigger long-term killer** when there is no clinic (→ 12-04). A wound
>   you survive in the first hour can still kill in the second week. Cleaning matters as much
>   as stopping the bleeding.

## Do this now: serious bleeding

1. **Make the scene safe.** Stop the chainsaw, step off the ice. A second casualty helps
   nobody.
2. **Find the bleeding.** Cut or pull clothing away. Blood soaks into dark cloth unseen.
3. **Press hard** on the wound with gauze or cloth and a gloved hand. **Do not lift to check.**
4. **Arm or leg still bleeding through pressure? Tourniquet:** 2 to 3 inches above the
   wound, between the wound and the body, never over a joint. Tighten until the bleeding
   stops **and** there is no pulse below it. Still bleeding? A second tourniquet above the
   first. **Write the time on it. Never loosen it while help can still come.**
5. **Groin, armpit or neck?** Pack the wound with gauze and press hard.
   **Chest or belly?** Do **not** pack. Cover and press.
6. **Keep them warm and lying flat.** Watch breathing. Then read → 12-16.

## Do this now: preparation

- **Take the Stop the Bleed course.** Every adult who might respond. Then buy genuine
  tourniquets and a practice one, and drill putting one on yourself, one-handed, in the dark,
  with gloves on.
- **Put a trauma kit where wood is cut.** Axe and saw injuries to the leg and foot are among
  the most likely serious bleeding events in a long emergency (→ 16-06). Until you have
  counted tourniquets, hemostatic gauze and pressure bandages on → 22-04, assume you do not
  have them (→ 12-01, "buy first").
- **Check tetanus vaccination dates for everyone** while clinics are open (→ 12-04, → 25-09).
- **Stock gauze in bulk.** One serious wound managed for two weeks uses far more dressing
  than any commercial kit contains.

## The order of operations for serious bleeding

This is the framework taught by Stop the Bleed (American College of Surgeons; Stop the Bleed,
US Department of Defense): direct pressure, wound packing, tourniquet. Learn it in a class; use
this page as the reminder.

1. **Your own safety first.** Falling wood, a running saw, a fire, thin ice.
2. **Find the bleeding.** Look and feel. Use trauma shears to cut clothing away.
3. **Firm, direct pressure** with a gloved hand and gauze or cloth, **hard**, and **do not
   lift to check.** Most bleeding stops with steady direct pressure. It is the single most
   effective action and it needs no equipment.
4. **On an arm or leg, if pressure is not controlling it, use a tourniquet** (Stop the Bleed;
   USUHS, Stop the Bleed just-in-time instructions):
   - **2 to 3 inches above the wound, between the wound and the body, never over a joint.**
     The bone of the knee or elbow stops the strap from squeezing the artery. If the wound is
     at or just below a joint, go above the joint.
   - **Tighten until the bleeding stops**, then lock the windlass (the twisting handle) in its
     clip. Military guidance tightens until both the bleeding and the pulse below it are gone
     (TCCC Guidelines, 2026).
   - **If it still bleeds, put a second tourniquet above the first.**
   - **Write the time on it**: on the strap, the skin, the forehead, anywhere it will be seen.
     Stop the Bleed kits carry a marker for this.
   - It will hurt a great deal. Warn the person; that is expected (Stop the Bleed, US
     Department of Defense).
   - **Small children:** on a small child's thin limb a windlass tourniquet may not close down
     enough. Use direct pressure and packing instead.{{#if has.young_child}} You have a young
     child: practise direct pressure and packing on a pillow, not only the tourniquet.{{/if}}
5. **Junctional wounds** (groin, armpit, neck, where a tourniquet cannot go): **pack the wound**
   with hemostatic or plain gauze, pushing it in firmly until the wound is filled, and hold
   hard pressure. Packing is exactly what the course teaches. **Never pack a wound in the
   chest or abdomen**: packing there pushes into the organs. Cover it and hold firm pressure. That is an injury to move toward whatever care exists (→ 12-16).
6. **Keep them warm** (→ 08-05), **lying flat**, and watch them. Blood loss plus cold is a
   dangerous combination{{#if is.cold_winters}}, and in your winters "keep them warm" is not
   an afterthought{{/if}}.

### Once a tourniquet is on

**Never loosen a tourniquet once placed while help can still come; mark the time. If no help
will ever come, the conversion decision is DC-25 (→ 30-26).** Loosening lets the wound bleed
again and can release clots into the blood, and both kill. While help can come, this is a floor
(→ 30-01).

Be honest about what this means with no hospital. A tourniquet left on for many hours costs
the limb below it. Keep the person warm, keep them lying down, give fluids by mouth if they are
awake and can swallow, and move toward any care that exists (→ 12-16).

**When no help will ever come.** Military guidance for long, remote care converts a tourniquet
to wound packing and a pressure bandage only when all its criteria are met: not in shock, the
wound can be watched closely, not an amputation, and every effort to convert in less than 2
hours. **People trained below medic level "should not attempt tourniquet conversion beyond 2
hours"** without a medic's direction, and otherwise keep the tourniquet in place; the 6-hour
limit applies only where close monitoring and laboratory tests exist (TCCC Guidelines, 2026). For
a household, that means within 2 hours or not at all, whoever does it, medic training or not
(→ 30-26). Keeping it on risks the limb; converting risks the
bleeding starting again. Your household's choice: {{choice DC-25}} → 30-26.

**Myths that persist anyway:** a tourniquet does **not** automatically cost the limb when care
arrives within a few hours; pressing "pressure points" and raising the limb are **not**
substitutes for direct pressure; and **improvised tourniquets often fail** because they are too
narrow or lack a rigid windlass. Buy real ones.

## Cleaning a wound: the part that decides the outcome

With no clinic and possibly no antibiotics (→ 12-04), **cleaning the wound is your infection
control.**

- **Wash your hands first** (CDC, About Hygiene) and put on gloves. Every time.
- **Irrigate (flush) with lots of clean drinkable water.** This is the core of it. **Volume and
  pressure matter more than sterility.** A large syringe, a squeeze bottle, or a plastic bag
  with a pinhole gives the pressure. Use far more water than feels necessary.
- **Remove dirt and debris**: grit, splinters, gravel, bark, cloth. Anything left in the wound
  becomes an abscess (a pocket of pus) later. Use fine tweezers and good light (→ 10-01).
- **Antiseptics** (iodine, chlorhexidine) are for the **skin around** the wound. Strong
  antiseptics poured into a wound damage healthy tissue as well as germs. Clean water is the
  workhorse.
- **Dress it:** a non-stick layer against the wound, absorbent gauze over it, secured.
- **Change the dressing regularly and look at the wound every time.** This is how you catch
  infection early (→ 12-04).

## When not to close a wound

**Read → 12-14 before closing anything.** In brief: **closing a dirty wound traps the infection
inside.** Wounds that are dirty, deep, punctures, bites, more than several hours old, or from an
axe or chainsaw in a dirty place should generally **be left open**, packed lightly, and allowed
to heal from the bottom up. It is slower and uglier, and far safer without a surgeon and
antibiotics.

## Eye injuries: shield, flush, or dark rest

Wood chips, wire ends, sunlight off snow. The three responses are very different, and mixing
them up is what costs sight.

- **Something stuck in or through the eye: shield it. Never remove it, never press**
  (MedlinePlus, Eye emergencies). Use rigid protection that rests on the bone around the eye,
  not on the eye: **a clean paper cup, or half a foam cup, taped over the eye socket.** Cover
  the other eye too if the person can bear it, because the eyes move together and movement
  grinds the object. No rubbing, no rinsing around it. **Nothing to eat or drink** (it may need
  surgery). Move toward whatever care exists (→ 12-16). The shield's job is to change nothing
  on the way.
- **Loose grit, eyelash or sawdust:** blinking and tears clear most of it. Help with a **gentle
  flush of clean water** (MedlinePlus). Never fingers or tweezers on the eyeball, and **never
  rub.**
- **Scratched eye (corneal abrasion):** the grit is out but it still feels like glass:
  scratchy pain, tears, light hurts. Flush once, then **stop touching it.** MedlinePlus says
  the scratchy feeling after a tiny object is removed should go away within a day or two. If pain gets worse, redness
  spreads, there is discharge, or sight gets worse instead of better, treat it as an infection
  (→ 12-04). Tight patching is no longer routine; do not press or rub an injured eye. Dark
  glasses for comfort are fine.
- **Snow blindness:** hours on snow or water glare, then that evening both eyes burn, water,
  and cannot stand light (American Academy of Ophthalmology, What Is Photokeratitis). It is a
  sunburn of the clear front of the eye. **It usually goes away by itself, gradually, in a day
  or two** (American Academy of Ophthalmology). Treatment is dark rest: contact lenses out, a dim room or covered eyes, a cool cloth
  over closed lids, comfort measures from → 12-13. **Prevention is the real medicine:
  UV-blocking sunglasses or goggles on every snow and ice day (→ 11-08).** The field
  improvisation works: cardboard, bark or cloth with two narrow horizontal slits, tied on.
  Both eyes at once plus a day of glare means snow blindness; one eye, or no glare, means look
  for the other causes above.
- **Chemical splash in the eye** is covered on → 12-03 and → 12-17: flush with clean water for
  **at least 15 minutes** (MedlinePlus), and **at least 30 minutes for lye or another strong
  alkali** (CDC/ATSDR, Sodium Hydroxide), from the nose side outward, lids held open, starting
  now. It is more urgent than anything else in this list.

## Watching for infection: the second-week killer

Check at every dressing change and know the signs (→ 12-04; CDC, About Sepsis):
- Redness, swelling, warmth and pain that **increase after day two or three.** Normal healing
  gets less uncomfortable, not more.
- **Red streaks** spreading from the wound.
- **Pus or a foul smell.**
- **Fever, chills, feeling unwell all over.**
- **Confusion, fast breathing, fast heartbeat, extreme discomfort**: signs of **sepsis**, the
  body's overwhelming reaction to infection, a life-threatening emergency.

**Tetanus** (lockjaw, a germ from soil that causes muscle spasms) is a real risk with punctures
and dirty wounds. Early signs are jaw stiffness and muscle spasms. **There is no home treatment
once it starts.** The defence is the vaccine. After any wound, ask whether a booster is due now.
The CDC wound rule (CDC, Tetanus clinical guidance; full table on → 12-04): **for someone who
completed the childhood series, a clean minor wound needs a booster if the last dose was 10 or
more years ago; a dirty or major wound (puncture, soil, bite, burn, crush, frostbite) needs one
if it was 5 or more years ago. Never vaccinated, an incomplete series or an unknown history:
vaccinate for any wound, and a dirty or major wound also needs tetanus immune globulin**, which
only a clinic has. Everyone's dates belong on → 25-09.

## Honest limits

With no clinic, some injuries are beyond what a household can manage: bleeding inside the body
you cannot reach, deep chest or belly wounds, broken bones that cut off blood to a limb, severe
head injuries, and infections that outrun what you have. **→ 12-16 covers those decisions.**
Knowing your limits in advance is part of the plan. Prevention (→ 16-02 sharp tools, → 16-06
safe technique, → 11-06 traction) does more than any treatment page.

## Scavenge or substitute

- **Dressings:** clean cotton cloth, boiled and dried; **sanitary pads and diapers** (sealed,
  very absorbent, excellent for this); clean torn sheets.
- **Flushing:** any squeeze bottle, a plastic bag with a pinhole, a turkey baster, and clean
  drinkable water (→ 03-04).
- **Honey** on clean wounds and burns has traditional and evidence-supported use: it fights
  germs and keeps the wound moist. **Not for deep punctures, and never for a baby under 12
  months** (risk of infant botulism, a rare paralysing illness).
- **Sugar packing** of infected wounds is another traditional method with real history.
- **Tape:** duct tape over gauze, not directly on skin; cloth strips tied.
- **Sterilising instruments:** boiling for a sustained time; flame for metal.
- **What you cannot improvise well:** a tourniquet, hemostatic gauze, and the training. Get all
  three now.

## When it runs out

- **Dressings go fastest.** One significant wound dressed twice a day for two weeks is dozens
  of dressings. Count in dressing changes, not packets (→ 23-02).
- **Ration:** wash and reuse cloth dressings, boiled, dried, stored clean. Keep sterile
  commercial dressings for fresh, deep or dirty wounds and for the first days.
- **Resupply:** medicine cabinets and workplace kits (→ 22-02); trade (→ 18-02); cloth, which is
  plentiful (→ 11-05).
- **Substitute:** clean cloth for gauze; boiled water for saline; soap, water and flushing for
  antiseptic; honey or sugar for antibiotic ointment on suitable wounds.
- **What cannot be substituted is cleanliness** (→ 13-02). Clean hands, clean water and clean
  cloth at every dressing change prevent far more harm than any supply here treats. When
  supplies run low, be more careful, not less.
- **The real answer is prevention.** Sharp tools that cut instead of glancing off (→ 16-02), a
  proper stance, chaps, gloves, not swinging an axe when exhausted or cold, traction on ice
  (→ 11-06). The wound you do not get costs nothing to treat.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** trauma kit at the work area and in the kitchen. Wood splitting and cooking
  are where the injuries are. Use clean water for flushing generously while you have it.
- **B (moving):** a tourniquet and a pressure bandage for each adult, reachable with one hand.
  Water for flushing is scarce on the move, so wound cleaning gets worse exactly when dirt goes
  up. Clean thoroughly at the next stop with real water.
- **C (resettled):** more axe work, more animals, more building, further from help. More
  capability, not less. A trauma kit at every work site, and protective gear treated as
  required.
