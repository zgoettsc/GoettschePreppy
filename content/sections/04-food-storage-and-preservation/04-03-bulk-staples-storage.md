# Long-term bulk staples: grains, beans, sugar and salt

**What this page is for.** How to pack dry staples (rice, wheat, beans, oats, sugar, salt) so
they keep for years or decades, which foods do not keep, and how to cook stored beans safely.

> **CAUTION.** Two real hazards. **Moisture:** sealing damp food in an oxygen-free bucket grows
> mould and, in the wrong conditions, botulism (a deadly toxin, → 29-08). **Only dry,
> low-moisture foods go into oxygen-free storage.** **Rodents and insects:** a chewed bucket is
> a lost bucket, and they will find it (→ 04-11).

## Do this now

1. **Store only truly dry staples** in sealed, oxygen-free containers: white rice, wheat,
   dried beans, oats, pasta, sugar, salt, powdered milk.
2. **Never** seal moist food, fresh produce, brown rice (its oil goes rancid) or anything oily
   in oxygen-free storage.
3. **Use food-grade buckets, Mylar liner bags and oxygen absorbers** for anything you want to
   keep for a decade (method below).
4. **Label the outside** with contents, weight and date, plus **the ingredient label** if
   anyone in the house has a food allergy (→ 02-04).
5. **Lift it off concrete** and keep it cool, dark and dry.
6. **Dried kidney-type beans must boil hard before any slow cooking** (rule below).

## What stores for decades, and what does not

How long dry food keeps depends most on heat. Utah State University Extension reports Brigham
Young University's finding that stored wheat kept acceptable quality for **25 years in a cold
basement but only 5 years in a hot garage or attic** (Utah State University Extension, Food
Storage Conditions), and that shelf life roughly **halves for every 10.8 °F rise in storage
temperature** (Utah State University Extension, Food Storage: Will I Use It?). The table below
ranks foods; the years depend on your storage.

| Food | Life when sealed, dry and cool | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Wheat berries** (whole wheat grains) | Decades (25 years cold, 5 hot, above) | Needs a grain grinder (→ 16-01) |
| **White rice** | Decades | The benchmark storage staple |
| **Dried beans, lentils** | Decades (harder to cook with age) | Soak longer as they age |
| **Rolled oats, pasta** | Decades | Check pasta for egg in the ingredients |
| **Sugar, salt** | Keep as long as they stay dry | No oxygen absorbers with sugar: it sets hard |
| **Powdered milk** | Years (nonfat keeps best) | → 04-14 |
| **Brown rice** | **Months** | Its oil goes rancid: **not** a long-term staple |
| **Whole-grain flour** | Months | Same problem; store the **grain**, not the flour |
| **Cooking oil** | Rotate it | Cannot be stored long (→ 04-12) |
| **Nuts and seeds** | Months | Oil content; also common allergens |

**The pattern: oil is what spoils.** Whole grains keep far longer than flour, white rice far
longer than brown. **Store the raw material and process it as you need it**, which is why a
hand grain grinder is an important purchase.

## The container system

1. **Food-grade bucket** (HDPE plastic marked food-safe), 5 or 6 gallons.
2. **Mylar liner bag.** This is the real oxygen and moisture barrier; plastic alone lets air
   through slowly.
3. **Oxygen absorbers,** sized to the container: use the size the absorber maker gives for
   that volume (a 5-gallon bucket needs far more than a #10 can). They remove oxygen, which
   kills insects and slows rancidity. **They start working the moment their pack is opened,**
   so open it last, work fast, and **reseal the unused absorbers in an airtight jar at once.**
   A Mylar bag that has pulled tight by the next day is working. **Absorbers are not
   desiccants** (they do not dry food): **the food must already be dry, about 10 percent
   moisture or less.** Moist food sealed without oxygen can grow botulism (Utah State
   University Extension, Food Storage: Will I Use It?).
4. **Seal the Mylar** with a clothes iron or a hair straightener, then lid the bucket.
5. **Label the outside:** contents, weight, date packed, and the ingredient label.

**No oxygen absorbers with:** sugar or brown sugar (they set into a brick), salt (not needed),
or anything with real moisture or oil in it.

**A working bucket** with a screw-top ("gamma") lid for the staple you are eating now, plus
sealed buckets for the deep stock, is the practical arrangement.

## Where to store it

- **Cool, dark, dry, steady.** Heat shortens storage life fast (→ 04-19 has the Utah State
  figures).
- **Off the concrete:** pallets, boards or shelving. Concrete wicks moisture and swings in
  temperature.
- {{#if has.basement}}**Your basement is usually the best place.**{{else}}**An interior closet
  or the coolest room is usually the best place.**{{/if}} Attics and garages are not: too hot
  and too changeable.
- **Spread it out.** Do not keep everything in one place (fire, flood, theft).

## Allergen discipline for bulk food

Repackaging throws away the label. If anyone in the house has a food allergy (→ 02-04):

- **Cut out the ingredient panel and tape it inside the lid,** or file it in a sheet.
- **Label the bucket boldly** as safe or not safe for that person.
- **Never share scoops** between an allergen-containing bucket and a safe one.
- **Single-ingredient staples** (plain rice, plain beans, plain oats) are the safest to
  bulk-store because there is nothing hidden to check.

## Using bulk staples

- **A grain grinder** turns wheat into flour; a hand-cranked one works with no power (→ 16-01).
- **Beans need long cooking.** Pair them with an insulated "haybox" cooker (→ 07-02) to cut
  fuel.
- **The kidney-bean rule.** For kidney beans and other dry beans of the same family (red,
  white, pinto, black): **soak at least 5 hours, THROW AWAY the soak water, then boil in fresh
  water for at least 30 minutes BEFORE any haybox or slow finish** (FDA, Bad Bug Book). Raw or
  undercooked kidney beans carry a natural toxin (a lectin called phytohaemagglutinin): **as
  few as 4 or 5 raw beans can cause severe vomiting and diarrhoea,** starting within 1 to 3
  hours. Slow cookers often reach only about 75 °C (167 °F) inside, which does not destroy it
  (FDA). Never cook dry beans from raw in a haybox or any slow, low-heat method. Canned beans
  are already safe.
- **Sprouting** turns stored grains and beans into fresh greens in winter (→ 04-16).
- **Old beans get harder.** Soak longer, cook longer, or use a pressure cooker.

## Gear and supplies

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Food-grade buckets (many) | The container | 5 to 6 gallon; often free from bakeries |
| **Mylar liner bags** | The real oxygen and moisture barrier | Sized to the bucket |
| **Oxygen absorbers** | Stops insects, slows rancidity | Sized to the container by the maker's chart; reseal spares in an airtight jar at once |
| Screw-top ("gamma") lids | Working buckets you open often | A few |
| Impulse sealer or a clothes iron | Sealing Mylar | An iron works |
| **Hand grain grinder** | Wheat is useless without it | Test it now |
| Bulk white rice, wheat, beans, oats, sugar, salt | The calorie base | → 04-01 |
| Pallets or shelving | Off the concrete | Often free |
| Labels, marker, saved ingredient panels | Rotation and allergen safety | → 02-04 |
| Rodent-proof outer protection | They chew buckets | → 04-11 |

**If you don't have it:**

- **Bakery and restaurant buckets** are often free and food-grade. Ask what was in them; avoid
  anything oily or strong-smelling.
- Glass jars with tight lids are excellent for smaller amounts.
- Metal cans and bins stop rodents better than plastic (→ 04-11).
- Original packaging inside a sealed tote is a reasonable first step.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** pounds on hand ÷ daily use (→ 04-01). Bulk staples are the backbone of the
  household runway.
- **Ration:** stretch grains with sprouting (→ 04-16) and beans; add fat for calories (→ 04-12).
- **Resupply:** growing grain at household scale is hard on a small lot, so **bulk staples are
  a finite, buy-it-now resource.** The move is to garden vegetables (→ 05-01), game (→ 05-07)
  and trade (→ 18-02).
- **The honest limit:** you cannot store your way to years. You can store your way to a year,
  and use that year to start producing.

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** buy and pack now. Cool storage, spread out, labelled.
- **B (moving):** buckets are heavy; take what fits, and know what you are leaving (→ 01-05).
- **C (rural or at a destination):** better storage space; same rules. Rodent pressure is
  usually higher (→ 04-11).
