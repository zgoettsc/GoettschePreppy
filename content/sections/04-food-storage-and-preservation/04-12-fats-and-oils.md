# Fats and oils: storage and rendering

**What this page is for.** Why fat matters more than most food plans allow, how long each kind
keeps, and how to render animal fat into cooking fat that stores for months.

> **CAUTION.** Fat is the **most calorie-dense food you can store** (about 4,000 calories per
> pound, more than double any grain) and the **first to go rancid.** It is also a common hiding
> place for allergens: in the US, "vegetable oil" usually means **soybean oil,** and soy is one
> of the nine major allergens (FDA, Food Allergies). Read every label.

## Do this now

1. **Stock fat on purpose.** It is where calories hide in a small space, and stored-food diets
   most often fall short on fat.
2. **Read every oil label** if anyone in the house has a food allergy (→ 02-04). "Vegetable oil"
   is the trap. Single-source oils name what they are: olive, canola, corn, sunflower, coconut.
3. **Ask the allergist about highly refined soybean oil.** Refined oils are treated differently
   under US labelling law, and the answer opens or closes a large amount of shelf-stable food.
   {{#if has.anaphylaxis}}Someone in your household has an allergy marked for anaphylaxis: get
   this answer in writing for the allergy plan.{{/if}}
4. **Store cool, dark and sealed.** Heat, light and air make fat rancid.
5. **Learn to render** (below). It turns animal fat you would otherwise waste into months of
   cooking fat and soap (→ 13-09).

## Why fat matters more than people plan for

- **Calorie density:** about 4,000 calories per pound against about 1,600 for grain. A gallon of
  oil is roughly **30,000 calories** in a container you can carry (worked from USDA FoodData
  Central: oil 884 calories per 100 g).
- **Eating and cooking:** rice and beans with no fat is a bleak, hard-to-eat diet. Fat makes
  stored food into meals people finish, which matters a great deal with children.
- **Nutrition:** fat carries the fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and is a real need.
- **Other uses:** soap (→ 13-09), lamps (→ 10-02), lubrication, waterproofing.

## Shelf life: the limit

Liquid cooking oil: **about 1 to 2 years unopened, depending on the oil; a few months to a year
once opened.** A fridge or cold room extends it (NDSU Extension, Is It Time for an Oil Change in
Your Kitchen?, 2017). For the other fats, use the maker's date and your own nose, and rotate.

| Fat | How it keeps (cool, dark, sealed) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Coconut oil** | Among the more stable fats; use the maker's date | Solid at room temperature |
| **Olive oil** | About 1 to 2 years unopened | Check for blends |
| **Canola, corn, sunflower** | About 1 to 2 years unopened | Single-source; **not** "vegetable oil" |
| **Shortening (canned)** | Stable while sealed; use the maker's date | Check for soy |
| **Lard, tallow (rendered, sealed)** | Keeps longer cold than warm | Well rendered keeps best |
| **Butter: COMMERCIALLY canned, or ghee** | Use the maker's date | Ghee (butter with the milk solids removed) keeps far better than butter |
| **Nuts and seeds** | Months | High oil; also common allergens |

**Never home-can butter, milk, cheese or any dairy.** There is no tested process for it (NCHFP);
the fat shields botulism spores from the heat. **Floor:** **Low-acid foods are pressure-canned to
the published time and pressure, or not canned.** Botulism has no field treatment and the food
looks and smells normal (→ 30-01). Make ghee and keep it cool, or freeze butter.

**Rancid fat smells like crayons or old paint.** It is not immediately dangerous, but it tastes
bad and has lost nutrition, and children will simply refuse it.

**The rotation rule:** fat is the **one bulk food you cannot store for decades.** Buy amounts you
will actually use up in a year or two, and keep buying (→ 04-10).

## Rendering: the renewable source

Rendering is melting raw animal fat slowly and straining it, which turns it into cooking fat that
keeps. It makes fat **renewable** rather than purely stored, if you hunt (→ 05-07) or keep
animals (Section 05).

**Method:**

1. **Cut the fat small** (or grind it). Trim off meat and blood; they cause spoilage.
2. **Heat low and slow:** a heavy pot over low coals (→ 06-02) or a Dutch oven (→ 07-04). A
   little water at the start stops scorching; it boils off.
3. **Let it melt out** over hours. Do not brown it: high heat gives dark, strong-tasting fat.
4. **Strain** through cloth into clean jars.
5. **Cool and seal.** Well rendered fat is white or pale and smells clean.
6. **The crisp bits left over** (cracklings) are food.

**Sources:** pork fat makes **lard** (good for cooking and baking); beef and deer fat make
**tallow** (harder; better for candles, soap and waterproofing than for baking; deer tallow is
waxy and strong-tasting). Poultry fat renders easily and tastes good.

**For hunting households:** deer are lean, and deer fat is not prized for cooking. If you want
rendered cooking fat, pork and beef fat are far better. A butcher may sell it cheaply now.

## Storage in practice

- **Cool, dark, sealed:** a cool shelf, not above the stove.
- **Small containers** so you open one at a time; each opening starts the clock.
- **Metal or glass** over plastic for long storage.
- **Keep a "used grease" jar** for cooking fat: strain and reuse it several times, then use the
  last of it for soap (→ 13-09) rather than throwing it away.

## The allergen check, again

If anyone in the house has a food allergy (→ 02-04):

- **"Vegetable oil" usually means soybean oil.** Avoid it unless the allergist clears refined soy.
- **Shortening** and **margarine** often contain soy.
- **Buy single-source oils** and keep the label (→ 04-03).
- **Fat you rendered yourself is fully traceable,** which makes it one of the safest fats in the
  house for an allergic person.

## Gear and supplies

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cooking oil: **single-source, label checked** | Calories and cooking | Olive, canola, corn, coconut |
| Coconut oil, canned shortening | Longest-keeping fats | Check ingredients |
| Ghee or **commercially** canned butter | Keeps far better than butter | **Never home-canned dairy** |
| Heavy pot or Dutch oven for rendering | Turns waste fat into food | → 07-04 |
| Straining cloth and wide-mouth jars | Rendering and storage | Reusable |
| Grease jar kept by the stove or fire | Reuse, then soap | → 13-09 |
| Labels and dates | Fat is the fastest-rotating bulk food | → 04-10 |

**If you don't have it:**

- **Fat from anything you butcher or hunt** (→ 05-09): render it rather than throw it away.
- Bacon grease and cooking drippings, strained and kept.
- **Fat from a butcher** is often cheap or free now; buy it and render it while that is easy.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** count in **pounds or quarts,** and remember it is the **shortest-lived** bulk food
  (about 1 to 2 years for liquid oil, not decades).
- **Ration:** spend fat on flavour and calories, not on deep frying. Reuse cooking grease.
- **Resupply:** **rendering** is the renewable answer, from hunting (→ 05-07), livestock
  (Section 05) or trade. It is one of the few foods with a real renewable path at household scale.
- **The long-term point:** grains keep for decades; **fat does not.** In a long emergency,
  rendered animal fat is how you get calorie density.

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** buy and rotate; render fat from any meat you process.
- **B (moving):** fat is the best calories-per-pound food to carry; a jar of rendered fat is
  remarkable energy in a small space.
- **C (rural or at a destination):** rendering becomes routine; pair it with hunting and
  butchering (→ 05-09).
