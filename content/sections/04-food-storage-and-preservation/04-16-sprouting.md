# Sprouting grains and beans for fresh food

**What this page is for.** How to turn dry beans, lentils and grains you already store into fresh
green food in a few days, on a counter, with no soil, light or fuel, and who should eat sprouts
cooked rather than raw.

> **CAUTION.** Sprouting is the cheapest fresh food you can have in a long winter, and **sprouts
> are a known higher-risk raw food:** the warm, wet conditions that grow them also grow bacteria.
> **FDA advises that children, older adults, pregnant women and people with weakened immune
> systems should not eat raw sprouts of any kind; cooking sprouts thoroughly kills the bacteria**
> (FoodSafety.gov/FDA, Sprouts: What You Should Know). Rinsing does not remove bacteria that are
> inside the sprout. Keep jars scrupulously clean.

## Do this now

1. **Buy sprouting seed now,** and note that **the dry beans and grains you already store will
   sprout,** which makes this nearly free.
2. **Set up two jars** with mesh lids and start a rotation, so something is always ready.
3. **Rinse twice a day, morning and night.** That is the whole technique and the whole safety
   control.
4. **Do not sprout anything someone in the house is allergic to** (→ 02-04).
5. **Cook sprouts thoroughly,** steaming hot all the way through, never just lightly, **for children,
   older adults, anyone pregnant and anyone with a weakened immune system.**

   {{#if has.elder}}You have an older adult in the house: their sprouts are cooked.{{/if}}
   {{#if has.pregnant}}Someone in the house is pregnant: her sprouts are cooked.{{/if}}

## Why this matters

In winter, with no garden and no shops, a diet of stored grain, beans and canned goods has
enough calories but **too few vitamins.** That is the classic long-winter deficiency problem;
historically, scurvy (lack of vitamin C).

**Sprouting fixes it with what you already store.** A handful of dry beans becomes fresh green
food in 3 to 5 days, on a kitchen counter, with **no soil, no light, no garden and no fuel.**
Sprouting raises vitamin content, including vitamin C, which dry seeds have almost none of.

**It also multiplies volume** several times over, which stretches stored staples.

## What to sprout

| Seed | Days | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Lentils** | 2 to 4 | Easiest; the best first choice |
| **Mung beans** | 3 to 5 | The classic bean sprout; forgiving |
| **Alfalfa, clover** | 4 to 6 | Small, mild, high yield |
| **Radish, broccoli** | 3 to 5 | Peppery; strong nutrition |
| **Wheat, barley, hull-on oat groats** | 2 to 3 | From bulk storage (→ 04-03). **Rolled, steel-cut or hulled oats will not sprout:** only whole groats with the hull |
| **Chickpeas, peas** | 2 to 4 | Hearty; good cooked. Chickpeas and peas are legumes, like peanuts: ask the allergist before giving them to someone with a peanut allergy |
| **Sunflower** | 2 to 3 | Hulled seed |
| **Soy, sesame** | — | Common allergens: skip them if anyone in the house is allergic |
| **Kidney beans** (and other *Phaseolus* beans) | — | **Do not eat raw sprouted kidney beans.** They contain a natural toxin; boil them hard as on → 04-03 (FDA, Bad Bug Book) |

**Use seed sold for sprouting or for food,** not garden seed. Garden seed is often coated with
fungicide and is **not food** (→ 05-03).

## The method

1. **Rinse and look over** a few tablespoons of seed; throw out damaged ones.
2. **Soak** overnight (4 to 12 hours, depending on the seed) in a clean jar.
3. **Drain completely.** Standing water is what spoils a batch.
4. **Rinse and drain twice a day,** morning and evening.
5. **Keep at room temperature, out of direct sun,** tilted mouth-down so it drains.
6. **Ready in 2 to 6 days:** eat when the sprout is about as long as the seed.
7. **Keep finished sprouts cold** and use them within a few days.

**Signs it has failed:** a musty or sour smell, sliminess, or visible mould. **Throw the batch
out** and sterilise the jar; do not try to rinse it back to health.

## Safety, honestly

Sprouts are on every food-safety agency's higher-risk list. The bacteria come **in the seed;** a
contaminated batch of seed is multiplied by the warm, wet jar, and rinsing does not reach bacteria
inside the sprout. Commercial sprouting seed is sometimes sanitised; **beans from a storage bucket
are not.** Apply the strict version:

- **Scrupulously clean jars and lids** (boiling water between batches).
- **Clean water for rinsing** (→ 03-04).
- **Rinse well and drain completely,** twice a day, without fail.
- **Cook sprouts** for children, older adults, anyone pregnant and anyone with a weakened immune
  system (→ 02-18): cooked thoroughly in a soup or stew until steaming hot all through. FDA says
  these groups should avoid raw **or lightly cooked** sprouts of any kind (FDA, Selecting and
  Serving Produce Safely). Thorough cooking kills the bacteria. Healthy adults can reduce their own risk the same way.
- **Do not eat a batch that smells wrong.**
- If someone gets sick after eating sprouts: → 12-05.

Indoor microgreens (grown in soil and light, cut above the soil) are another winter green, and
some households prefer them for children (→ 05-14).

## Fitting it into the routine

- **Two jars on a rotation** give a continuous supply from a few tablespoons of seed a week.
- **It needs no fuel and no light,** a rare thing in this binder (→ 06-04).
- **Children can help with supervision.** Rinsing jars is a job a small child can share with an
  adult, but the adult owns the hygiene (→ 20-07), and children's sprouts are cooked.

## Gear and supplies

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Wide-mouth jars (several) | The sprouting vessel | You may already have canning jars (→ 04-04) |
| **Mesh sprouting lids,** or cheesecloth and bands | Rinse and drain without losing seed | Cheap; buy several |
| Sprouting seed (lentil, mung, alfalfa, radish) | Dedicated and food-grade | Stored food beans and grains also sprout |
| A rack or bowl to hold jars tilted | Draining is the safety control | Improvised is fine |
| Clean water | Rinsing twice daily | → 03-04 |

**If you don't have it:**

- **Cheesecloth, window screen or a clean sock** with a rubber band replaces a sprouting lid.
- **Your stored lentils, mung beans, wheat and peas** are sprouting seed; no special purchase
  needed.
- Any jar, any bowl, any counter.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** effectively unlimited while you have dry seed, and sprouting **multiplies** the
  volume, so it stretches the pantry.
- **Ration:** none needed; it is a net gain.
- **Resupply:** save seed from the garden (→ 05-03), or trade for it.
- **The strategic point:** of every method in this section, sprouting has the best ratio of
  nutrition to cost, fuel, space and effort. **In a long winter it is the single best answer to
  the vitamin problem.**

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** run it all winter on the kitchen counter.
- **B (moving):** a jar and a handful of lentils travel; you can sprout on the road.
- **C (rural or at a destination):** the same, plus a real garden in season (→ 05-01).
