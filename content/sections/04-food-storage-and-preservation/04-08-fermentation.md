# Fermentation and root cellaring

**What this page is for.** Two ways to keep vegetables for months with no jars-per-batch, no
fuel and no electricity: fermenting them in salt brine (sauerkraut is the model), and storing
raw crops cool in a "root cellar", which can be any cold, dark corner with the right humidity.

> **CAUTION.** Fermentation is safe **because of the salt strength and the absence of air.**
> Get the salt amount from a tested recipe and keep the vegetables **under the brine.** Too
> little salt, or vegetables exposed to air, and the wrong organisms grow. A good ferment smells
> sour and clean; **slimy, foul, pink or fuzzy means throw it out.**

## Do this now

1. **Learn sauerkraut first.** Cabbage and salt, nothing else. It is the most forgiving ferment
   and a real source of vitamin C through winter.
2. **Measure the salt exactly** to the recipe. It is the safety control, not seasoning.
3. **Keep everything under the brine** with a weight, a bag of brine, or a cabbage leaf on top.
4. **Ferment cool:** 60 to 75 °F. Above 75 °F kraut may go soft; below 60 °F it may not ferment
   (USDA Complete Guide, Guide 6).
5. **Store raw what keeps raw:** potatoes, carrots, beets, cabbage, onions, squash, apples.

## Why fermentation matters

- **Almost nothing runs out.** Salt, water, vegetables, a crock. No lids, no new jars each batch.
- **No fuel at all,** unlike canning (→ 04-04), which uses a lot (→ 06-04).
- **Nutrition.** Fermented vegetables keep their vitamins well and give **vitamin C through
  winter,** the old answer to scurvy.
- **It scales with a garden** (→ 05-01): cabbage is easy to grow and turns directly into months
  of stored food.

## How it works

Salt makes conditions where **lactic acid bacteria** (which tolerate salt) outgrow the organisms
that spoil food. They make lactic acid, the pH drops (the food becomes more acidic, → 29-08), and
the food preserves itself. Air is the enemy: the food must stay **under** its brine.

**The method (sauerkraut):**

1. **Shred the cabbage.** Weigh it.
2. **Add salt at the tested rate.** The USDA rate for sauerkraut is **3 tablespoons of canning
   or pickling salt per 5 lb of shredded cabbage** (USDA Complete Guide, Guide 6). Weigh the
   cabbage so the salt matches it. Other vegetables have their own tested rates: **use the
   Guide, not memory.** {{#if size.large}}Guide 6 is bound in your binder at the end of this
   section, after the last food-storage page; the "Sources for this page" list below links to
   it.{{else}}Your binder is the Small download, which only cites Guide 6; the Large and
   Everything downloads carry it. Keep a paper copy.{{/if}}
3. **Massage or pound** until the cabbage releases its own liquid.
4. **Pack it into a crock or jar,** pressing down so the brine covers everything.
5. **Weight it down** so nothing floats. If the juice does not cover the cabbage, add brine
   that was boiled and cooled: 1½ tablespoons of salt per quart of water (Guide 6).
6. **Cover loosely:** gas must escape. An airlock lid or a cloth works.
7. **Ferment cool.** USDA: **70 to 75 °F for about 3 to 4 weeks; 60 to 65 °F for 5 to 6 weeks;
   below 60 °F it may not ferment; above 75 °F it may go soft.** Taste as it goes.
8. **Skim any surface film.** When it tastes right, move it somewhere cold to slow it down.

**Judging it:** a good ferment smells **sour, clean and cabbagey;** cloudy brine is normal.
**Throw it out** if it is slimy, foul, pink or red, or has fuzzy mould. A thin white film on the
surface (kahm yeast) is common and can be skimmed off.

**Do not water-bath-can a ferment casually.** Heat kills the live cultures, and canning
fermented food needs its own tested recipe (→ 04-04).

## Root cellaring: storage that costs nothing

Many crops keep raw for months, with **no processing at all,** in the right conditions. The
four storage groups below are Washington State University Extension's (Storing Your Garden
Harvest, 2019).

| Crop | Wants | Keeps |
|---|---|---|
| **Potatoes, cabbage, apples** | Cold and moist: 32 to 40 °F, 80 to 90 percent humidity, dark; **apples kept apart from the vegetables** | Months |
| **Carrots, beets, turnips, parsnips** | Cold and very moist: 32 to 40 °F, 90 to 95 percent humidity: pack in damp sand | Months |
| **Onions, garlic** | Cold and **dry:** 32 to 50 °F, 60 to 70 percent humidity, airy | Months |
| **Winter squash, pumpkins** | Cool and **dry:** 50 to 60 °F, 60 to 70 percent humidity | Months |

Potatoes are the fussiest: check them often (WSU).

**Two rules:** **apples, pears and tomatoes give off ethylene gas,** which ages other crops
faster; keep them away from cabbage, carrots, potatoes and onions (WSU Extension).
And **onions and squash want dry air** while roots want humid air: two different corners.

**Where to do it.** {{#if has.basement}}Your basement is the asset here.{{else}}Any unheated room,
closet against an outside wall, crawlspace or shed that stays cold but above freezing will
work.{{/if}} In a grid-down winter, if you heat only one room (→ 08-01), the unheated rest of
the house becomes a working cold store (→ 04-09). {{#if !is.cold_winters}}Where winters
are mild, a cool spot is harder to find; the crops above still keep for weeks to a few months in
the coolest place you have.{{/if}}

## Allergen note

Fermenting suits a food-allergic household: cabbage, salt and water, all easy to check
(→ 02-04). Watch for recipes that add soy sauce, fish sauce or sesame, as some kimchi-style
recipes do. Keep allergy-safe batches separate and labelled.

## Gear and supplies

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fermentation crock, or wide-mouth jars | The vessel | Crocks are traditional; jars work |
| **Fermentation weights** | Keeping food under brine is the safety control | Glass weights, or a boiled stone |
| Airlock lids (or cloth and a band) | Lets gas out, keeps air off | Cheap |
| **Salt: a large quantity** | The preservative | → 04-13; **non-iodised** canning salt for ferments |
| **Kitchen scale** | Weigh the vegetables so the salt matches the recipe | The safety-critical tool |
| **USDA Complete Guide, Guide 6** (fermented foods and pickled vegetables) | Tested rates | Bound in the Large and Everything downloads |
| Damp sand and bins or crates | Roots keep for months | Free |
| Thermometer and humidity gauge for the cold store | Know your conditions | Cheap |
| Shredder, mandoline or cabbage knife | Prep in volume | → 16-02 |

**If you don't have it:**

- **Any food-safe bucket** with a plate and a weight ferments cabbage well.
- A **boiled rock** or a water-filled bag makes a weight.
- Sand, leaves or straw in crates for root storage; an unheated corner as the cellar.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** **salt** is the only real consumable, and it does not spoil. Buy a lot now
  (→ 04-13). Everything else is reusable.
- **Ration:** none needed. This is the method to lean on because it uses almost nothing.
- **Resupply:** salt is the one thing to stockpile heavily; most places cannot make it.
- **The strategic point:** **fermenting, drying and root cellaring together need no lids, no fuel
  and no electricity.** In a long emergency they are the preserving system; canning is the
  luxury while lids last.

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** ferment now for practice; use the coldest part of the house as a
  cellar in winter.
- **B (moving):** does not travel: crocks are heavy and ferments need stillness.
- **C (rural or at a destination):** the core preserving method alongside drying. Build a real
  root cellar if the site allows.
