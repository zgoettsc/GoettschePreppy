# Rotation, dating and inventory

**What this page is for.** How to keep a food store fresh and counted, what the dates on food
packages mean, and how to run the "pantry week" that finds problems before an emergency does.

> **CAUTION.** The most common way a food stockpile fails is not disaster. It is **quiet
> expiry.** Food bought once and never looked at again becomes a pile of unknowns exactly when
> you need certainty. Rotation is boring, and it is the difference between a supply and a guess.

## Do this now

1. **Date every item on purchase,** in marker, large enough to read on a dim shelf.
2. **Shelve new behind old** so your hand always reaches the oldest first.
3. **Count everything once,** on paper, and keep the sheet with the food (→ 22-04).
4. **Run a "pantry week"** every few months: cook only from stores.
5. **Recount and work out the runways every three months,** and write them on → 23-02.

## Dates: what they mean and what they do not

- **"Best if used by", "sell by" and most "use by" dates are about quality, not safety,** for
  shelf-stable food. **Infant formula is the exception:** its use-by date matters (USDA FSIS,
  Food Product Dating). How to read the different date formats: → 29-14.
- **Canned food stays safe as long as the seal is unbroken** and the can was never frozen
  (freezing can burst cans and break seals). **Throw out any badly dented, bulging, rusty or
  leaking can or jar** before opening it. Best quality lasts to the code date, usually 2 to 5
  years from manufacture; high-acid canned food (tomatoes, fruit) keeps its best quality for a
  shorter time than low-acid food (meat, vegetables) (Utah State University Extension, Storing
  Canned Goods). Home-canned food: use within a year for quality, and before two years.
- **The real limits** are: rancid fat in oils and whole grains (→ 04-12), rust or seam damage in
  cans, moisture in dry goods, and any broken seal.
- **Your own purchase-date mark is often more useful** than the printed date: it tells you how
  long *you* have had it.
- **How strictly to follow the dates** when food is short is a decision card:
  {{choice DC-14}} → 30-15. Bulging, leaking or spurting cans are not part of that choice:
  they are thrown away.

## The rotation system that works

1. **One direction.** New goes in at the back or top; you take from the front or bottom.
2. **Group by type,** not by purchase date; rotate within each group.
3. **Working stock at eye level,** deep stock below or behind.
4. **Open one at a time.** An open bucket or box is on the clock; finish it before opening the
   next.
5. **Write down what you take.** A pencil on a clipboard by the shelf is enough.

## The inventory sheet

Keep it simple enough that you will keep it up:

| Item | Unit | Qty | Date packed | Where | Safe for each allergy? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| White rice | 5-gal bucket | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ |
| Pinto beans | 5-gal bucket | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ |
| Cooking oil | quart | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ |
| Canned tomatoes | can | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ |
| ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ |

**The two columns that matter most:** **date** (rotation) and, if anyone in the house has a food
allergy, **safe or not** (→ 02-04). For freeze-dried food, use the fuller table on → 04-19. The
binder's expiry tracker is → 26-08.

## Pantry week: the check that finds real problems

Every few months, cook **only** from stores for a week. It shows:

- Food nobody will eat (stop buying it).
- Food you do not know how to cook (learn now).
- Missing basics: the spice, the fat, the can opener.
- Items past their useful life.
- **Whether everyone in the house, including anyone on a restricted diet, can actually eat a
  week of your stored food.** This is the most important finding.

**Do one in winter, cooking outdoors** if you can (→ 07-01), and you will learn more in a
weekend than in a year of reading (→ 24-02).

## Working out the runways

Every three months, update → 23-02:

- **Household food-days** = total calories on hand ÷ {{ derived.calories.day }} (→ 04-01).
- **Each restricted diet's food-days,** from that person's safe food only; usually the shorter
  number.
- **Water-days** (→ 03-01), **fuel-days** (→ 06-04), and **medicine-days** for anyone on daily
  medicine (→ 12-07).

Those numbers are the dashboard for the stay-or-go decision (→ 01-02).

## Gear and supplies

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Permanent markers (a pack) | Date everything | Cheap, always missing |
| Clipboard and printed inventory sheets | Kept up beats elegant | Keep it with the food |
| Shelving that lets you rotate front to back | The system works or it does not | |
| Labels for repackaged goods, saved ingredient panels | Allergen safety (→ 02-04) | Tape inside the lid |
| A reminder every three months | Habits decay | Same day you test the smoke alarms |

**If you don't have it:**

- Paper and pencil beat any app that needs a charged phone.
- Any shelf can be arranged front to back; masking tape and marker label everything.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** this page makes the runway numbers rather than using anything up.
- **The failure to avoid:** finding in month three that half the pantry expired in year one, or
  that the deep stock is full of something an allergic person cannot eat. Both are prevented by
  a marker and an hour every three months.

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** build the habit now; it is the cheapest discipline in the binder.
- **B (moving):** the inventory sheet tells you what to take in minutes (→ 01-06).
- **C (rural or at a destination):** count everything again on arrival; the numbers all change.
