# Salt, sugar, vinegar and honey: the preserving staples

**What this page is for.** The four foods that make almost all other food preservation work:
why to stock them, how much, how to store them, and the one safety rule about honey.

> **CAUTION.** These four are the chemical basis of nearly all food preserving. They keep
> essentially **for ever,** they are **cheap now,** and most households **cannot make any of
> them** at useful scale. That makes them the best value-per-dollar stockpile in the food
> section. **One safety rule: never give honey to a baby under 12 months.** It can carry
> botulism spores that a baby's gut cannot handle (→ 20-01).

## Do this now

1. **Buy salt in quantity. Then buy more.** It is the master preservative and it never expires.
2. **Stock sugar and honey** for preserving, calories and morale (→ 21-02).
3. **Stock vinegar of known strength (5 percent acidity)** for pickling. Canning recipes assume
   it.
4. **Keep them dry and sealed.** They do not spoil, but they cake, absorb moisture and attract
   pests (→ 04-11).
5. **No honey for any baby under 12 months.** {{#if has.infant}}**You have a child under 2 in the
   house: check the age before anyone offers honey,** including honey in baked goods or
   medicine.{{/if}}

## Salt: the one you cannot replace

Salt is the base of curing (→ 04-07), fermenting (→ 04-08), brining, and preserving meat and
vegetables. It also seasons food that is otherwise dull, which matters more over months than
people expect.

- **Storage: indefinite.** Keep it dry; it cakes but stays usable.
- **Kinds:** plain, non-iodised **canning or pickling salt** for ferments and cures: **iodised
  salt and anti-caking agents can discolour ferments and cloud brine.** Keep both: iodised for the
  table (iodine is a real nutritional need) and plain for preserving.
- **Quantity:** far more than you think. Curing meat uses pounds at a time.
- **Most households cannot practically make food salt.** It is a stockpile item, and so an
  excellent **barter good** (→ 18-02).
{{#if has.saltwater_pool}}- **Your saltwater pool** holds a large amount of dissolved salt, and
  distilling pool water (→ 03-03) leaves concentrated brine behind. That residue is
  **emergency-grade, not food-grade:** pool water carries cyanuric acid (a chlorine stabiliser)
  and whatever else was ever added to it. Treat boiled-down pool salt as a true last resort for
  preserving brines, never the plan, and never as table salt for children.{{/if}}

## Sugar and honey

- **Sugar** keeps indefinitely if dry. Used for jams, preserving fruit, baking and morale. **Do not
  pack sugar with oxygen absorbers:** it sets into a solid brick (→ 04-03).
- **Honey** keeps practically for ever. Crystallising is normal and reverses with gentle warmth. It
  is a real morale food.
- **Never for babies under 12 months.** Honey can contain *Clostridium botulinum* spores. An older
  child's or adult's gut handles them; a baby's cannot. This is a hard rule (→ 20-01, → 02-13).

## Vinegar

- **Use vinegar of known acidity (5 percent)** for canning. Recipes are calculated on it, and
  homemade vinegar of unknown strength is **not safe** for canning (→ 04-04).
- **Keeps indefinitely.**
- **Uses beyond food:** cleaning and descaling when other cleaners run out (→ 13-03). **Floor:**
  **Bleach or pool shock is never mixed with ammonia, acids or other cleaners.** Vinegar is an
  acid; the mix gives off chlorine gas (→ 30-01).
- **You can make vinegar** from fruit or cider with a "mother" culture. It is fine for eating and
  cleaning, but **not for canning,** because you cannot check its acidity.

## The barter angle

Salt and sugar are close to ideal trade goods (→ 18-02): everyone wants them, they keep for ever,
they divide into small amounts, and few people can make them. Unlike ammunition (→ 14-08),
trading them puts no risk in someone else's hands.

## Storage

- **Airtight and dry.** Moisture is the only enemy.
- **Food-grade buckets** for bulk salt and sugar; a working container in the kitchen.
- **Off concrete,** cool and dark (→ 04-03).
- **Pest-proof:** sugar draws insects and rodents (→ 04-11).

## Quantities to think about

| Item | How to size it | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Salt** | **The biggest of the three:** a year of table use plus every cure and ferment you plan | Curing and fermenting use it by the cup and the pound; cheap; keeps for ever |
| Sugar | A year of baking plus the jam and fruit recipes you plan | Preserving, baking, morale |
| Honey | What you would use in a year | Keeps; morale |
| Vinegar (5 percent) | Your pickling recipes for a season, plus cleaning | Pickling, cleaning |
| Canning or pickling salt | A separate stock | Ferments and cures (→ 04-08) |
| Curing salt (nitrite) | Small, labelled, **locked** | → 04-07; poisonous in small amounts |

**The method:** write down what your {{ household.size }} people use in a normal month, multiply
by 12, then add what your tested preserving recipes call for (for example, the USDA sauerkraut
recipe uses 3 tablespoons of salt per 5 lb of cabbage, → 04-08). Check the total against what
you have.

## Gear and supplies

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bulk salt** (plain and iodised) | The master preservative | The cheapest high-value stockpile there is |
| Bulk sugar | Preserving and morale | Dry, sealed, no oxygen absorbers |
| Honey | Keeps for ever; morale | **Not for babies under 12 months** |
| Vinegar, 5 percent acidity | Canning and cleaning | Known strength matters |
| Airtight buckets or containers | Moisture is the only threat | → 04-03 |
| Kitchen scale | Cure and ferment amounts are by weight | → 04-08 |
| Labels | Tell canning salt from curing salt | Critical: curing salt is poisonous |

**If you don't have it:**

- **Vinegar can be made** from fruit or cider: fine for eating and cleaning, **never for canning.**
- Maple sap can be boiled down to syrup where the trees grow; it takes an enormous amount of fuel
  (→ 06-04).
- **Salt has no substitute.** That is exactly why you stockpile it now.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** simply the amount you bought ÷ how fast you use it. These do not spoil.
- **Ration:** salt used for **preserving** creates stored food; salt used for seasoning is spent.
  Put preserving first.
- **Resupply:** trade (→ 18-02) is the realistic route.
- **The strategic point:** of everything in this section, **salt is the highest-value,
  lowest-cost, longest-lasting purchase you can make today.** If the budget is tight this month,
  buy salt.

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** buy in bulk now.
- **B (moving):** salt is heavy but valuable; carry some. It is compact currency (→ 18-02).
- **C (rural or at a destination):** essential for preserving game and garden produce; a top
  trade good with neighbours.
