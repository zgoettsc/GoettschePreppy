# Water-bath canning (high-acid foods)

**What this page is for.** How boiling-water canning works, which foods it is safe for, how to
adjust for your altitude, and when to throw a jar out. It teaches the method. The tested
recipes, with their process times, are in the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning. If canning
words are new to you, read → 29-08 first.

> **SAFETY.** Botulism is a toxin that paralyses, including the breathing muscles. It is
> invisible, has no smell or taste, and can kill.
>
> **Floor:** **Low-acid foods are pressure-canned to the published time and pressure, or not
> canned.** Botulism has no field treatment and the food looks and smells normal (→ 30-01).
>
> High-acid foods only in a boiling-water canner; everything low-acid goes to a pressure
> canner (→ 04-05). **Never** invent a recipe, never cut the acid, and never use an old family
> recipe that contradicts current USDA guidance. **Use a current tested recipe every time.**

## Do this now

1. **Have the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning (2015 revision) where you can read it with
   no internet.** {{#if size.large}}Your binder carries it: the Introduction and all seven
   Guides are bound at the end of this section, after the last food-storage page, and the
   "Sources for this page" list below links to each one. If a Guide could not be downloaded
   when the binder was made, it is named on the "Sources not captured" page in Section 25:
   get a paper copy of that one.{{else}}Your binder is the Small download, which cites the
   Guide but does not carry it. Make the Large or Everything download, or keep a paper copy.
   **Without it, this binder holds no tested recipe and no process time, and you must not can
   from memory.**{{/if}} A current tested recipe book is a sensible second copy.
2. **Water-bath ONLY these:** fruits, jams and jellies, pickles, and **properly acidified**
   tomatoes and salsas.
3. **Never water-bath:** vegetables, meat, poultry, fish, beans, soups or anything low-acid.
   Those are pressure-canning foods, no exceptions (→ 04-05).
4. **Acidify tomatoes. For whole, crushed or juiced tomatoes: per PINT, 1 tablespoon bottled
   lemon juice OR ¼ teaspoon citric acid; per QUART, 2 tablespoons bottled lemon juice OR
   ½ teaspoon citric acid** (USDA Complete Guide, Guide 3). Put it in the jar before filling.
   **Bottled** juice only: fresh lemons vary. Vinegar of 5 percent acidity also works, at
   4 tablespoons per quart, but may change the flavour (Guide 3). Salsas and mixed tomato
   recipes print their own acid amounts: use the recipe's. Tomatoes are not reliably acidic
   enough on their own, and pH strips are unreliable in coloured food: the acid goes in
   whatever a strip says.
5. **Adjust the process time for your altitude** (table below). If the recipe prints its own
   altitude table, **the recipe's table governs.**
6. **Before eating ANY home-canned tomatoes or low-acid food, yours or anyone else's, boil it
   hard in an open pan for 10 minutes plus 1 minute per 1,000 ft of elevation,** liquid
   included (CDC, Botulism Prevention). Your times are in the table below. Heat destroys the
   toxin; this is the cheap backstop when the process is doubtful or unknown (a traded jar, a
   jar you are unsure of). **It does not rescue a bulging, leaking or foul jar: those are thrown
   away unopened.** The same rule is on → 04-05.

## Why acidity decides everything

*Clostridium botulinum* spores are common in soil and are **not killed by boiling water**.
What stops them is **acid**: at pH 4.6 or below (→ 29-08), the spores cannot grow and make
toxin. So:

- **High-acid (pH 4.6 or lower):** fruit, pickles, properly acidified tomatoes. Boiling water
  is enough, because it only has to kill the organisms that spoil food, not botulism spores.
- **Low-acid (pH above 4.6):** vegetables, meat, fish, beans. These **must** reach the
  temperatures only a pressure canner reaches (→ 04-05).

**This is not a preference. It is the whole reason home canning is safe or lethal.**

## The method

1. **Inspect jars.** Throw out any with chips or cracks. **Use new lids each time;** bands are
   reusable.
2. **Wash and heat** the jars; keep them hot so they do not crack.
3. **Prepare the food to a tested recipe,** including the acid it specifies.
4. **Fill the jars,** leaving the **headspace** (empty space at the top) the recipe gives. It
   decides whether a seal forms.
5. **Remove air bubbles,** wipe the rim completely clean (a smear of food stops a seal), fit the
   lid, and screw the band on fingertip-tight, not hard.
6. **Load the canner,** jars on a rack, with **at least 1 inch of water over the jar tops**
   (at least 2 inches if the process is longer than 30 minutes) (USDA Complete Guide, Guide 1).
7. **Bring to a full rolling boil, then start timing.** Add the altitude time from the table.
8. **Turn off the heat, wait 5 minutes, lift the jars out** and set them on a towel,
   undisturbed, for 12 to 24 hours.
9. **Check the seals:** the lid is pulled down (concave) and does not flex. **Any unsealed jar
   goes in the fridge and is eaten now,** or is reprocessed with a new lid.
10. **Remove the bands, wipe, label with contents and date,** and store cool and dark.

## When to throw it out: no exceptions

Throw away, without tasting, any jar that:

- has a **bulging or unsealed lid,** or spurts when opened
- shows **mould, cloudiness or bubbling**
- smells **wrong**
- has leaked, or has food dried under the lid

**Never taste to check.** Put it where children and animals cannot reach it. Eating food from
a doubtful but sealed, normal-looking jar is a decision card: {{choice DC-14}} → 30-15. Bulging,
leaking or spurting jars are not part of that choice: they are thrown away.

## Canning without the grid

Water-bath canning works over an outdoor fire (→ 07-01) or a camp burner (→ 07-05): it needs only
a big pot, a rack and a hard, steady boil.

- **It uses a lot of fuel** (→ 06-04). Do a full canner load per fire, not one jar.
- **Holding a rolling boil for the whole process time** is the real challenge on wood. Build
  the fire for it and have wood ready.
- **A windbreak cuts the fuel cost a great deal** (→ 07-01).
- **Open flame indoors** (a camp stove in the kitchen) is a decision card: {{choice DC-20}}
  → 30-21.

## Allergen note

Home canning suits a food-allergic household: you know every ingredient because you put it in
(→ 02-04). **Label the allergy-safe jars distinctly** and keep them apart. Watch for shared
equipment and surfaces, and for recipes that add a common allergen.

## Gear and supplies

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Boiling-water canner and rack (or a big stockpot and rack) | The vessel | Deep enough for 1 to 2 in of water over the jars |
| **Mason jars** (pints and quarts), many | Reusable for decades | Check for chips every use |
| **Lids: a large stock** | **Single-use: the real consumable** | Buy deep, or a reusable-lid system |
| Bands | Reusable | A few spares |
| Jar lifter, funnel, bubble tool, magnetic lid lifter | Safety and speed | Cheap kit |
| **USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning (2015), all seven Guides** | The tested recipes and times | Bound in the Large and Everything downloads; a paper copy is good insurance |
| pH strips (optional) | Curiosity only | **Never a substitute for the printed acid amounts** |
| Bottled lemon juice, citric acid, 5 percent vinegar | Acidifying | → 04-13; bottled for known acidity |
| Labels and marker | Contents and date | Not optional |

**If you don't have it:**

- Any large pot with a rack (or a folded towel on the bottom) works as a canner.
- **Jars are easy to find second-hand; lids are not.** Lids are the thing to stockpile.
- Commercial food jars (from pasta sauce and the like) are generally **not** suitable: mason
  jars are made for the heat stress.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** **lids** are the limiting consumable. Count them: one lid per jar per batch.
- **Ration:** keep canning for foods that really need it; use drying (→ 04-06), fermenting
  (→ 04-08) and winter cold (→ 04-09) for the rest. None of those use lids.
- **Resupply:** reusable-lid systems are a real long-term answer; buy some now. Fermenting and
  drying need no consumables at all.
- **The strategic point:** canning is your highest-quality preserving method and the most
  dependent on consumables. Fermenting and drying are the methods that last.

## Phase notes

- **A (sheltering at home):** learn it now, on the kitchen stove, with a tested recipe.
- **B (moving):** jars are heavy and fragile; this does not travel.
- **C (rural or at a destination):** the core way to keep a garden surplus; pair it with
  fermenting and drying to save lids.
