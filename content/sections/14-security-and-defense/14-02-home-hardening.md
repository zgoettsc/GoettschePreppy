# Hardening the home without trapping the family

**What this page is for.** Cheap changes to doors, windows, lights and gates that make an
opportunist pick another house and give you warning, without ever stopping your family getting
out in a fire.

> **CAUTION.** Two rules come before everything here. **First: never trap your family.** Bars,
> barricades and screwed-shut windows that stop an intruder also stop you escaping a fire, and
> **fire is a more likely killer than intrusion** (→ 14-01). **Second: work you do now, cheaply,
> in daylight, is worth ten times what you improvise in the dark.**

## Do this now

1. **Fix the door frame, not the lock.** Nearly every forced entry into a home is a kicked door
   splitting a soft wooden frame, not a picked lock.
2. **Put 3-inch screws** in every exterior door's hinges and strike plate (the metal plate the
   bolt goes into), long enough to reach the framing stud behind. A small box of screws and
   twenty minutes; it is the best-value security change in this binder.
3. **Add a reinforced strike plate** or a door-jamb reinforcement kit on exterior doors.
4. **Block sliding doors** with a bar or pin in the track.
5. **Keep a way out:** every bedroom needs an exit that opens from the inside without a key.
6. **Light the outside** with motion-activated solar lights on the approaches.

## The real weak point: the frame

A deadbolt is only as strong as the wood it goes into. Ordinary construction uses a soft pine
door frame with short screws into the trim. A single kick splits it.

{{figure fig-door-strike-3in-screws}}

**The fixes, best value first:**

1. **3-inch screws** in hinges and strike plates, reaching the stud behind the frame.
2. **Reinforced strike plate** (a long steel plate spanning several inches).
3. **Door-jamb reinforcement kit** (a full-length steel channel) for the main doors.
4. **Solid-core or steel exterior doors** if you are replacing a door anyway.
5. **A second deadbolt** or a floor-mounted brace for the most exposed door.

**Start with the doors people actually force:** the back door, the garage side door, and any
door hidden from the street.

## Windows and sliding doors

- **Sliding doors:** a bar or dowel in the track, plus an anti-lift screw in the upper track.
- **Ground-floor windows:** extra sash locks or pins. Security film on the glass does not stop it
  breaking, but it makes getting through quickly much harder.
- **Basement windows** are often the weakest point and rarely considered.
- **Never screw bedroom windows shut.** They are a way out in a fire. This is not negotiable
  {{#if has.child}}with children in the house{{else}}for anyone{{/if}}.

## Lighting and what can be seen

- **Motion-activated solar lights** on approaches need no wiring and keep working when the power
  is out, which is exactly when you need them.
- **Trim shrubs** away from doors and windows so nobody can hide there.
- **Keep climbing aids out of the yard**: bins, ladders and patio furniture near fences.
- **Balance this against not standing out (→ 14-03).** Floodlights on a dark street make your
  house the obvious one. Motion-activated lights are the compromise: dark until something moves.

## The garage

{{#if has.garage}}Your house has an attached garage.{{else}}If you have or move to a house with
an attached garage:{{/if}} it is a common weak point, with a house door that is often hollow
with a cheap lock, and an overhead door that can sometimes be released from outside.

- **Upgrade the house-to-garage door** like an exterior door, because it is one.
- **Protect the overhead door's emergency release** against someone hooking it from outside
  with a coat hanger: use a zip-tie that breaks when pulled hard from inside, or a release
  shield. **Never disable the release.** Without power it is the only way to open that door,
  and the garage is a second way out; disabling it breaks this page's first rule.
- **The carbon monoxide floor applies:** never run a generator, charcoal, or any engine indoors,
  in a garage, or near an open window. Carbon monoxide kills in minutes with no warning
  (CDC, Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Basics; → 08-02).

## Gates and padlocks

If you have a fenced yard, padlocks on the gates turn the fence into a real boundary that slows
an opportunist and makes noise.

- **Open the packages now and put the keys where every adult can find them.** A padlock whose
  key is lost in a drawer locks *you* out. Write where the keys hang on the photo below or by
  hand; it does not go in your settings.
- **Check whether the locks are keyed alike.** Two padlocks of the same model are keyed
  differently unless they were sold as a keyed-alike set (the same key number on both
  packages). If they differ, put all the keys on one ring, tagged by gate, and make the spare
  ring match.
- **The lock is only as strong as the hasp and latch it hangs on.** A hasp screwed into soft wood
  where bolt cutters can reach it makes the padlock decoration. Bolt a shrouded hasp through the
  gate frame with carriage bolts (heads outside, nuts inside).
- **A locked gate must never trap anyone.** In a fire or a forced evacuation the gates are ways
  out. Everyone old enough knows where the key is, and the key ring hangs by the door you would
  leave through, not inside a safe. Firefighters and medics also need a way in: unlock when you
  call for help.
- **Lock the gates at the start of an event; unlock them only while someone is outside** (water
  hauling, generator runs). Add gravel, bells or cans on the gates for warning (→ 14-12).
- **Outdoor locks freeze and seize.** Every three to six months, and again before winter, blow
  the dust out of the keyway and put a light shot of **PTFE (Teflon) lock lubricant** in the
  keyway and shackle holes, then work the key several times. Regular lubrication helps stop locks
  freezing. **Not graphite, not silicone:** the manufacturer's own care guidance says never use
  either, and that graphite is often put on too heavily and jams the moving parts (Master Lock,
  Padlock care and lubrication). A jammed lock in deep cold is exactly the lock that traps your
  family. Frozen anyway? Warm the body of the lock with
  a hand warmer or warm water. **Never force the key.** Check the locks on your twice-yearly kit
  day (→ 22-03).

## Layered, not fortified

Do not try to turn a house into a bunker. It does not work, it is expensive, it alarms the
neighbours (→ 14-03) and it creates fire traps. The goal is **delay, noise and visibility**:
enough that an opportunist chooses another house, and enough that you get warning.

| Layer | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Street and yard | Lighting, clear views, gravel, a dog (→ 14-12) |
| Perimeter (fence, if any) | Delay and a clear boundary |
| Structure | Doors, frames, windows, locks |
| Inside | A gathering room where the family goes |
| Response | Awareness, communication, neighbours (→ 14-01, → 18-01) |

## The inside gathering room

Pick the room where the family goes if something happens at night. In winter this is often the
room you are already heating and sleeping in (→ 08-01). It should have a solid door, a phone or
radio, a light, and nobody should have to cross the house to reach the children.

**Do not confuse it with the storm shelter.** Your tornado shelter{{#if has.shelter_room}}
({{ dwelling.shelter_room }}){{/if}} is chosen for different reasons: lowest floor, inside,
away from windows (→ 17-02, → 17-06). Know both, and which one is for which emergency.

## Fire: the rule that overrides the rest

- **Two ways out of every sleeping area**, always.
- **Smoke and CO alarms**, battery-powered and tested (→ 08-02).
- **Fire extinguishers** where fires start: kitchen, heater, fuel storage (→ 29-12).
- **Practise the exit with the children** (→ 24-02). A four-year-old needs to have done it, not
  just heard about it.
- **Never barricade a bedroom window** that is someone's way out.

## Gear

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **3-inch screws** (a box) | The best-value upgrade here | Hinges and strike plates, into studs |
| Reinforced strike plates or jamb kits | Stops the kicked-door failure | Main exterior doors |
| Sliding-door bars or track pins | Cheap, effective | Plus anti-lift screws |
| Window sash locks or pins | Ground floor and basement | Not on escape windows |
| Security window film | Slows fast entry through glass | Ground floor |
| Motion-activated solar lights | Warning without the grid | Approaches and dark sides |
| Reinforcement for the garage-to-house door | Common weak point | Treat it as an exterior door |
| Extra deadbolt or floor brace | Most exposed door | Opens without a key from inside |
| Smoke and CO alarms, extinguishers | The more likely threat | → 08-02 |
| Gravel on approaches | Free, silent alarm | → 14-01 |

| PTFE lock lubricant | Keeps outdoor locks working in cold | Every 3 to 6 months and before winter; never graphite or silicone (manufacturer) |

**If you do not have it:**

- **A 2×4 across a door** in brackets is a strong barricade, but **only where it does not block
  a way out**, and it must lift off from inside.
- Furniture against a door for delay and noise.
- Bells, cans or gravel for warning; boards over broken glass.
- Doorstop wedges are cheap and surprisingly effective.

## When it runs out

Hardening does not get used up: it is a one-time job that keeps paying. Lumber and screws can be
found almost anywhere; the method matters more than the material. Most of it also helps in
normal life, and a reinforced, weather-sealed door doubles as winterizing (→ 08-03).

## By phase

- **A (at home):** do all of it now, in daylight, with a drill. Cheap and permanent.
- **B (on the move):** you are leaving the house; lock it and take what matters.
- **C (a new place):** look at unfamiliar buildings the same way (doors, windows, approaches),
  with more attention to long views and access roads.
