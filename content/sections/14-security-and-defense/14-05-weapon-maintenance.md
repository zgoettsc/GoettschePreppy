# Firearm maintenance and cleaning without resupply

**What this page is for.** Keeping firearms working for years with no gun shop: how to unload
safely before any work, what really stops guns working, how to fight rust in an unheated house,
what to use when gun oil runs out, and how to keep lead away from children. If you have never
handled a firearm, read → 29-11 first and ask someone who knows the specific gun to show you.

> **CAUTION.** Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products.
> Fixed in every source (floor, → 30-01). In a long emergency the failures change: **rust and
> broken springs, not dirt**, take guns out of service. An unheated house swinging between cold
> and warm puts condensation on cold steel, and it works on every firearm at once while you are
> busy with other things.

## Do this now

1. **Before any maintenance, unload and check three times.** Magazine out and the magazine well
   checked; chamber checked **by eye and by finger**; **every round of ammunition carried out of
   the room**, including loaded magazines and loose rounds (the same routine as → 14-15); muzzle
   pointed at something that would stop a bullet the whole time. Cleaning and "cycling the
   action" (working the moving parts) are when unintended shots happen.
2. **Read the lead and solvent rules below** before the first cleaning: never in the kitchen,
   never on a food surface, gloves on, wash before touching children or food.
3. **Lubrication beats cleanliness** for reliability. A dirty, well-oiled rifle keeps working; a
   spotless dry one often does not.
4. **Wipe every firearm down monthly** in storage, and **at once** after damp, snow, or coming
   from cold into warm.
5. **Stock oil and grease like ammunition.** Cheap now, hard to get later.
6. **Clean rimfire (.22) and suppressed firearms more often.** Both get dirty fast (→ 14-10).

## What really stops firearms working

| Cause | How often | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Magazines** | **Most common** | Replace; they wear out (→ 14-06) |
| **Too little oil** | Very common | Lubricate properly, more than you think |
| **Broken springs** | Common over time | Spare springs (→ 14-06) |
| **Rust and corrosion** | The long-term killer | Oil film, dry storage |
| **Worn extractor or ejector** (the parts that pull out and throw out the empty case) | Common after many rounds | Spares |
| **Carbon fouling** (burnt powder) | Least common real cause | Clean the parts where it builds up |

## Notes by type

**AR-pattern rifles** (the common semi-automatic rifle family):

- **Run it wet.** The bolt carrier group (the main moving part) needs a real film of oil on the
  bolt, cam pin and carrier rails. Too little oil causes far more stoppages than dirt.
- **Parts that need attention:** bolt lugs, the tail of the bolt (carbon builds behind the gas
  rings), the extractor and its spring, the cam pin, the inside of the carrier, and the chamber.
- **Gas rings wear.** A common check: a bolt pulled out to its extended position should hold its
  own weight when the carrier is stood on end. Keep spares (→ 14-06).
- **Do not over-scrub the bore.** A steel rod used hard without a bore guide does more harm over a
  lifetime than carbon ever would. A bore snake (a pull-through cord) and moderate solvent use is
  fine.
- **Suppressed rifles get dirtier and hotter**: more gas and carbon blows back into the action.
  They need more oil and more frequent attention (→ 14-10).

**Rimfire (.22 LR):** dirty ammunition with waxy bullets that fouls chambers and suppressors fast.
A neglected .22 becomes sluggish and unreliable. Clean the chamber and bolt face regularly. **A
rimfire suppressor must be taken apart and cleaned**, or lead and carbon will weld it solid
(→ 14-10).

**Shotguns:**

- Gas-operated semi-automatics are the sensitive ones. Keep the piston, gas ports and magazine tube
  clean; some use an O-ring that wears out.
- Pump-action shotguns are very tolerant; keep the action and magazine tube clean and lightly
  oiled.
- **Store with the action closed and springs relaxed** where the design allows, and keep the bore
  oiled. Long, thin barrels rust easily.

**Every firearm:** the printed manual is the authority on how it comes apart and goes back
together. Keep one for each (→ 19-01).

## Fighting rust: the priority when the heat is off

This is the part most people underestimate, especially in a house you are no longer heating
(→ 08-01).

- **Temperature swings make condensation.** A cold firearm brought into a warm room gets water
  on it and in it. Let it warm up **before** opening the case, then wipe it down.
- **A thin film of oil on every outside surface** is the basic protection. Check monthly.
- **Silicone-treated gun socks** and **VCI paper or emitters** (vapour corrosion inhibitor, a
  material that slowly releases a rust-preventing vapour) in storage containers add real
  protection.
- **Desiccant** (moisture-absorbing packets such as silica gel) in any sealed container, dried and
  reused. **Dry it outdoors or in the garage on a pan kept only for this, never in the kitchen
  oven:** desiccant that has lived in a gun case carries lead dust.
- **Do not store firearms in foam-lined cases long-term.** Foam holds moisture against the steel.
- **Watch the bore.** A rusted bore is the one damage you cannot easily undo.

## When the gun oil runs out

Purpose-made gun oil is best. After that, roughly in order of usefulness:

- **Motor oil** (any weight): works well, and you probably have some.
- **Automatic transmission fluid**: thin, spreads well, decent rust protection; a long-standing
  improvised gun oil.
- **Chainsaw bar oil or hydraulic oil**: sticky, good for long storage, poor in cold.
- **Axle or lithium grease**: sparingly, on rails and high-pressure points.
- **Rendered animal fat**: last resort. It can go rancid and attract pests, but it beats running
  dry.
- **Avoid:** water-displacing spray as a *lubricant* (fine for driving off water, poor as oil, and
  it can creep into cartridge primers), vegetable oils (they gum up), and anything that dries hard.

**In deep cold**, heavy oils and greases stiffen. Use **thinner** oil, not more of it.

## When solvent runs out

- **Hot soapy water** works for general fouling. Dry thoroughly and oil at once.
- **Mineral spirits or kerosene** cut carbon well. Store them as fuel (→ 06-06, → 29-01).
- **Do not** use household bleach or ammonia cleaners casually. Ammonia attacks copper and, left in
  place, can damage a bore.
- **Wood ash in hot water** makes a crude degreaser for very heavy fouling. **It is lye: caustic.**
  Gloves and eye protection; rinse well (→ 16-02 has the same warning for tools).

## Lead and solvent: keeping it away from children

Fouling from the bore and action is **lead-rich** (from primers and bullets), and solvents are
petroleum. The CDC states that **no safe blood lead level in children has been identified**,
that children under 6 are at highest risk because they put their hands in their mouths, and that
hobbies can bring lead home on hands, clothes and surfaces (CDC, Childhood Lead Poisoning
Prevention). → 14-08 says the same about reloading and casting. Every time:

- **Clean outdoors, in the garage, or in a room with a window open. Never in the kitchen, never on
  a table anyone eats at.** Lay down newspaper or a mat kept for this; it goes in the bin after.
- **Nitrile gloves on.** Patches, rags and used solvent go into a **lidded metal can** (a coffee
  can works) kept with the cleaning kit, out of children's reach.
- **Wash hands and forearms with soap and water** (hand sanitiser does not remove lead) and
  **change your shirt** before you pick up a child or make food.
- **Suppressor cleaning is the worst of it** (→ 14-10). Rimfire suppressor sludge is mostly lead:
  outdoors, gloves, and the sludge goes in the sealed can.

## A realistic schedule

| When | What |
|---|---|
| **After every use** | Wipe down, check the bore is clear, light oil; clean if fired a lot |
| **After wet, snow, or cold-to-warm** | **At once**: full wipe-down and re-oil |
| **Monthly in storage** | **Unload and check first (above)**, look for rust, wipe, re-oil, work the action |
| **Every few hundred rounds** | Deeper clean of bolt and chamber; check springs and extractor |
| **Once a year** | Full take-down of the main firearms; replace worn springs (→ 14-06) |

## Gear

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Gun oil and grease, **lots** | The real consumable | Buy in bulk now |
| A bore snake for each caliber | Fast, portable, no rod damage | One per caliber |
| Cleaning rods, bore guides, jags and brushes | Proper bore work | Coated rod, correct sizes |
| Carbon scraper (AR-pattern) | The one deposit that matters | Cheap |
| Solvent and mineral spirits | Removes fouling | Store as fuel (→ 06-06) |
| Patches, rags, nitrile gloves | Consumables; stock deep | Old T-shirts work; gloves block lead |
| Lidded metal can for used patches and rags | Keeps lead and solvent sealed | With the kit, out of children's reach |
| **Desiccant and VCI paper or emitters** | Rust is the long-term killer | Re-dry desiccant with heat, outdoors |
| Gun socks or oiled cases (not foam) | Storage protection | Avoid trapped moisture |
| Small brushes, picks, cotton swabs | Detail work | Dental picks are ideal |
| Headspace gauges, per centerfire caliber | Check after any barrel or bolt swap (→ 14-06) | Go/no-go at least |
| **Printed manual for every firearm** | How it comes apart; parts lists | Keep with documents (→ 19-01) |

**If you do not have it:** motor oil and transmission fluid are in every garage and vehicle. Any
cloth makes rags. A shoelace with a small weight makes a crude bore snake.

## When it runs out

- **Oil is the one to hoard.** It protects everything, and a rusted firearm is a permanent loss.
- **Protection first, cleanliness second.** If you have one ounce of oil left, it goes on outside
  surfaces and in the bore, not into a deep clean.
- **Firearms fail from neglect and tired springs, not from use.** A collection wiped down monthly
  and stored dry will outlast the crisis; one left in a damp basement for a year may not.

## By phase

- **A (at home):** monthly check; watch an unheated house for condensation.
- **B (on the move):** a small kit (oil, a bore snake, a rag, a multi-tool); keep firearms out of
  repeated cold-to-warm swings where you can.
- **C (rural or a new place):** damp outbuildings are hard on steel. Keep working firearms in the
  heated space with you, locked (→ 14-14).
