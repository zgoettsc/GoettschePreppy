# Situational awareness and honest threat assessment

**What this page is for.** Security in a long emergency is mostly about noticing things early
and not walking into trouble. This page ranks the dangers a household really faces, shows how to
build a habit of noticing what is normal and what is not, and sets up a few family rules. It
does not teach fighting.

> **CAUTION.** The realistic threats when the power and services fail are **not** roving gangs.
> They are, in order: **fire, carbon monoxide, cold, medical failure, illness, accidents, and
> desperate ordinary people.** Planning for the dramatic scenario while ignoring the ordinary ones
> is the classic mistake, and it uses up attention you need elsewhere.

## Do this now

1. **Rank your real threats** with the table below and spend effort in that order.
2. **Know your block**: who lives where, who is elderly or alone, who has skills, who has medical
   needs. That knowledge is both security and community (→ 18-01).
3. **Walk and look twice a day**: around the property and down the street, morning and evening.
4. **Set a family duress word and rally points** (→ 15-03).
5. **Keep information flowing**: a weather radio and talking to neighbours tell you more than any
   weapon (→ 15-01, → 15-04).

## The honest threat ranking

| Rank | Threat | Why it is higher than you think |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Fire** | No 911, wood heat, candles, children. A house fire ends everything at once (→ 29-12) |
| 2 | **Carbon monoxide** | Silent, and the temptation to bring heaters and engines inside grows as it gets colder (→ 08-02, → 29-03) |
| 3 | **Cold and hypothermia** | Worst for {{#if has.infant}}your infant, {{/if}}{{#if has.elder}}your elders, {{/if}}small children, older people and anyone sick (→ 08-05) |
| 4 | **Medical failure** | A medicine or device someone depends on runs out; an allergic reaction or injury with no emergency room (→ 12-11) |
| 5 | **Illness and dirty water** | Diarrhoeal disease spreads fast, especially with small children (→ 03-04) |
| 6 | **Accidents** | Axes, saws, ladders, boiling water, with no ambulance |
| 7 | **Theft** | Real, but usually of property, not violence against people |
| 8 | **Desperate people asking for help** | Common; a social problem before it is a security one (→ 14-03) |
| 9 | **Organised violence** | Real, but rare, and it comes late if at all |

**Spend your preparation in that order.** A carbon monoxide alarm, a fire extinguisher and a good
first-aid kit protect against more likely deaths than any firearm. Outage guidance puts
alarms, safe lighting and safe heat first for the same reason (American Red Cross, Power Outage
Safety).

## Awareness is a habit, not a state of alarm

- **Learn normal first.** You cannot notice something odd without knowing what normal is. What
  does your street sound like at 9 p.m.? Who walks a dog at 7 a.m.? Once the grid is down,
  normal changes: learn it again.
- **Things worth noticing:** vehicles that circle or park oddly; people looking at houses rather
  than walking past; sudden silence; dogs reacting; smoke where there should be none; a
  neighbour's routine breaking.
- **Trust the feeling, then check it.** Unease is often your brain spotting a pattern before you
  can name it. Do not dismiss it, and do not act on it blindly either.
- **Do not stare.** Noticing is not confronting. Note it, step back, talk it over.

## Three questions before you act

When something worries you, answer these first:

1. **What is actually happening?** What you saw, not what you guessed.
2. **What is the realistic worst case?** Not the imagined one.
3. **What is the least escalating response that works?** Almost always: step back, turn on a
   light, be seen, call a neighbour, lock a door. Not a weapon.

**Escalation is a one-way door.** The most important security skill is not winning a
confrontation; it is **not being in one** (→ 14-13). The household's written rule on force is
on → 14-11: {{choice DC-12}} → 30-13.

## Keeping watch: the realistic version

Films show a 24-hour armed watch. A household of {{ household.adults }} adults{{#if has.child}}
with children{{/if}} cannot keep that up and should not try: **lack of sleep will hurt you long
before an intruder does.**

- **Rely on locks, hardening and alarms** (→ 14-02, → 14-12) so that you can sleep.
- **Use layered early warning**: a dog, gravel under windows, a bell on a gate, motion lights.
- **Cooperate with neighbours.** A block that watches informally beats one exhausted household
  (→ 18-01).
- **If a real threat period arrives**, take turns on watch, but treat it as a short surge, not a
  permanent way of living.

## Information is the real awareness tool

- **NOAA Weather Radio** (→ 15-01): weather is the threat most likely to reach every household.
- **Scanner and radio traffic** (→ 15-04): what is actually happening in town.
- **Neighbours**: the best local information you will get.
- **Write down what you learn**, with the date, on paper. Rumour and fact separate quickly when
  they are written down.

## Family rules

- **Duress word:** an ordinary-sounding word that means "something is wrong, act now":
  __________ (keep it off any list a stranger could read; → 15-03).
- **Rally point if separated at home:** {{ contacts.meet.near_home }}. **Away from home:**
  {{ contacts.meet.in_town }} (→ 15-03).
- **Answering the door:** who does it, who does not, what is said: __________________________
- **Children's rules:** where to go, who to open the door for (nobody), what to say (nothing).
- **Practise them.** A rule nobody has rehearsed is a rule you do not have (→ 24-02).

## Gear

**Worth having now:**

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| NOAA weather radio | Weather is the most likely threat | → 15-01 |
| Scanner or handheld radio | Local information | → 15-04 |
| Motion-activated solar lights | Early warning with no wiring | Cheap, effective |
| Driveway or gate alarm, or a bell | Lets you sleep | A simple mechanical one works |
| Binoculars | Look without approaching | Also useful for weather and game |
| A written block map and neighbour list | Who is who, who needs help | → 18-01 |
| Notebook and pencil | Log what you see, dated | Separates rumour from fact |
| Fire extinguishers, smoke and CO alarms | The actual top threats | → 08-02, → 29-12 |

**If you do not have it:**

- **Gravel** under windows and along paths is a cheap, silent, reliable alarm.
- Tin cans on a string across a gate; a bell on a door.
- A dog is the best early-warning system there is (→ 14-12).

## When it runs out

- **Attention is the thing that runs out.** Constant high alert wears people out in days and
  damages judgement (→ 21-01).
- **Automate what you can** (alarms, lights, layout) so people only watch where they must. Sleep
  is a security measure.
- **Share the load with neighbours.** A shared watch is the only way to cover 24 hours for long.
- **The real failure:** exhaustion and fear leading to a bad decision, such as a confrontation
  that did not need to happen, or a missed CO alarm because everyone was watching the street.

## By phase

- **A (at home{{#if is.urban}}, in town{{/if}}):** neighbours close by mean more risk and more
  early warning. Invest in knowing your block.
- **B (on the move):** avoid crowds, narrow choke points and darkness; do not look worth stopping
  (→ 14-03).
- **C (rural or a new place):** fewer people, longer views, longer waits for help. Roads and
  approaches matter more; neighbours are farther away and count for more.
