# Defensive use of force: writing the household rule

**What this page is for.** Any household that keeps a means of defence, firearm or not, needs a
rule, written in calm advance, about when force may be used against another person. In the
world this binder is for there may be no court, no police and no lawyer, and **that is exactly
why the rule has to be written now, by the adults, in their own words.** This page gives the
facts and the questions. The worksheet itself is on → 30-13.

> **SAFETY.** Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products.
> Fixed in every source (floor, → 30-01). A shot fired is the one decision in this binder that
> cannot be taken back: there may be no surgeon for the person hit, none for a neighbour's child
> behind the wall, and no way to stop being "the house that shoots" afterwards. **Identify before
> anything else**, and know what is beyond your target (NSSF, Firearm safety). This page is not
> legal advice and teaches no tactics.

**Our rule:** {{choice DC-12}} → 30-13

## Do this now

1. **Read this page and → 30-13 together, every adult.** Write the rule on the worksheet, sign
   it, date it.
2. **Say it out loud** to each other, and rehearse it, including how you identify someone in the
   dark (→ 14-01).
3. **Decide what happens when only property is at stake** (the generator, fuel, food, the car).
   That is the question households most often get wrong under stress.
4. **Make avoiding the confrontation the first step of the plan.** Most encounters end at a locked
   door and a calm voice (→ 14-13).
5. **Know which way a shot would go** inside your house, toward the fewest people.
6. **Keep a good flashlight** where the rule might be used. You cannot rightly use force on
   someone you have not identified.

## The four tests

Across traditions of lawful self-defence the test has four parts. They are worth keeping even
when nobody enforces them.

- **Imminence:** the threat is happening now; not feared, threatened earlier, or over.
- **Necessity:** nothing less would stop it (a locked door, a light, a warning, leaving).
- **Proportionality:** the force matches the threat. **Deadly force** (force likely to kill or
  seriously injure, which includes firing a gun at someone) answers only a threat of death or
  serious injury **to a person**.
- **Reasonableness:** what a calm adult would believe in the same place, not what adrenaline
  believes at 3 a.m.

## Why these tests still matter with nobody enforcing them

- **A shot cannot be undone, and the neighbourhood is permanent.** After the first weeks, the
  block may be the only society you have (→ 18-01). A household that shoots someone over a fuel
  can becomes the household the block fears and turns against. A household that challenged,
  lit up and let a thief run is still part of the block the next morning.
- **There may be no trauma surgeon.** Any gunshot wound, to the other person or to you if it goes
  wrong, may be fatal. Bleeding control buys time, not a cure (→ 12-02).
- **Property can be replaced; the children watching cannot.** What children see at the door stays
  with them (CDC, Helping Children Cope During and After a Disaster; → 21-03).
- **Bullets go through walls.** Bullets can penetrate walls and ceilings (NSSF, Firearm safety),
  so ordinary rifle, shotgun and handgun ammunition can reach other rooms and other houses.

| What changes when services are gone | What does not change |
|---|---|
| Nobody is coming; you are the response | The four tests |
| Nobody will investigate | A bullet still goes through the wall into a family |
| Nobody will punish a bad shot | The neighbours remember, and judge, for years |
| Thieves grow bolder as hunger grows | Property is still not a life |

**Act as if you will have to explain it to the block the next morning, because you will.**

## Questions your rule must answer

The worksheet on → 30-13 asks these. Think them through here first.

- **Property only.** Someone is carrying off the generator and threatens nobody. What do you do?
- **The yard and outbuildings.** Is a person in the yard, the garage, the shed or at the fence
  treated the same as a person inside the house? Many households decide that someone outside is
  challenged, lit up and watched, not shot, unless they are an immediate threat to a person; a
  person retreating with your property is moving away, not toward you. Write your own answer.
- **Inside the house.** How does the household identify someone before anyone acts? A light and
  a challenge word the family knows (→ 15-03). Family members and neighbours coming in at night
  are the people most often shot by mistake.
- **Who.** Which adults may use a firearm in defence, and are they trained on that firearm
  (→ 14-14)?
- **The order of steps.** Lock, light, voice, leave, pepper spray, other. Pepper spray stops many
  people, though not all (National Institute of Justice, The Effectiveness and Safety of Pepper
  Spray).

## Backstops: the problem nobody talks about

{{#if is.urban}}Your home is in a town or suburb, with houses close on every side, so this applies
to you directly.{{else}}Even on rural land, a house has people in other rooms and a road or
neighbour somewhere downrange.{{/if}}

- **Common rifle, shotgun and handgun ammunition can go through several walls.**
- **Know what is beyond your target**, inside the house and outside it. In the dark, in a
  hallway, with adrenaline, this is truly hard.
- **This argues for:** knowing your home's layout cold; knowing the directions where a shot would
  go toward the fewest people; and considering ammunition choice with over-penetration in mind.
- **It argues even more strongly for avoiding the confrontation** (→ 14-13).

Firing at home for any other reason (pests, practice) is a separate decision: {{choice DC-11}}
→ 30-12.

## Firearms on the road

A safe way to carry firearms in a vehicle, whatever the law: **unloaded, cased and locked, with
ammunition stored separately, in the cabin, never in a trailer** (→ 14-03, → 22-05). That keeps
them from children, survives a crash without a loaded gun loose in the vehicle, and cannot be
grabbed from an unattended trailer.

> **Before the collapse:** your state's law, and the law of every state you cross, apply while
> they are enforced. Carry permits are not honoured everywhere, and some places ban firearms
> entirely (for example courthouses, schools and their grounds, federal buildings, and private
> property that posts it). Federal law protects travel through a state where you could not
> otherwise have the firearm only if you may lawfully have it where you start and where you end,
> and the firearm is unloaded with neither it nor ammunition readily reachable from the passenger
> compartment; in a vehicle with no separate trunk, it must be in a locked container other than
> the glove compartment or console (18 U.S.C. § 926A). Suppressors and other NFA items have their own rules (→ 14-10;
> ATF, National Firearms Act interstate transportation). Look up each state on your route while
> the information is published.

## Before the collapse: what the law says, in general terms

> **Before the collapse:** {{ home.state_name }} has its own self-defence laws, and they differ
> from state to state. This binder does not summarise them. In general terms, US states build on
> the same ideas as the four tests above, but differ on several points:
>
> - **Imminent threat and proportionality.** Deadly force is generally lawful only against a
>   threat of death or serious bodily harm (and in some states certain violent felonies) that is
>   happening now.
> - **Duty to retreat or stand your ground.** Some states require you to retreat first if you
>   safely can before using deadly force; others ("stand your ground") do not where you have a
>   right to be.
> - **Castle doctrine.** Most states give extra protection, or remove any duty to retreat, inside
>   your own home. What counts as the home (the yard, a garage, an outbuilding, a vehicle) varies.
> - **Property.** Deadly force to protect property alone is generally not lawful.
>
> Read your state's law while it is available, and write the section numbers on → 30-13. If you
> ever use force while the law still runs: report it at once, point out evidence and witnesses,
> say little, and ask for a lawyer before giving a statement.

## If it ever happens: afterwards

Almost no preparedness material covers this, and it matters more, not less, when there is nobody
to call.

1. **Stop the threat, then stop.** Firing after a threat has ended is a different act, and the
   neighbours will know the difference.
2. **Make the scene safe.** Firearms unloaded and secured, the scene lit, children kept away.
3. **Give first aid** to anyone hurt, including the other person if it is safe (→ 12-02). It
   matters morally, and it is what the neighbours will remember you did.
4. **Tell the block yourself, first, plainly**: who, what happened, what you did after. A shooting
   kept quiet becomes a rumour, and in a hungry neighbourhood rumours become feuds (→ 18-01).
5. **Expect a severe emotional aftermath**, for you, the other adults, and any child who saw or
   heard (→ 21-03). It does not resolve on its own.

## The honest strategic view

Weapons are the **last** layer of a security plan, not the first. Locks, lights, awareness, not
standing out, and good neighbours prevent far more harm than any weapon resolves (→ 14-12). The
household's realistic dangers are fire, carbon monoxide, cold and medical failure (→ 14-01). Keep
the proportion right, including in how much money and attention go here.

**If you do not have it:** the rule is the constraint, not equipment. The substitute for
everything on this page is avoidance (→ 14-13). A flashlight is the one thing you cannot do
without.

## When it runs out

Discipline fades as weeks of fear pile up. **Re-read the rule aloud** with the other adults every
season and after any scare (→ 25-10). The cheapest, most effective defence you have is the
decision not to be in the situation.

## By phase

- **A (at home):** neighbours, backstops and identification dominate{{#if is.urban}}; houses close
  together make this the hardest place to use a firearm safely{{/if}}.
- **B (on the move):** the vehicle rule above; do not display firearms (→ 14-03).
- **C (rural or a new place):** unfamiliar land, unknown neighbours, longer views, and the same
  household rule. Re-check every assumption on this page against the new place in the first week.
