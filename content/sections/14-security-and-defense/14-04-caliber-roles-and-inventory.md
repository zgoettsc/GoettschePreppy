# Caliber roles and the household firearms inventory

**What this page is for.** This page is logistics, not shooting. It answers two questions before
a crisis: **which gun does which job**, and **which ammunition is worth stocking deeply**. It ends
with an inventory log the household fills in by hand.

{{#if has.firearms}}Your profile says there are firearms in the home. Fill in the log at the
bottom and keep it current.{{else}}Your profile lists no firearms. This page stays in your binder
because firearms can be inherited, found or acquired in a long emergency, and someone has to know
what ammunition fits what. If that happens, start with → 29-11 and → 14-14, then use the log
below.{{/if}}

> **CAUTION.** Never fire or load ammunition you have not matched to the marking on the gun. The
> caliber is stamped on the barrel; the headstamp on the base of the cartridge must match it
> (SAAMI, Generally Accepted Firearms and Ammunition Interchangeability). A cartridge that fits
> is not proof it is safe. New to firearms? Read → 29-11 first.

## Do this now

1. **Fill in the inventory log** below and keep it current. A count you have not written down is
   a guess.
2. **Stock by job, not evenly.** The calibers that do the most work deserve the most depth.
3. **If you own a .22 LR, stock it deeper than feels reasonable.** It is cheap and light, it feeds
   small game for years, and **it cannot be reloaded** (→ 14-08): what you have is what you get.
4. **Standardise where you can.** Fewer calibers means simpler supply, simpler spare parts and
   fewer ways to be short of the wrong thing.
5. **Count magazines like ammunition.** They wear out and get lost (→ 14-06).

## Words on this page

- **Caliber** is the size and type of cartridge a rifle or handgun is made for (".22 LR", "9mm").
  **Gauge** is the shotgun equivalent ("12 gauge"; a smaller number is a bigger bore). **Gauges
  are never interchangeable:** a 20-gauge shell dropped into a 12-gauge gun can slide forward and
  stick in the barrel, and the next 12-gauge shell fired behind it bursts the barrel (SAAMI,
  Generally Accepted Firearms and Ammunition Interchangeability). Keep each gauge in its own
  marked box.
- **Rimfire** cartridges (like .22 LR) have the priming compound in the rim; they cannot be
  reloaded. **Centerfire** cartridges have a replaceable primer in the centre of the base.
- **Platform** means a family of firearms that share parts, such as "AR-pattern rifle".

More words: → 29-11.

## Common calibers and their jobs

This table covers the calibers most common in US homes. It is general; your own firearms may
differ. Hunting methods, legal calibers and seasons are set by your state's wildlife agency and
change every year (→ 05-07).

| Caliber or gauge | Usual main job | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **.22 LR** (rimfire) | Small game, pests, training | Cheap, light, many rounds per pound. Cannot be reloaded |
| **9mm** | Handgun, defence | The most common centerfire handgun round in the US; best odds of finding more |
| **.45 ACP** | Handgun, defence | Heavier and less common than 9mm |
| **5.56 / .223** | Rifle: defence, varmints, general use | Very common. Read the barrel marking: SAAMI treats them as different cartridges, and the gun's maker decides what is safe |
| **.308** | Rifle: deer and larger game, longer range | Common hunting round; heavier |
| **Other hunting rifle calibers** (.30-06, .270, 6.5 Creedmoor and many more) | Rifle: deer and larger game | How easy each is to find varies a lot by caliber and region. Treat a less common one as finite |
| **12 gauge** | Shotgun: birds, small game, deer (slug), defence | The most versatile single firearm: birdshot, buckshot or slug. Very common |
| **20 gauge** | Shotgun: birds, small game | Less recoil; easier for smaller or newer shooters. Less common than 12 |

**When ammunition becomes scarce, what you can find follows what is common.** 9mm, 5.56/.223,
.22 LR and 12 gauge are the ones most likely to turn up. Two consequences:

- **Stock uncommon calibers now.** What you buy today may be your lifetime supply.
- **Common calibers have trade value, and that is the problem** (below).

## A hard note on ammunition as barter

Ammunition is very valuable in a crisis, which is exactly why trading it is dangerous. It arms
whoever you trade with, possibly against you, and it tells them you have more. If you trade it at
all, trade only with people you would trust armed, and never in a caliber that feeds your own
main firearms (→ 18-02, → 14-08).

## The case for standardising

Many calibers is a supply burden. You cannot carry them all, stock them all deeply, or keep the
cleaning gear, spare parts and know-how for each. Consider naming:

- **One main rifle caliber**
- **One main handgun caliber**
- **One small-game firearm** (often .22 LR)
- **One hunting rifle caliber**
- **One shotgun gauge**

Everything else becomes secondary: kept and maintained, but not what the plan depends on. Write
the decision in the log so the go-bag and vehicle load (→ 01-03) are not improvised.

## Matching firearms to game

→ 05-07 covers hunting. This only maps firearms to game in general terms.

- **Small game and pests:** .22 LR is the usual choice.
- **Upland birds:** 12 or 20 gauge with birdshot.
- **Waterfowl:** shotgun with **non-toxic shot** (steel, bismuth and others).
- **Deer:** a deer-legal rifle caliber, or a shotgun slug.

> **Before the collapse:** your state's wildlife agency sets legal methods, calibers, seasons and
> licences for hunting, and some calibers are not legal for deer in many states. Federal rules
> require non-toxic shot for waterfowl. Get the current regulations each year while they are
> published, and keep a copy in the binder kit.

## Gear

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Deep stock of your small-game caliber | Most use per pound and dollar | Sealed containers (→ 14-07) |
| Extra depth in your least-common calibers | Hardest to find later | What you buy is what you will have |
| Non-toxic shot, if you hunt waterfowl | Required for waterfowl | Steel or bismuth |
| Slugs and buckshot, if you have a shotgun | Deer and defence from one gun | Check where they hit (→ 14-09) |
| Magazines, several per firearm | The most common failure point | Treat as consumable (→ 14-06) |
| The written inventory log | Planning needs numbers | Copy with documents (→ 19-01) |
| Current state hunting regulations, printed | Rules change every year | Print each year |

**If you do not have it:** common calibers are the substitute. Firearms in 9mm, 5.56/.223,
.22 LR and 12 gauge are more useful in a long emergency than unusual ones, because their
ammunition is what you will actually find.

## When it runs out

- **How long it lasts:** rounds on hand divided by realistic use per year, for each job. Hunting
  use is countable. **Practice is what actually drains a stockpile**, so budget it on purpose.
- **Stretch it:** practise with .22 LR instead of centerfire where you can: the same basics for a
  fraction of the cost, and it saves the ammunition that matters.
- **Resupply:** → 14-08, on reloading, commonality, and what can and cannot be made.
- **Dry practice** (an unloaded firearm, no ammunition in the room) keeps most skills alive at no
  cost in ammunition (→ 14-15).

## By phase

- **A (at home):** inventory and standardising, done now.
- **B (on the move):** you cannot carry it all. Decide in advance which firearms and calibers
  travel; ammunition is heavy (→ 01-03). Firearms ride in the cabin, locked (→ 14-03, → 14-14).
- **C (rural or a new place):** on most days small game and deer matter more than defence.
