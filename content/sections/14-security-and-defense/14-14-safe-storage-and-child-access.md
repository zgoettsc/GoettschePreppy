# Safe storage and keeping firearms from children

**What this page is for.** How to store firearms and ammunition so that no child, and no
unauthorised person, can get to them, while adults can still reach what they have chosen to keep
ready. It is in every binder: children visit homes with firearms, and firearms can come into any
household. New to firearms? Read → 29-11 first.

> **SAFETY.** **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products.**
> Fixed in every source (floor, → 30-01). In practice: **every firearm is locked, or on an
> adult's body. There is no third state.** Children find hiding places; they do not defeat good
> locks. Storage that depends on a child not finding something, not reaching something or not
> working something out is not storage (American Academy of Pediatrics, Guns in the Home).
> {{#if has.child}}**There are children in your household.** This page is not optional.{{/if}}

## Do this now

1. **Tonight: where is every firearm, right now?** Anything not in a locked container gets locked
   tonight, with a cable lock if nothing else (NSSF Project ChildSafe).
2. **The safest storage is unloaded, locked, with ammunition locked separately** (AAP, Guns in
   the Home). Start there.
3. **If the household keeps one firearm ready for defence**, it lives in a **quick-access
   lockbox** (keypad or fingerprint), fixed down, out of sight and reach. A loaded firearm in a
   nightstand drawer is never a plan.
4. **Lock ammunition separately** from firearms (→ 14-07).
5. **Write combinations and codes by hand**, on paper kept sealed and away from children and
   visitors. **Never put a safe combination, a lockbox code, a serial number or where firearms
   are kept in this binder or in the settings page.** The settings page never asks for them.
6. **Teach children the stop rule** (below) now, whatever their age (→ 20-04).

## Layer 1: every firearm locked

- **A safe or locked cabinet is the default home** for every firearm not deliberately kept ready:
  unloaded, as on → 14-07.
- **A cable lock through the action** (the moving parts), or a trigger lock, makes any firearm
  "locked" tonight while you buy better hardware. Many police departments hand out free cable
  locks through Project ChildSafe (NSSF).
- **A firearm kept ready for defence is a trade-off.** The safest storage is unloaded and locked
  with ammunition apart; a household that keeps one firearm ready accepts more risk for speed.
  If you do, the only form that still meets the floor is a **quick-access lockbox**: keypad or
  fingerprint, with any backup key kept where children cannot find it, **bolted or cabled down**
  so it cannot be carried off, out of sight and out of reach. Practise opening it in the dark
  (→ 14-15); a lockbox you fumble is a lockbox you will be tempted to leave open. **Never leave
  it open.**
- **On an adult's body counts as secured.** Loose on a counter "for a minute" never does.
- **Test fingerprint and keypad boxes monthly** and change batteries on a schedule. Tens of
  thousands of biometric (fingerprint) gun safes have been recalled because people who had not
  enrolled a fingerprint could open them, including a child; CPSC links one recalled model to a
  child's death (CPSC, Fortress Safe recall of biometric gun safes, 2023). Check any lockbox you
  own against CPSC's recall list while it exists, make sure an unenrolled finger does not open
  it, and test it against what a child would try: shaking, prying, pressing buttons at random.

## Layer 2: ammunition

- **Ammunition is locked separately** from firearms, in a different container or a locked
  compartment (→ 14-07). A child who gets past one barrier still faces a second.
- The only exception is a firearm the household chose to keep ready, with its ammunition, inside
  its locked quick-access box.
- **Loose cartridges are treasure to a small child.** Range-bag leftovers, coat pockets, the car
  console (→ 22-05): sweep them, and every stray round goes back into locked storage.

## Layer 3: what is true about children

- **Children defeat hiding places, not locks.** They find the top shelf, the sock drawer, the box
  in the garage. Even toddlers can find an unlocked gun and are strong enough to pull the
  trigger (AAP, Guns in the Home). **Hiding is not a layer. Height is not a layer. "They don't
  know about it" is not a layer.** Locks are, and children must not be able to get the keys or
  combinations either (AAP).
- **"They would never touch it" is not a layer either.** AAP notes that many studies show
  teaching children about gun safety, or not to touch a gun they find, is not enough. Teaching
  reduces risk; only locks remove access.
- **Other people's homes count.** Asking "Is there an unlocked gun where my child will play?" is a
  normal safety question, like asking about a pool fence or a food allergy (AAP). Ask it.

## Layer 4: teaching that grows with the child

Storage stays absolute; **teaching grows with the child** (→ 20-04): simple, absolute rules,
repeated, not fear.

- **Every child, as soon as they can follow a rule:** **"Stop. Don't touch. Leave the area. Tell
  a grown-up."** AAP's version: if they ever come across a gun, stay away from it and tell an
  adult at once (AAP, Guns in the Home). Repeat it until it is automatic, including for a gun
  found at a friend's house.
- **After that**, the household decides when each child learns the four rules (→ 29-11), when
  they may look at an unloaded, checked firearm with an adult, and when, if ever, they handle or
  shoot one under supervision. Answering questions honestly takes the mystery out: a firearm a
  child has seen safely with a parent is boring machinery with rules; one only glimpsed is a
  magnet.
- **Storage never relaxes as children get older**, because their friends are every age at once.

## Layer 5: visitors and other children

- **When children visit, the house goes fully locked**: any lockbox locked (it always is), nothing
  carried loose, range gear put away. Other people's children have no training you can count on,
  and yours may show off what they know.
- **Visiting adults' firearms** follow the house rules: locked in the safe or a lockbox, or left in
  their vehicle, unloaded and locked. Awkward for ten seconds; say it anyway.
- **Babysitters and house-sitters** get the same answer: everything is locked, and codes are not
  written anywhere a visitor could find them.
- **If you hand a firearm to another adult** (for example someone minding the house): show it to
  them in person, unloaded, beforehand; let them decide whether to take it at all; it stays locked
  until they do; **a holster goes on their belt first and the firearm goes into the holster already
  on their body**, so hands never pass in front of the muzzle; then the safe is closed and locked.
  On their body or locked are the only two states while they are there.

## The tension between readiness and child safety

Readiness and child safety pull against each other. Pretending otherwise produces either an
unlocked nightstand or a firearm nobody can reach. The honest resolution:

- **Locked is not negotiable.** A quick-access lockbox is seconds slower than a drawer and a
  lifetime safer. The drawer, the closet shelf, "unloaded but handy", the magnet under the bed:
  all fail the day a child gets curious.
- **Speed comes from practice**, not from leaving things unlocked (→ 14-15).
- **The rest of the plan buys the seconds back.** Warning layers (→ 14-12) exist so you are never
  surprised so badly that a lockbox is too slow.

**If you do not have it:** a cable lock through the action costs little or nothing and makes any

## When it runs out

- Locks do not run out; **batteries and vigilance do.** Lockbox batteries go on the battery count
  (→ 23-02); check the habit monthly.
- **This system is never relaxed to save resources.**
- Key and dial backups outlast electronics; cable locks are the fallback everywhere.
- **In a long crisis, storage gets harder:** firearms are handled more, adults are tired, children
  are home all day. These rules are written for exactly that week.

## By phase

- **A (at home):** everything above.
- **B (on the move):** firearms travel unloaded, locked, in the cabin, never in a trailer, with
  ammunition apart (→ 14-03, → 22-05). This is also the child-safe arrangement. At stops, nothing
  loose in the cabin.
- **C (a new place):** a new house has no storage. **Setting up Layer 1 is a first-day task**:
  locked cases, cable locks and a lockable closet stand in until something better exists.
