# Zeroing, optics and battery management

**What this page is for.** "Zeroing" means adjusting a sight so the bullet hits where you aim at
a chosen distance. This page covers choosing and recording a zero, keeping optics (scopes and red
dots) working when batteries are scarce, backup iron sights, and confirming a zero without using
much ammunition. New to firearms? Read → 29-11 first.

> **CAUTION.** Zeroing means live fire, and the four safety rules apply to every shot (→ 29-11):
> above all, know your target and what is beyond it. **Where** the household fires is a decision
> card: {{choice DC-11}} → 30-12. An optic is a **battery-powered part** on a mechanical tool. In
> a long emergency, two things end your ability to hit anything: **dead batteries** and **a zero
> you cannot check because ammunition is too precious**. Both are solved in advance, and almost
> nobody does it.

## Do this now

1. **Confirm and write down the zero of every rifle now**, while ammunition can still be bought.
   Put it on a card kept with the rifle.
2. **Know every optic's battery type** and stock **many** spares; count them like ammunition.
3. **Fit backup iron sights** to any rifle you depend on, and zero those too.
4. **If you shoot with a suppressor, zero with it on** (→ 14-10): the bullet's impact shifts.
5. **Tighten optic mounts to the maker's torque** and mark the screws so you can see if they move
   (→ 14-06).

## Choosing a zero

The zero you choose decides how much thinking you do under pressure. Common conventions:

| Firearm | Common approach | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **5.56 / .223 carbine** | **50/200-yard zero** | Little aiming adjustment needed across ordinary distances |
| **.308 and other deer rifles** | **100-yard zero** | A precise baseline to hold or adjust from |
| **.22 LR** | **50-yard zero** | Matches realistic small-game distances; drops fast beyond |
| **Shotgun with slugs** | **50-yard zero** | Practical slug distance |
| **Shotgun with shot** | **Pattern, do not zero** | Shoot each load at paper at your expected distance and look at the spread |

**Whatever you choose, write it down**, with the aiming adjustment ("hold") at the distances you
would really shoot. A zero you cannot describe is a zero you do not really have.

## The data card: the most valuable five minutes

Make one per rifle, protect it with a laminating pouch or clear tape, and keep it on the stock or
in the case. (Hunters call it a "dope card".)

{{figure fig-dope-card}}

```
RIFLE: ______________  CALIBER: ______  ZERO: ______ yd
LOAD:  ______________________  (bullet weight / brand / lot)
OPTIC: ______________  MOUNT TORQUE: ______ in-lb  BATTERY: ______
  50 yd  hold: ______      200 yd hold: ______
 100 yd  hold: ______      300 yd hold: ______
 SUPPRESSED shift: ______  IRONS zeroed: Y / N
 Zero confirmed (date): ______
```

**The ammunition matters.** Different loads, and even different lots of the same load, hit in
different places. If you stocked one hunting load, **zero for that load** and note it on the
card. Change loads and you have a new zero.

## Batteries: the dependency nobody plans for

- **List every optic's battery type** (CR2032, CR123A, AA, AAA) and standardise where you can.
- **Stock deeply.** Lithium primary (non-rechargeable) cells keep for years in storage and work
  in cold far better than alkaline (→ 29-02).
- **Cold cuts battery life.** A red dot that runs a year at room temperature runs noticeably less
  in sustained cold. Keep spares warm, in an inside pocket.
- **Alkaline batteries leak** and can destroy an optic left in storage. For anything stored with
  a battery in it, **use lithium** or take the battery out.
- **Know the run time.** Many red dots run a very long time; some magnified and lit scopes run far
  less. Turn them off, or use a motion-activated setting.
- **A magnified scope with an etched reticle still works with a dead battery**; only the
  illumination goes. **A red dot usually does not work at all.** That should shape what goes on
  the rifle you depend on most.

## Backup iron sights

- **Line them up with the red dot** ("co-witness") so switching is natural.
- **Zero the irons separately** and write it down. Iron sights never zeroed are decoration.
- **Practise using them**, including flipping up folding sights quickly.
- A rifle with iron sights never has a dead battery.

## Mounting and torque

- **Use a torque wrench that reads in inch-pounds** (→ 14-06). Too tight crushes scope tubes; too
  loose lets the optic shift, which shows up as a zero that wanders for no clear reason.
- **Mark the screws** with a paint line after tightening. One glance shows if anything moved.
- **Thread locker** only as the maker says.
- **Check the zero again after** any mount work, a hard drop, a long trip, or a barrel or bolt
  change.

## Zeroing without using much ammunition

- **Bore sight first** (look through the barrel of a bolt-action rifle, or use a laser or optical
  bore sighter) to get on paper in one or two shots instead of ten.
- **Shoot small groups from a steady rest:** three rounds, adjust, confirm. A careful process uses
  only a handful of rounds per rifle.
- **Confirm, do not redo.** Once zeroed, a periodic single-shot check at a known distance tells
  you what you need.
- **Dry practice** (→ 14-15) covers everything except recoil, and uses no ammunition.

## Optics care in the field

- **Lens covers on** when moving; grit is what scratches glass.
- **Do not wipe a dry lens.** Blow grit off first, then use a lens cloth.
- **Fogging:** a cold optic brought into a warm room fogs, like the condensation problem on
  → 14-05. Let it warm up before wiping.
- **Heat shimmer** ("mirage") rises off a hot suppressor and blurs the view (→ 14-10). Let it cool.
- **Store optics dry**, with alkaline batteries removed for long storage.

## Gear

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Lithium batteries, deep stock, for each type** | The real optic dependency | Long shelf life; good in cold |
| Backup iron sights on rifles you depend on | Works with no battery | Zero them separately |
| **Inch-pound torque wrench** | Mounts that hold zero | Also → 14-06 |
| Bore sighter (laser or optical) | Zero with very little ammunition | Cheap; saves rounds |
| Data cards and laminating pouches | The numbers, on the rifle | Or clear tape |
| Lens pen, cloth, covers | Glass care | Blow off grit first |
| Rest, bipod or sandbag | A zero is only as good as what the rifle rests on | A bag of rice works |
| A spare optic, even a modest one | Optics fail | Already mounted if possible |
| Paper targets and a marker | Confirming zero | Print a stack now |

**If you do not have it:** iron sights replace everything electronic, which is the argument for
keeping at least one rifle with good irons. Cardboard and paint make targets; a rolled sleeping
bag makes a rest. CR123A and CR2032 cells turn up in cameras, sensors and other devices.

## When it runs out

- **Batteries, in hours of run time per optic.** Write the number in the log.
- **Stretch them:** turn optics **off** when not in use; use motion-activated settings; use iron
  sights for routine carry and save battery life for when it matters.
- **The lasting answer:** an etched-reticle scope or good iron sights never depend on a battery.
  Over several years, that is worth more than any electronic advantage.

## By phase

- **A (at home):** zero everything, make the data cards, stock batteries. Cheap and easy now.
- **B (on the move):** travel can shift a zero; confirm on arrival if you can spare a round and a
  safe place to fire it.
- **C (rural or a new place):** longer shots at game matter more; a confirmed hunting zero and a
  data card earn their keep (→ 05-07). In sustained cold, lithium batteries are clearly right.
