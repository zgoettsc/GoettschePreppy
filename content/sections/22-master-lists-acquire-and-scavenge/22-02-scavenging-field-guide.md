# Scavenging field guide: what to look for

**What this page is for.** Knowing what is valuable before you need it, so that the things you
already own, are given, trade for, or find genuinely abandoned do not get overlooked. "Salvage"
means recovering useful material from things that are broken, discarded or abandoned. It also
carries real hazards (lead, mould, spoiled food, sharp steel, unstable buildings, contaminated
containers), and the second half of this page is about not being hurt by them.

> **CAUTION.** **Taking property that is not yours is theft**, in any scenario, and this page
> does not say otherwise. The most dangerous part of scavenging is not other people: it is a
> preventable injury with no doctor available.

## Do this now

1. **Learn the six categories below.** They are the things that get used up constantly and stop
   being made: **containers, cloth, cordage, fasteners, fuel, blades.**
2. **Start with your own house.** Most households already own hundreds of items on this page:
   the garage, the basement, the shed, the junk drawer, the car. **Inventory before you
   scavenge** (→ 22-04).
3. **Settle the household's ethics now, out loud, before anyone is desperate** (below).
   Deciding under pressure is how people become someone they did not intend to be.
4. **Never let a child handle salvage.** Lead, glass, chemicals and rusted steel.

## The six categories that matter most

Ranked by how fast you use them up, times how impossible they are to make at home.

1. **Containers.** Buckets (food grade especially), fuel cans, drums, jars, bottles, coolers,
   tubs, tanks. You will never have enough. Water (→ 03-02), food (→ 04-03), waste (→ 13-01)
   and transport all compete for them. *Look at:* building sites (drywall-compound buckets),
   restaurants and bakeries (food-grade buckets), farms (drums, large plastic totes), garages.
2. **Cloth and clothing.** Especially **wool**, and especially **children's sizes**, which
   quietly run out as children grow (→ 11-02). Blankets, curtains, canvas, tarps, sheets,
   towels, thread. *Look at:* closets, thrift stores, upholstery, mattress covers, drop cloths.
3. **Cordage and tape.** Rope, paracord, wire, baling wire, chain, ratchet straps, zip ties,
   duct tape (→ 16-03). *Look at:* garages, farms, boats, trailers, job sites.
4. **Fasteners and hardware.** Screws, nails, bolts, nuts, washers, hinges, hose clamps,
   springs. Small, heavy, and impossible to make at home (→ 16-07). *Look at:* junk drawers,
   workshop coffee cans, broken furniture, pallets.
5. **Fuel and things that burn.** Propane cylinders, lamp oil, charcoal, seasoned wood, scrap
   lumber, paper. **Never burn painted, treated or pressure-treated wood indoors, or under
   anything you cook on** (→ 06-05). *Look at:* grills, camping gear, garages, building scrap,
   fallen limbs.
6. **Blades and steel.** Knives, axes, saws, files, drill bits, hand tools. Good steel outlasts
   almost everything else in this binder (→ 16-01). *Look at:* old toolboxes, farm sheds.
   **Rust is not disqualifying:** rusted good steel cleans up; cheap steel never gets good.

## What is worth its weight when moving (Phase B)

Weight is the limit the moment you are moving (→ 01-03). In order of value per pound:
**medication → water treatment → dense high-calorie food → fire → cordage → a blade → a light →
warm dry layers.** Almost everything else on this page is for staying put (Phase A) or
settling (Phase C). **Anything you cannot carry uphill for a mile is not going with you.**

## The hard limits: things not to take or use

| Never take or use | Why |
|---|---|
| **Insulin, epinephrine or any prescription medicine whose storage history you do not know** | Heat and freezing ruin some medicines with no visible sign, and a damaged rescue device may fail exactly when it must not (→ 29-07). **A frozen-then-thawed insulin vial or pen is discarded.** Your *own* expired stock is a separate household choice: {{choice DC-04}} → 30-05. Found antibiotics: {{choice DC-16}} → 30-17 |
| **Food without a full, intact ingredient label, for anyone with a food allergy**{{#if has.anaphylaxis}} (this household has one){{/if}} | Unlabelled or traded food can carry the allergen. Where the household draws that line: {{choice DC-18}} → 30-19, → 02-04 |
| **Refrigerated or frozen food from a dead fridge or freezer** | FoodSafety.gov: refrigerated food is safe about **4 hours** without power; a full freezer about **48 hours** (24 if half full). **When in doubt, throw it out** (USDA/HHS, Food Safety During a Power Outage; → 04-17) |
| **Bulging, leaking or spurting cans** | **Bulging, leaking or spurting cans are thrown out, unopened.** Botulism gives no warning taste or smell (→ 29-08). Dented or rusty cans are a household choice: {{choice DC-14}} → 30-15 |
| **Containers that held chemicals, fuel or pesticide** | Never for water or food, ever, however well washed |
| **Painted or pressure-treated wood, for indoor burning or cooking** | Lead, arsenic and other preservatives go into the smoke and the ash |
| **Damaged, dropped or unknown-history gas cylinders** | A leaking valve or weakened cylinder is a fire you carry home |
| **Anything inside a building that is unsound** | Burned, flooded or collapsed: nothing in there is worth a broken leg with no emergency room |

## Hazards, and how not to get hurt

- **Lead:** paint from before 1978, some solder, some glazes. Do not sand it, burn it or let
  children near it; wash hands (CDC, About Childhood Lead Poisoning Prevention; → 13-02).
- **Mould and floodwater residue:** N95 mask, gloves, ventilation. Treat porous material that
  floodwater touched as lost (→ 17-03).
- **Sharps and rusted steel:** gloves and boots, always. **Know your tetanus status** (→ 12-04).
  Use tools as their makers intend (OSHA, Hand and Power Tools).
- **Buildings:** floors, stairs and roofs fail after fire and flood. Never go in alone.
- **Germs:** treat everything you bring home as contaminated: clean it, then disinfect it, then
  wash your hands (CDC, When and How to Clean and Disinfect Your Home; → 13-11).
- **Injury is the real risk.** A cut hand with no antibiotics is a life-threatening event
  (→ 12-04).

## The ethics question: settle it now

Decide, as a household, **before** anyone is hungry, where these lines fall:

- **Your own property, and goods freely given or traded:** never in question.
- **Material that is genuinely abandoned:** "abandoned" means actually ownerless, not "nobody
  is home right now".
- **A neighbour's property while they are away:** this is theft, and it is also how you lose
  the only network that will keep you alive (→ 18-01). **Ask. Trade. Leave a note and a debt.**
- **While any authority still functions, the law applies**, and so do the consequences.

## Keeping quiet about it (OPSEC)

"OPSEC" means not advertising what you have or what you plan (→ 14-03).

- **Do not display what you have.** Carrying visible salvage marks you.
- **Go with someone; tell someone where you are going and when you are due back** (→ 15-03).
- **Daylight, known routes, no fixed pattern.**
- **Carry less than you can carry**, so you can still move quickly.

## When it runs out

Scavenging always runs down. The supply of found goods shrinks every week and the effort per
item rises (→ 23-04).

- **How long:** weeks to months in a town; then it is picked over.
- **Ration:** take what you need and what stores well; leave what you cannot use. Hoarding
  what you cannot maintain costs trust you will need.
- **Replace it with:** **trade** (→ 18-02) and **skill** (→ 18-03), which outlast scavenging
  by years; then producing (→ 05-01), repairing (→ 16-05) and making (→ 13-09, → 10-04).

**Plan for scavenging to end.** The households that do well long-term are the ones producing
food and fuel by the time it does.

## Phase notes

- **A ({{ home.setting }}):** {{#if is.urban}}the most material and the most people. Your own
  house first, then trade with neighbours, then genuinely abandoned material.{{else}}less
  material, fewer people. Outbuildings, farm scrap and your own sheds first, then trade with
  neighbours.{{/if}}
- **B (moving):** almost nothing is worth the weight or the delay. Water, fuel and food only.
  **Do not detour to scavenge while travelling with children.**
- **C (establishing):** farms, outbuildings and abandoned homesteads hold tools, fencing,
  containers and hardware far more often than food.
