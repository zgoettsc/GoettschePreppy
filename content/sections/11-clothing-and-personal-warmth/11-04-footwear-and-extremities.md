# Feet, hands and face: protecting the extremities

**What this page is for.** How to keep fingers, toes, ears, nose and cheeks from cold injury: why they
go first, the two foot injuries (frostbite and trench foot), mittens versus gloves, the face, and a
check-and-respond routine.

> **CAUTION.** **Extremities are where cold actually injures people.** Hypothermia takes the core;
> **frostbite takes fingers, toes, ears, nose and cheeks**, often while the rest of you feels fine
> (CDC, Preventing Frostbite). Exposed skin can freeze in minutes in hard cold; read the time on the
> NWS wind chill chart for today's conditions (→ 11-01). **Frostbitten skin goes numb: the warning is
> the absence of a warning.** If a body part stops hurting and starts feeling like nothing, that is
> the emergency: → 08-05.

## Do this now

1. **Mittens, not gloves, for everyone,** with a thin liner glove inside for brief fiddly work.
2. **Far more wool socks than seems reasonable.** Socks are the highest-turnover item in this
   section.
3. **Check that boots fit with thick socks on.** Snug with thin socks means a frostbite trap with thick
   ones (→ 11-06).
4. **The face-check habit:** in hard cold, look at each other's faces every few minutes. White or waxy
   patches on cheeks, nose and ears are visible to a companion and invisible to the person who has
   them.

## Why the extremities go first

When the core gets cold, the body **deliberately** cuts blood flow to the hands, feet and skin to
protect the heart and brain. Two useful consequences:

1. **Cold hands are often a core problem.** If your hands will not warm up, **put on a hat and another
   mid layer.** Warming the core brings blood back to the hands; another glove does much less.
2. **Anything tight makes it worse:** tight boots, tight socks, tight cuffs, watch bands, sitting
   cross-legged for a long time. **Snug is the enemy.**

Nicotine narrows blood vessels; alcohol sends core heat to the skin and loses it; dehydration
thickens the blood. All three make cold injury more likely. The Wilderness Medical Society's advice
for preventing frostbite includes avoiding alcohol and anything that reduces blood flow, and not
wearing tight clothing or footwear (AFP, Frostbite: Prevention and Initial Management).

## Feet

Feet are hardest to protect: **inside a closed container that fills with sweat, standing on frozen
ground, doing all the work.**

**Two different injuries, both serious:**

- **Frostbite:** tissue actually freezes. Needs temperatures below freezing.
- **Trench foot (non-freezing cold injury):** feet kept wet and cold **above** freezing. No freeze
  needed, just wet socks and time; OSHA says it can happen at temperatures as high as 60 °F if feet
  stay wet. Painful and disabling (OSHA, Cold Stress). When drying is hard (→ 11-03), it is a likely
  injury.

**The rules that prevent both:**

- **Wool socks. Never cotton.**
- **Change socks whenever they are damp,** often more than once a day when working. Carry a spare pair
  in an inside pocket, where body heat keeps it dry and warm.
- **Boots roomy enough for thick socks with wiggle room** (→ 11-06).
- **Dry the boots, not just the socks:** pull the liners, stuff with newspaper (→ 11-03).
- **Vapour-barrier socks:** a plastic bag between a thin inner sock and a thick outer sock keeps sweat
  out of the boot's insulation. The inner sock gets damp; change it. Useful on long cold days.
- **Air the feet daily:** dry, warm, bare feet by the heat for a while, then dry socks. This one habit
  prevents most foot problems.
- **Loosen laces when sitting still.**

## Hands

- **Mittens over gloves.** Mitten with a liner glove inside is the best system: pull the mitten off for
  a task, work quickly in the liner, mitten back on.
- **Never bare hands on cold metal.** Skin sticks and tears, and metal pulls heat out very fast. This
  matters around tools, vehicles and firearms (→ 14-05).
- **Keep a spare pair of mittens dry,** always.
- **Chemical hand warmers** are cheap, keep for years, and help anyone standing still outside. **They
  get hot enough to burn.** Use them outside clothing or in a pocket, never against bare or numb skin,
  never inside a small child's mittens or boots (→ 11-02). Keep the packets away from small children,
  who chew them.
- **Idle hands get cold.** Move your fingers, swing your arms, put your hands in your armpits. **The
  armpits and groin are the warmest places on the body** and the fastest way to rewarm hands.

## Face, ears and nose

- **The first place frostbite shows, and the last place anyone checks.**
- **Balaclava or face mask plus goggles** in wind. In hard cold, **nothing exposed.**
- **Watch each other:** white, waxy or grey patches on cheeks, nose and ear tips (CDC).
- **Breath freezes in the mask:** a scarf or mask over the mouth collects moisture and freezes against
  the face. A gaiter you can turn to a dry section, or a mask built to vent, works better.
- **Eyes:** wind and blowing snow hurt and blur vision; in bright snow, **snow blindness** is real.
  Sunglasses or goggles in sun, even in winter, especially with fresh snow (→ 17-01).

## Check and respond

**Check** often in hard cold, at an interval you set before going out and shorten as the wind
chill falls toward the chart's frostbite bands (→ 11-01); **check children more often than adults,
and every few minutes when a child is carried** (→ 11-02):

- Can everyone feel and wiggle all fingers and toes?
- Is anyone's face showing white or waxy patches?
- Is anyone unusually quiet?
- Are anyone's socks wet?

**Respond at once:**

- Numb, white, waxy or hard skin: **stop, get inside, → 08-05.**
- **Do not rub** frostbitten skin (CDC).
- **No direct high heat:** stove, heater, hot water. Numb skin burns without feeling it (CDC).
- **Do not thaw a frostbitten foot if the person then has to walk on it and it may freeze again.**
  A frostbitten part is rewarmed only if there is no risk of it refreezing (AFP, Frostbite;
  → 01-03).
- **Skin-to-skin** (armpits, groin, a companion's belly) is the safe way to rewarm cold-but-not-frozen
  fingers and toes.

## Gear

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Wool socks, many pairs per person** | Highest-turnover item | Merino or wool blend; never cotton |
| Thin liner socks | Wicking layer under thick socks | Fewer blisters, less wet |
| **Mittens, 2 to 3 pairs per person** | Warmer than gloves | Plus overmitts for wet work |
| Liner gloves, 2 to 3 pairs per person | Fine work without bare skin | |
| Balaclavas or neck gaiters, 2 per person | Face and ears; turn to a dry section | Not a loose scarf for children |
| Goggles or wraparound sunglasses | Wind, blowing snow, snow blindness | |
| Chemical hand and toe warmers | Cheap, long shelf life | Outside clothing only |
| Waterproof overmitts | Wet work without soaking insulation | |
| Insulated boots and spare liners | → 11-06 | Liners dry separately |
| Vapour-barrier bags (bread bags) | Keep boot insulation dry | Free |
| Lip balm and skin ointment | Cracked lips and split fingertips hurt and can get infected | → 12-02 |
| Nail clippers | Long toenails in tight boots cause injury | |

**No gear?** Socks from wool sweaters cut and sewn (→ 11-07), or two thin pairs for one thick. Mittens
from sweater sleeves sewn shut, or a wool sock over a glove. Overmitts from plastic bags or a tarp
scrap. A cut sleeve as a gaiter; a T-shirt as a balaclava; a scarf for adults only. Any sunglasses; a
slit visor cut from cardboard or bark is the traditional snow-blindness fix. A warm (not hot) water
bottle or hands in armpits instead of warmers; nothing hot goes inside a child's mitten. Bread or
grocery bags as vapour barriers.

## When it runs out

- **Socks and gloves are the true consumables of this section.** Stock heavily, learn to darn
  (→ 11-07), keep every wool sweater as raw material.
- **Runway:** count pairs per person and divide by real daily changes. Outdoor winter work can use **two
  pairs of socks a day.** Write that on → 23-02.
- **Ration:** dry and rewear rather than wearing fresh every time. **A dried, reworn wool sock beats a
  damp clean one.** Give the best socks to whoever works outside most.
- **The permanent substitute is behaviour:** shorter exposures, more warming breaks, checking each
  other, and not doing outdoor work under an Extreme Cold Warning (→ 29-10) when it could wait.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** exposures are short (the yard, the woodpile, the water haul). The risk is a routine
  chore done carelessly in hard cold. Dry mittens by the door; check faces when anyone comes in.
- **B (moving):** **the highest-risk page in this section for travel.** Long exposure, wet feet, no
  drying, and the refreezing problem. Spare dry socks changed at every stop; face checks at every stop,
  children included.
- **C (established somewhere else):** daily outdoor work means daily wet feet and hands. Build the
  drying setup and the "air the feet by the heat" habit early.
