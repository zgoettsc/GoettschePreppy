# Layering for extreme cold

**What this page is for.** How to dress so that cold, wind and wet do not injure anyone: the layer
system, why sweat is the main danger, the wind chill table with frostbite times, dressing for a
cold house indoors, and a count sheet of what each person needs.

> **SAFETY.** In hard cold, clothing is life-support equipment. **Exposed skin can freeze in
> minutes**: read the time from the NWS wind chill chart for today's temperature and wind, not
> from a guess (NWS, Wind Chill Chart). **The two things that injure are wet and wind.** Being
> wet, from rain, sweat or cold water, can bring on hypothermia even above 40 °F (CDC, Preventing
> Hypothermia). **Cotton, when wet, is dangerous** (→ 11-05). Shivering hard, confused,
> clumsy, or no longer shivering while still cold: that is hypothermia, → 08-05 now.

## Do this now

1. **Lay out what each person actually owns,** by person, against the count sheet below. Most
   families find plenty of adult coats and no real deep-cold gear for the children (→ 11-02). Do it
   in early autumn.
2. **Buy base layers first:** wool or synthetic long underwear, top and bottom, **two sets per
   person** so one dries while the other is worn.
3. **Take every cotton item out of the winter kit:** cotton socks, cotton long johns, hoodies as
   outer layers, jeans.
4. **Read the wind chill table before anyone goes out.** "It's −5 and windy" and "20 minutes to
   frostbite" are different facts, and only the second changes what people do.
5. **Set your stop line:** when your NWS office ({{ region.home.nws_office }}) issues an **Extreme
   Cold Warning** (the product called a Wind Chill Warning before October 2024; NWS; → 29-10),
   nobody goes out except for essential minutes with no skin showing,
   children stay in, and vehicles stay home. Criteria are set by each office (NWS, Wind Chill
   Chart). Your office's criterion: wind chill at or below ______ °F.

## The system, not the coat

Think in layers, not in one warm coat. A layer system adapts to how hard you are working, lets you
shed heat before you sweat, and dries in stages.

| Layer | Job | Material | How it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Base** (against skin) | Move sweat away from the skin | Merino wool, polyester, polypropylene | **Cotton holds sweat and chills you** |
| **Mid** (insulation) | Trap air: this is the warmth | Fleece, wool, down, synthetic fill | Squashed or wet: no loft, no warmth |
| **Outer** (shell) | Block wind and water | Windproof or waterproof-breathable nylon | Not windproof; or so airtight you soak it from inside |
| **Extremities** | Head, hands, feet, face | Wool, insulated, windproof | Where frostbite happens (→ 11-04) |

{{figure fig-layer-system}}

**Insulation is trapped air, not fabric.** That explains everything else:

- **Several thin layers beat one thick one:** more trapped air, and you can adjust.
- **Squashed insulation does not insulate.** Tight boots, a pack strap crushing a puffy jacket,
  sitting on your down layer: all make cold spots.
- **Wet insulation does not insulate.** Water pushes out the air and carries heat away far faster
  than air does.
- **Wind strips away the warm air layer.** That is what wind chill is, and why a windproof shell
  over modest insulation beats thick insulation that the wind blows through.

## The rule that matters most: don't sweat

**Sweat is one of the main ways people get into trouble in hard cold,** and it happens while they
feel great. You shovel, carry wood or walk, you overheat, you sweat into your base layer, then you
stop and **that wet layer chills you**. OSHA lists wetness, dressing improperly and exhaustion
among the risk factors for cold stress (OSHA, Cold Stress).

- **Start slightly cold.** Comfortable standing still means overdressed for working.
- **Vent before you sweat, not after:** open zips, open the front, take the hat off. Your head is
  your fastest thermostat.
- **Shed a layer before hard work;** put it back the moment you stop.
- **The C-O-L-D memory aid:** keep it **C**lean (dirty insulation mats down), avoid **O**verheating,
  wear it **L**oose and in **L**ayers, stay **D**ry.

## What to wear, roughly

A starting point, not a rule: adjust by how you feel standing still, and let the wind chill chart
below decide the face and the time limit.

| Conditions | What is needed |
|---|---|
| Around freezing, dry, calm | Base + light mid + shell |
| Well below freezing, light wind | Base + fleece + insulated shell; hat, gloves |
| Below zero °F | Base + heavy mid + insulated parka; wool hat and hood, mittens, insulated boots, face covered |
| Wind chill in a frostbite band on the chart | Everything: **no exposed skin at all**, goggles or a face mask, mittens over liner gloves, and **a time limit outdoors set before going out** |
| Working hard in any of these | **Fewer layers than standing still.** Vent hard; layer up the moment you stop |

**Extremities are where people get hurt** (→ 11-04). Mittens are much warmer than gloves because
the fingers share heat; a thin liner glove inside lets you do fiddly work briefly without bare skin.

## The wind chill table

Values from the **NWS wind chill chart** (public domain). Read across from the air temperature and
down from the steady wind speed; the cell is the wind chill in °F (NWS, Wind Chill Chart).

| Air temp ↓ / Wind → | 10 mph | 15 mph | 20 mph | 25 mph | 30 mph | 35 mph | 40 mph |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **10 °F** | −4 | −7 | −9 | −11 | −12 | −14 | −15 |
| **5 °F** | −10 | −13 | −15 | −17 | −19 | −21 | −22 |
| **0 °F** | −16 | −19 | −22 | −24 | −26 | −27 | −29 |
| **−5 °F** | −22 | −26 | −29 | −31 | −33 | −34 | −36 |
| **−10 °F** | −28 | −32 | −35 | −37 | −39 | −41 | −43 |
| **−15 °F** | −35 | −39 | −42 | −44 | −46 | −48 | −50 |
| **−20 °F** | −41 | −45 | −48 | −51 | −53 | −55 | −57 |
| **−25 °F** | −47 | −51 | −55 | −58 | −60 | −62 | −64 |

**Frostbite time on exposed skin: read it from the NWS chart's shading, not from the wind chill
number alone.** The chart shades frostbite time by temperature and wind together, so the same wind
chill can fall in a different band at a different wind speed. At ordinary winds of 10 to 25 mph,
read from the chart itself (NWS, Wind Chill Chart, 2001 graphic):

- **About 30 minutes** begins at a wind chill of about −19 to −24 °F. NWS's own example: 0 °F
  air with a 15 mph wind is −19 °F, and exposed skin can freeze in 30 minutes.
- **About 10 minutes** begins at about −37 °F with a 25 mph wind, about −42 °F at 20 mph, and
  about −45 °F at 10 to 15 mph. The stronger the wind, the sooner this band starts.
- **About 5 minutes** begins at about −58 to −64 °F with a 20 to 25 mph wind, and about −71 °F
  at 15 mph. At 10 mph the table above never reaches it.

**When the number sits near a boundary, use the shorter time.** Example: −20 °F air with a 20 mph
wind reads **−48 °F: about ten minutes to frostbite** on any exposed skin (NWS chart). The chart was
built for adults, so **be more cautious with children, older people and anyone in poor health**
(NWS; → 11-02).

## Wet cold near freezing

Places that get ice storms and days of wet weather around 30 °F injure people through **wet**, not
deep cold.{{#if is.cold_winters}} Your home has deep cold; if you travel or move south, you may meet
this instead.{{/if}}

- **A waterproof shell over wool or synthetic fill beats down.** In freezing rain the shell does the
  work, and the insulation under it must keep working when damp. **Down fails wet** (→ 11-05).
- **Trench foot** (non-freezing cold injury) is the wet-feet injury: no freeze needed, and OSHA
  says it can happen at temperatures as high as 60 °F if feet stay wet (OSHA, Cold Stress;
  → 11-04). **Dry socks whenever they are damp; feet dried, warmed and checked at every change;
  boots dried every night** (→ 11-03).
- **Pack for it:** rain shell, waterproof boots and extra wool socks outrank an expedition parka.

## The gear under the layers

Some people carry things in the cold that must stay working: an insulin pump or glucose sensor, a
phone that runs a medical app, fast sugar, epinephrine, an inhaler.

- **Dress so one device site can be reached without undressing:** a half-zip or button-front base
  layer over a belly site, or a sleeve that pushes up over an arm site.
- **Phones and spare batteries ride in an inner pocket, against the body.** Cold kills lithium
  batteries fast (→ 09-06).
- **Fast sugar in a chest pocket reachable with the shell on,** never buried in a pack.
- **Cold fingers do not bleed for a finger-stick test:** warm the hands in the armpits first. Keep the
  meter and strips warm too.
- **Low blood sugar and hypothermia look alike.** When in doubt, test (→ 08-05).
- **Medicines that must not freeze** ride inside the layers too.

## Living in a cold house

If the house is heated to one room (→ 08-01), **you dress for indoor cold too.** In a cold house,
people live in base layers, wool socks, a fleece and a hat **indoors, all day.** That is normal, it is
how people lived before central heat, and it cuts the fuel you burn (→ 06-04).

**Sleeping cold is a skill:** change into dry base layers before bed (never sleep in what you sweated
in), a wool hat and socks, and insulation **under** you as well as over. A cold mattress or floor
takes enormous heat. Sleeping together shares heat.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Cotton anywhere in the system,** especially socks and base layers.
- **Boots laced too tight:** cuts circulation and freezes toes.
- **Skipping the hat** because the coat is warm.
- **Bare hands "just for a minute"** at −20 °F. Keep liner gloves on.
- **Sweating into your only dry base layer.**
- **A shell that is not windproof.**
- **Alcohol:** it makes you feel warm while you lose heat faster, and it impairs judgment. CDC
  lists drinking alcohol as a hypothermia risk, and OSHA says no alcohol in the cold (CDC; OSHA).
- **Not eating or drinking.** Staying warm burns calories, and you get dehydrated in the cold without
  feeling thirsty (→ 03-01).

## Gear

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Wool or synthetic base layers, 2 sets per person** | The foundation | Highest-value clothing purchase |
| Wool or fleece mid layers, 2 per person | The insulation | Fleece dries fast; wool works damp |
| Insulated parka and windproof shell | Blocks the wind | A hood matters in hard cold |
| Insulated bibs or snow pants | Legs get forgotten | Bibs beat pants for children |
| **Wool socks, 6 pairs per person** | The most-used, most-failed item | |
| Wool hats, 2 per person; balaclava or gaiter | Thermostat and face cover | One always gets lost |
| **Mittens** and liner gloves | Mittens are warmer; liners let you work | Spares: they get wet and lost |
| Insulated waterproof boots, sized for thick socks | → 11-06 | Tight boots freeze toes |
| Goggles or wraparound glasses | Wind, blowing snow, snow glare | |
| Wool blankets, sleeping bags | Sleeping cold and travel | |

**No gear?** **Wool** from thrift stores, closets, blankets and army surplus is the best cold-weather
scavenge: it keeps insulating when damp. Down comforters cut and sewn into vests (→ 16-04); fleece
blankets as mid layers. A trash bag or tarp as an emergency shell (vent it, or you soak yourself from
inside). Newspaper stuffed between layers really works. Wool sweaters cut into socks; two thin pairs
for one thick. A cut sleeve as a gaiter; a T-shirt as a balaclava. **Do not** build a cold-weather
system from cotton hoodies and jeans.

## When it runs out

- **Clothing runs out by wearing out and getting wet,** not by being used. The consumables are **socks
  and gloves**.
- **Drying is the real bottleneck** (→ 11-03). In a cold house wet gear can take days; gear that is
  not dry is not insulation. That is why two sets of everything is the real requirement.
- **Rotate:** wear one set, dry one set. **Change base layers before bed, every night.**
- **Repair rather than replace** (→ 11-07).
- **Substitute:** wool blankets become ponchos, vests and liners; layering more thin things replaces
  one lost thick thing. **Trapped air is the goal.**
- **Children outgrow everything:** see → 11-02 and → 11-09. It is a standing line on → 23-02.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** dress for a cold house indoors and the full system outdoors. Start the rotation
  and drying routine early (→ 11-03).
- **B (moving):** the highest-risk clothing situation. Adults carrying children sweat while the
  children, not moving, freeze (→ 11-02). Vent constantly, stop before you sweat, check the children
  every few minutes, and **strongly prefer not to move in winter** (→ 01-02, → 01-03). One clothing
  bag per person, and the children's next-size bag, travel with you (→ 22-05).
- **C (established somewhere else):** more outdoor work means more sweat and more wet gear. Build a
  drying setup near the stove before the first hard freeze (→ 11-03).
