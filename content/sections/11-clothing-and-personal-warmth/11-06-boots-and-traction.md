# Boots, traction and winter footwear

**What this page is for.** What makes a winter boot work, how to fit it, how to stop falls on ice, and
how to keep boots alive through a long winter.

> **CAUTION.** **A fall on ice is a serious injury in a winter outage,** and with no emergency
> room behind it a broken wrist, hip or a head injury is a crisis (→ 12-09, → 12-19,
> → 12-16). **An adult carrying a small child across ice is the situation to design against.**
> Traction devices cost little and prevent it. **The second hazard is the boot itself: a boot that is
> too tight freezes your feet,** because squeezed circulation makes frostbite more likely; frostbite
> prevention guidance says avoid tight footwear (AFP, Frostbite; → 11-04).

## Do this now

1. **Traction devices for every adult, and a pair by every door.** Buy spares: the rubber perishes and
   they get lost.
2. **Try boots on with the thick wool socks you will wear.** Toes touching, or toes that cannot wiggle:
   **go up a size.**
3. **Choose boots with removable liners.** With no dryer, a boot you cannot dry is a boot you lose for
   days (→ 11-03).
4. **Children's boots: size up, and buy the next size ahead** (→ 11-02, → 11-09).

## What makes a winter boot work

1. **Room for thick socks and air.** Insulation is trapped air, and toes must move. **Snug boots are
   cold boots.**
2. **A removable liner.** The liner soaks up sweat and takes a day or more to dry inside the boot. Pull
   it, dry it separately, and the boot is wearable tomorrow. This feature is worth more than extra
   insulation in an outage.
3. **A waterproof lower or rubber bottom:** snow, slush, meltwater on the entry floor.
4. **A thick, insulating sole.** Cold comes up through the sole from frozen ground; a thin sole means a
   cold foot whatever is above it.
5. **Height and a good cuff,** with **gaiters** for deep snow.
6. **Traction that works on ice,** not just snow.

**Boot temperature ratings are marketing figures** measured under assumptions that usually involve
walking. Use them only to compare boots, not as a promise for standing still.

### The cold-country answer

**Pac boots** (a rubber lower, a leather or nylon upper, and a removable felt or wool liner) are the
traditional and still-correct answer for deep cold: waterproof below, very warm, and the liner comes
out to dry. They are clumsy for long walks. **Insulated leather work boots** are better for walking and
working and worse for standing still in hard cold. **Rubber overboots** pull over ordinary shoes for
chores. Ideally own both kinds: one for standing and chores, one for distance.

## Traction: the injury-prevention item

Ice under snow is invisible, and freeze-thaw cycles glaze everything.

| Device | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Coil or spring traction devices** | Packed snow, general walking | Cheap, light, easy on and off |
| **Spike or stud devices** | Bare ice | Best grip; take them off inside |
| **Short hex-head screws in the sole lugs** | Improvised, permanent, cheap | Works well |
| Studded rubber overshoes | Chores | Slip over boots |
| Grit on the path | The path itself | Below |

**Treat the ground, not just the feet:** wood ash from the stove (→ 06-02), sand, cat litter or gravel on
the path from the door to the woodpile and the water. You walk that path many times a day all winter;
make it safe once.

## Falls, seriously

- **Most falls happen on routine trips:** carrying something, in the dark, in a hurry.
- **Carrying a child raises the stakes:** you cannot put your hands out. **Do not carry a small child
  across ice:** clear and grit the path, make two trips, or use a sled.
- **A sled** moves children, water and wood across snow and ice without carrying.
- **Light the path** (→ 10-01). Winter evenings are long.
- **Walk flat-footed with short steps, hands out of pockets.** Teach children the same.

## Fit, socks and frostbite

- **Fit with the socks you will wear,** in the afternoon (feet swell).
- **Toes must wiggle.** If not, circulation is squeezed and you will get cold or frostbitten feet
  whatever the rating (→ 11-04).
- **Do not over-lace.** Loosen when sitting still.
- **Two thin socks can beat one thick one** (a wicking liner and a wool outer), **only if the boot is
  not then tight.**
- **A vapour barrier** (a bread bag between liner sock and outer sock) keeps the boot's insulation dry
  over a long day (→ 11-04).

## Care and repair

- **Dry the liners every night:** pull them out, stand them near (not on) the heat, stuff the shells with
  newspaper (→ 11-03).
- **Never dry boots on the stove:** it ruins the glue, cracks leather and shrinks the fit.
- **Condition leather** with beeswax or oil a few times a season (→ 10-04).
- **Repair early:** a small sole separation becomes a lost boot. Shoe adhesive, contact cement,
  stitching (→ 16-05).
- **Rotate pairs** so each dries fully.

## Gear

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Pac boots with removable liners**, per person | Standing in deep cold; liners dry separately | Sized for thick socks |
| Insulated leather work boots, per adult | Walking and working | Waterproofed |
| **Spare liners** for each pair | The wear item and the drying bottleneck | Rarely stocked |
| **Traction devices, 2 or more per adult** | Fall prevention | They perish; keep spares |
| Rubber overboots | Slush, chores, mud | |
| Gaiters | Snow out of the boot top | |
| Sled | Moves children, water and wood | |
| Grit, sand or ash bucket by the door | Treat the path | |
| Boot repair kit: adhesive, contact cement, awl and thread | Sole separation is the common failure | → 16-05 |
| Beeswax or leather conditioner | Waterproofing wears off | |
| Boot dryer or rack | Days versus overnight | |
| **Children's boots in the next size** | They outgrow them yearly | → 11-09 |

**No gear?** Traction: short hex-head screws driven into the lugs; chain or wire wrapped over the boot;
rope wound round the sole. Overboots: heavy plastic bags taped at the top (no grip: add the screws).
Liners: wool felt, layered wool socks, sheepskin, cut-up wool blanket. Insoles: felt, foam pad,
cardboard for a while, dry grass. Gaiters: cut trouser legs, tarp scraps. Repairs: wire, cord and duct
tape hold a failing boot long enough to get home.

## When it runs out

- **Boots are among the hardest things to improvise and the worst to lose.** A household without
  working boots in winter cannot fetch water or wood. Treat boots as **critical equipment.**
- **Runway:** a good pair lasts years; **liners and soles wear out.** Stock spare liners and repair
  supplies now.
- **Ration:** rotate pairs, dry nightly, condition, repair at once. **Keep one good pair per adult as
  untouchable** and use lesser footwear for dirty work.
- **Children again:** boots are outgrown fast. Next sizes ahead, on → 11-09 and → 23-02.
- **Traction devices perish:** check them every autumn.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** short trips, many times a day, on ice: exactly the frequent-fall pattern. Grit the
  paths, cleats by the door, a sled for children and loads.
- **B (moving):** boots are the most important equipment on a winter move. Spare dry socks at every
  stop, gaiters, a repair kit. A failed boot on the move in winter is an emergency (→ 01-03).
- **C (established somewhere else):** daily outdoor work. Two pairs per adult, spare liners, a drying
  spot by the door.
