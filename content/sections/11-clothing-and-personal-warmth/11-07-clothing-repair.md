# Repairing and extending clothing

**What this page is for.** The four repairs that keep most clothing working, how to make clothes last,
how to remake adult clothing into what you need (especially children's clothes), and what to stock.
Damaged or wet clothing stops protecting you from cold (OSHA, Cold Stress; CDC, Preventing
Hypothermia), so repair is part of staying warm.

In a long emergency the clothing in your closets may be the last you can buy. The question becomes how
long you can keep it working and what you can make from what is around you. **Fabric is one of the few
things that is truly plentiful;** the limits are **skill, needles and thread.**

## Do this now

1. **Buy a real sewing kit and a large stock of thread, needles and pins.** They are tiny, cheap, never
   expire, and are nearly impossible to improvise. **Thread is the consumable that limits everything
   here.**
2. **Learn four things this month:** sew on a button, darn a sock, patch a knee, fix a zipper pull.
3. **Start a rag bag and a parts bin.** Every worn-out garment gives up its buttons, zippers, elastic,
   snaps, cord and usable fabric before anything is thrown away.
4. **Fix things now, while it is easy.** Five minutes on a small tear saves a garment.

## The four repairs that matter most

**1. Darning socks.** Socks wear out fastest (→ 11-04), and darning is easy:

- Put a smooth round object inside the sock (a darning egg, a light bulb, a smooth stone) to hold the
  shape.
- Sew a row of parallel stitches **across** the hole in one direction, then **weave** over and under
  through them at right angles, filling the hole with new fabric.
- **Wool yarn on wool socks:** matching the fibre keeps warmth and stretch.
- **Reinforce before it is a hole,** when the heel goes thin.

**2. Patching** knees, elbows and seats:

- **Patch from the inside** where the garment is under strain and should lie flat.
- **Patch from the outside** where it gets rubbed (canvas knees, work gear).
- **Round the corners;** square corners peel.
- Iron-on patches do not last on hard-use clothes; **stitch round the edge** and they become a real
  repair.

**3. Seams and buttons.** Sew a split seam with **backstitch** along the original line, then a second
pass. **Backstitch, not running stitch, for anything under load.** A button takes five minutes.

**4. Zippers,** the most common serious failure in outerwear:

- **Teeth separating behind the slider** with teeth intact: often fixed by squeezing the slider gently
  with pliers; it has spread and stopped meshing.
- **Missing pull:** a paperclip, a split ring or cord.
- **Missing stop at the end:** stitch a hard bar of thread across the track.
- **Replacing a whole zipper is a real project,** which is why **spare zippers are worth stocking.**
- **Backup fastenings:** buttons, snaps, hook-and-loop, toggles, a cord and loop. **A coat that closes
  with buttons is a working coat.**

## Making things last

- **Reinforce before failure:** a second layer at knees, elbows, seat and cuffs of work clothes while
  they are new.
- **Patch shells at once.** A pinhole in a waterproof shell lets water in and the whole layer system
  fails (→ 11-05). Adhesive nylon repair tape works; keep a roll.
- **Re-waterproof** once a year with wax or spray.
- **Replace elastic;** it perishes with age. Stock some.
- **Wash less, gently.** Every wash costs water, fuel and drying (→ 13-04) and wears the garment. Wool can
  often be aired instead of washed.
- **Store correctly:** clean, dry, sealed against moths (wool), loose (down).

## Remaking

| Source | Becomes |
|---|---|
| **Adult wool sweater** | Two or three pairs of children's mittens, a child's vest, sock liners, boot liners |
| **Wool blanket** | Poncho, vest, mittens, boot liners, leg wraps, a child's coat |
| **Fleece blanket** | Mid-layer jacket, children's layers, hats, neck gaiters |
| **Down comforter** | Vest, a baby's bunting, a jacket liner |
| **Tarp or tent fly** | Windproof shell, gaiters, overmitts, rain poncho |
| **Old jeans or canvas** | Patches, knee reinforcement, tool rolls, aprons |
| **Leather coat or sofa** | Glove palms, boot patches, straps |
| **Adult clothes generally** | The next size up for children (→ 11-02) |
| **Worn-out anything** | Buttons, zippers, elastic, snaps, cord, thread, rags |

**Cutting down adult clothes for children is the most valuable remaking skill,** because children's
clothing runs out by growth and nothing prevents it (→ 11-09).

**Felting wool:** washing wool hot and agitating it on purpose makes a dense fabric that can be cut
without hemming. It is excellent for mittens, boot liners and insoles, and it rescues shrunken sweaters.

## Tools and skill

Very little: needles in several sizes (including a heavy one for canvas and leather), strong thread,
scissors, pins, a thimble and patience. A **sewing machine** multiplies your work while there is power;
a **hand-crank or treadle machine** is one of the great pieces of grid-free equipment. **The skill is the
real asset,** and one of the most tradeable there is (→ 18-03).

## Gear

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Thread: large spools, several weights and colours** | The limiting consumable | Include heavy-duty and upholstery thread |
| **Needles: many, several sizes** | They bend, break and vanish | Include leather and curved needles |
| Wool darning yarn and a darning egg | Socks wear fastest | |
| Fabric scissors and a seam ripper | Sharp scissors make everything possible | Do not cut paper with them |
| Pins, many safety pins, thimble | A safety pin is a repair in itself | |
| **Spare zippers, buttons, snaps, hook-and-loop, elastic** | Parts that fail and cannot be made | Small and cheap |
| Patches: canvas, denim, nylon repair tape | The common repairs | |
| Awl and waxed thread | Boots, packs, heavy gear (→ 16-05) | |
| Water-repellent spray or wax bar | Restores waterproofing | Yearly |
| Sewing machine; hand-crank or treadle if found | Speed now; no power needed later | |
| Rag bag and parts bin | Nothing is thrown away whole | |

**No gear?** Fabric is in every closet, curtain, sheet, blanket, tarp, tent and sofa. Thread: unravel it
from a tightly woven edge; dental floss for heavy repairs; **the inner strands of paracord make
excellent thread.** Needles are the hard one: **stock real needles now.** Buttons from other garments,
carved wood or bone, cut plastic. Elastic: a drawstring channel, ties, hook-and-loop. Darning egg: a
light bulb, a smooth stone, a doorknob. Patches: any sturdy fabric; old jeans are ideal.

## When it runs out

- **Fabric lasts a very long time; thread and needles do not.** Stock thread and needles in quantities
  that feel absurd.
- **Runway:** a household that sews can keep clothing working for years; one that cannot is limited to
  what is intact now, often a season or two in hard use.
- **Ration:** repair early (cheap) rather than late (costly in thread and fabric). Keep the best thread
  for load-bearing repairs.
- **Resupply:** unpicking seams from unwanted garments gives usable thread; the parts bin is a real
  supply.
- **The ladder:** repair, patch, remake, felt, improvise. Each step uses more skill and less material.
- **Very long term:** spinning and weaving, and fibre animals where land allows. It starts with being
  able to sew (→ 24-01).

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** learn the four repairs, build the kit, start the rag bag and parts bin, reinforce work
  clothes.
- **B (moving):** a small repair kit: a few needles, strong thread, safety pins, nylon repair tape, a
  spare zipper pull, duct tape. A failed coat zipper or boot seam on a winter move is an emergency
  (→ 11-06).
- **C (established somewhere else):** repair becomes routine work, and remaking is how growing children
  get clothed. Sewing is one of the most natural things to trade (→ 18-03).
