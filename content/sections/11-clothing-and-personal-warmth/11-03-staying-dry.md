# Staying dry, and drying gear without a dryer

**What this page is for.** How clothes get wet in winter and how to stop it, how to dry clothes and
boots in a cold house with no dryer, and how to deal with the damp air that drying creates.

> **CAUTION.** **Wet is what turns cold into injury.** Wet clothing loses most of its insulation and
> pulls heat from the body far faster than dry; it is a recognised hypothermia risk (CDC, Preventing
> Hypothermia; OSHA, Cold Stress). Two hazards on this page: **(1) fire:** clothing hung on or too
> near a stove or heater is a classic house fire; **(2) damp air:** drying laundry in a sealed winter
> house puts a lot of water into the air, which condenses on cold surfaces and grows mould. **Never
> use an unvented fuel heater to speed drying in a closed room** (→ 08-02), and never hang anything
> on or over a portable heater (→ 08-10).

## Do this now

1. **Build a drying setup before the first hard freeze:** a rack or line **near, never on,** the heat
   source, placed so nothing can fall onto it.
2. **The two-set rule:** everyone has two of everything, one worn, one drying (→ 11-01).
3. **Change base layers before bed, every night, for everyone.** Sleeping in a damp layer is how a
   warm household ends up cold at 3 a.m.
4. **A boot tray and a drying spot by the door,** and the rule that wet gear goes there at once.

## How clothes get wet, and how to stop each one

1. **Sweat, the biggest.** Vent **before** you overheat (→ 11-01). Most winter wet comes from inside
   the clothes.
2. **Snow and rain.** Windproof or waterproof shell, gaiters, bibs, hood, and brushing snow off
   **before** coming inside: snow melted indoors is water in your gear.
3. **Wet work.** Hauling water (→ 03-02, → 03-06), washing, melting snow, animal chores. Waterproof
   gloves and a dedicated "wet work" outer set.
4. **Children** in snow are soaked within the hour (→ 11-02). Plan for it.

## Why drying is hard in a winter outage

Drying needs **heat, moving air, and somewhere for the moisture to go.** In a cold outage you are short
of all three:

- The house is **cold** (→ 08-01), so water evaporates slowly.
- The house is **sealed** for warmth (→ 08-03), so moisture has nowhere to go.
- **Cold air holds very little water.** Outside air is very dry, so bringing some in and warming it
  dries things fast, but every bit of air you heat costs fuel.

**Expect wet gear to take a day or more to dry in a cold house.** Heavy boots and thick mittens can
take **several days.** Plan around that.

## Methods that work

1. **Near the heat, never on it.** A rack or line at a safe distance from the stove or heater, where
   rising warm air reaches it. **Never draped on a stove, stovepipe, fireplace screen, or anywhere it
   can fall in.** Never left drying by a fire overnight.
2. **High up.** Warm air rises: a line near the ceiling of the heated room dries far faster than a
   rack on the floor. It is the best free drying spot you have.
3. **Freeze-drying outdoors.** Hang wet laundry outside in below-freezing air. It freezes stiff, then
   the ice turns straight to vapour and the clothes end up dry. It takes a day or more, works best in
   dry, sunny cold, and **uses no fuel at all.** Bring items in stiff and let the last moisture leave
   indoors.{{#if is.cold_winters}} Your winters are cold enough for this on most dry days.{{/if}}
4. **Wring, roll and press first.** Wring hard, then **roll the garment in a dry towel and stand on
   it.** This removes a surprising amount of water and cuts drying time a lot.
5. **Moving air.** A small fan across a rack dries far better than more heat in still air: a very
   good use of a little electricity (→ 09-01).
6. **Body heat, carefully.** Damp (not soaked) socks and mittens can be dried inside your clothing
   against your body while you sleep. It works and costs nothing.
7. **Boots** dry from the inside out and take longest:
   - **Pull out the liners and insoles** and dry them separately: that is most of the job.
   - **Stuff newspaper inside** and replace it as it gets damp.
   - **Never dry boots on a stove:** heat ruins the glue, cracks leather and shrinks the fit (→ 11-06).
   - A boot dryer on battery or solar power is a small load and worth it if you have the watts.

## Managing the damp you create

Drying inside a sealed winter house puts water into the air. It condenses on the coldest surfaces
(windows, outside walls, corners), where it grows mould and soaks insulation.

- **Vent on purpose.** A window opened wide for a short time swaps humid air for very dry cold air,
  and costs less heat than people fear because you are swapping air, not chilling the walls.
- **Dry in the heated room, and vent that room,** rather than spreading wet gear through the house.
- **Keep indoor relative humidity between 30 and 50%, never above 60%** (EPA, A Brief Guide to Mold,
  Moisture and Your Home). A cheap hygrometer reads it. In deep cold the practical ceiling is lower,
  because cold glass condenses moisture long before the room feels damp.
- **Watch the windows.** Water or frost on the inside of the glass means the humidity is already too
  high for that surface: vent now.
- **Watch for mould** in corners, behind furniture and on outside walls. It is bad for everyone,
  especially small children.
- **The tension is real:** → 08-03 says seal the house; this page says vent it. The answer is **brief,
  targeted venting of the room where the moisture is made.**

## The rotation

- **Two sets of everything per person, one worn, one drying.**
- **Wet gear off at the door and straight to the drying spot.**
- **Base layers changed before bed,** every night.
- **Socks changed more often than you think.** Damp socks in boots lead straight to trench foot and
  frostbite (→ 11-04).
- **Nothing goes into a drawer damp.**

**No gear?** Racks and lines from cord, wire, broom handles across chair backs, a ladder laid flat.
Boot stuffing: newspaper, dry cloth, dry grass, uncooked rice in a sock. Desiccants: silica packets
saved from packaging, uncooked rice. Waterproofing: trash bags as overmitts and gaiters; a tarp
poncho; beeswax rubbed into leather and canvas (→ 10-04); plastic bags between sock layers as a
vapour barrier (it keeps the boot's insulation dry at the cost of a damp inner sock). Pressing: a dry
towel, a flat surface and your weight.

## When it runs out

- **Drying capacity, not clothing, is the real limit.** If you can dry one set a day, you can get one
  set wet a day. **That limits work, not just laundry.** Do wet work in one block, then dry.
- **Fuel is the hidden cost:** drying near the stove costs wood (→ 06-04). Outdoor freeze-drying is
  free.
- **Ration:** fewer garments, worn longer. **A base layer worn three days but kept dry beats a clean
  one that is damp.** Dryness beats cleanliness here, within hygiene limits (→ 13-02).
- **Never get everything wet at once.** If the whole household soaks its gear in one afternoon, you
  have days with no dry outer layers. **Stagger the wet work.**
- **Substitute: wool.** Wool keeps insulating when damp, so it survives a failed drying system in a
  way cotton and down cannot (→ 11-05).

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** rack and high line in the heated room, freeze-drying outside on dry cold days, boot
  tray by the door, everyone on the two-set rotation.
- **B (moving):** **assume you cannot dry gear while moving in winter.** Staying dry (venting,
  waterproof shells) matters most on the move, and it is a strong argument against winter travel
  (→ 01-02, → 01-03). Carry a spare dry base layer per person sealed in a bag as an emergency
  reserve.
- **C (established somewhere else):** more outdoor work, more wet gear, and space to build a drying
  room near the stove. Build it early.
