# Materials: wool, synthetics and what fails

**What this page is for.** Which fabrics keep you warm when wet, which melt near fire, how to tell an
unlabelled fabric by a burn test, what to wear for saw and wood work, and how to make materials last.

> **CAUTION. Two material facts matter more than any brand.**
> **(1) Cotton is dangerous in the cold.** Wet cotton loses nearly all its insulation and holds water
> against your skin. OSHA and CDC guidance is to avoid it in cold and to get out of wet clothing
> (OSHA, Cold Stress; CDC, Preventing Hypothermia). Not in base layers, not in socks, not in the
> winter kit.
> **(2) Synthetics melt.** Fleece, nylon and polyester near an open flame or a hot stove **melt onto
> skin** and cause burns far worse than a fabric that just chars. **Wear wool, not fleece, around
> fire** (→ 06-01, → 07-01, → 12-03).

## Do this now

1. **Sort the winter kit by fibre.** Read the labels. Much of what you thought was cold-weather
   clothing will be cotton.
2. **Start buying wool secondhand:** sweaters, blankets, army surplus. It is cheap, lasts decades, and
   is the most valuable material in this section.
3. **Set aside a "fire clothing" set:** wool or heavy cotton canvas outer layers for stove and fire
   work.
4. **Learn the burn test** (below) to identify unlabelled scavenged fabric.

## The fibres in one table

| Fibre | Warm when wet? | Dries | Near flame | Durability | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Wool (especially merino)** | **Yes: the key property** | Slow | **Chars, puts itself out** | Very good | **The survival fibre** |
| **Polyester fleece** | Somewhat | **Fast** | **Melts: dangerous** | Good | Excellent mid layer; never near fire |
| Polypropylene | Somewhat | Very fast | **Melts** | Good | Good base layer; holds odour |
| Nylon | No insulation | Fast | **Melts** | **Excellent** | Shells, packs, outerwear |
| **Down** | **No: collapses** | **Very slow** | Burns | Good if dry | Warmest per ounce **if dry** |
| Synthetic fill | Somewhat | Moderate | Melts | Good | **More reliable than down in a wet winter** |
| **Cotton** | **No: dangerous** | **Very slow** | Chars, burns | Good | **Not in the winter kit; fine in summer** (→ 11-08) |
| Canvas or duck (cotton) | No | Slow | Chars | **Excellent** | Good work shell in dry cold |
| Linen | No | Fast | Chars | Good | Summer only |
| Silk | Poorly | Fast | Chars | Fair | Light base layer |
| Leather | No | Very slow | Chars | Excellent | Boots, gloves, wind blocking |

## Why wool

- **It insulates when wet.** Wool fibres are crimped and hold air even damp: the most important
  property when drying is hard (→ 11-03). **Wool survives the failure of your drying system.**
- **It resists fire:** it chars and puts itself out instead of melting. The right fibre for tending a
  stove or outdoor fire.
- **It resists odour** and can be worn much longer between washes than synthetics, which matters when
  washing costs water, fuel and drying (→ 13-04).
- **It is warm across a wide range** and breathes, so you sweat into it less.
- **It is everywhere:** sweaters, blankets, army surplus, coats. **A wool blanket is raw material** for
  socks, mittens, liners and a poncho (→ 11-07).
- **Downsides:** slow to dry, heavier than synthetics, itchy in coarse grades (merino is not), eaten by
  **moths** (store clean and sealed), and it shrinks if washed hot or agitated. **Wash wool cool and
  gently; never wring it hard.**

## Where synthetics win

- **Fleece dries fast:** a real advantage when drying is limited.
- **Polypropylene base layers** wick hard and dry fastest of all.
- **Nylon shells** are the only practical windproof and waterproof layer, and in hard wind the shell
  does more work than any insulation.
- **Synthetic fill keeps some loft when damp,** which makes it safer than down for a wet winter.

**Remember: they melt.** Fleece near the stove, a nylon shell brushing a stovepipe, synthetic long
johns while tending a fire: these cause burns that melt into the skin and are much harder to treat
(→ 12-03). **Wool or canvas for fire work.**

## Down: wins and losses

Down is the warmest insulation for its weight and packed size, **in dry cold.** Wet down clumps and
gives almost **no** insulation, and it takes a very long time to dry in a cold house. **In a wet or
long winter outage, make synthetic fill the main insulation and down the layer you keep dry.** If you
own down, protect it carefully.

## The burn test for unlabelled fabric

Useful when scavenging (→ 22-02). Snip a small piece, hold it with tweezers over something that cannot
burn, and light it **outdoors or over a sink**:

| Fibre | How it burns | Smell | What is left |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wool** | Slowly, **puts itself out** | Burning hair | Black bead that crushes |
| **Cotton or linen** | Fast and steady, keeps burning | Burning paper | Soft grey ash |
| **Polyester** | **Melts and shrinks away,** drips | Sweet chemical | **Hard black bead** |
| **Nylon** | Melts, drips, puts itself out | Celery-like, chemical | Hard grey-tan bead |
| **Acrylic** | Melts and burns readily | Sharp chemical | Hard, irregular black bead |
| **Rayon** | Fast, like cotton | Burning paper | Little ash |

**Hard bead: it melts; keep it away from fire and do not trust it wet. Crushable bead and a hair smell:
wool; keep it.**

## Other materials

- **Canvas or duck cotton:** excellent abrasion resistance for work layers, chaps, tool rolls, aprons.
  Not a base layer, but a good wind-blocking, spark-resistant work shell in dry cold.
- **Leather:** gloves, boots, repairs. Durable and wind-blocking, slow to dry, needs conditioning with
  beeswax and oil (→ 10-04).
- **Fur and hides:** if you process animals (→ 05-09, → 05-15), hides are insulation. Tanning is a real
  skill and a long project.
- **Sheepskin and shearling:** the best boot-liner material there is.

## Saw and wood work: the saw does not negotiate

Wood heat usually means a chainsaw (→ 06-04, → 16-06). With an emergency room, saw protection is
sensible; without one, a chainsaw cut to the leg can be a barely survivable wound (→ 12-02). OSHA's
chain-saw guidance covers leg, eye and face, hearing, hand and foot protection (OSHA, Chain Saw Safety;
OSHA, Logging eTool):

- **Chainsaw chaps or cut-resistant trousers.** The thigh is where saws land. Cut-resistant fibres pull
  out and jam the chain. Check the label for the cut-resistance standard. Wear them for **every** cut,
  including "just one quick cut". **No chaps, no saw** is a sensible household rule.
- **Eyes and face:** safety glasses under a mesh face screen for saw work. Safety glasses at least for
  splitting and for hammering wedges: steel chips from a struck wedge blind people.
- **Hearing:** muffs or plugs every session. Hearing lost to a saw does not come back, and hearing is a
  security sense (→ 14-01).
- **Hands:** snug grip gloves for control; padded anti-vibration gloves on long sessions.
- **Feet:** steel or composite toe boots for felling, cutting rounds and splitting. The winter boot and
  the safety-toe work boot may have to be two different boots.
- **Nothing loose near a running saw:** no scarves, drawstrings, flapping cuffs or untucked shirts;
  long hair tied back. Snug wool or canvas outer layers are right for both fire and saw.

## Care that makes materials last

- **Wool:** cool water, gentle, no wringing, dry flat. Store clean and sealed: **moths prefer dirty
  wool.** Cedar or lavender in storage.
- **Synthetics:** wash cool; fabric softener ruins wicking. They hold odour, so they need washing more
  often.
- **Down:** keep it dry; store it **loose**, not compressed (long compression kills loft).
- **Waterproof shells:** the coating wears off; re-treat with wax or a water-repellent spray. **Once
  the shell soaks through, the whole layer system fails.**
- **Everything:** mend small holes at once (→ 11-07).

## Gear

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Merino wool base layers, 2 sets per person** | Warm when damp, low odour, comfortable | The keystone purchase |
| Wool sweaters and blankets | Layers now, raw material later | Thrift stores, surplus |
| Fleece mid layers | Fast-drying insulation | **Never near fire** |
| Nylon windproof and waterproof shells | Wind is the danger | Re-treat yearly |
| Synthetic-fill parka or vest | More reliable than down when wet | |
| Canvas work jacket or bibs | Abrasion and spark resistance | The right kind of cotton |
| Leather work gloves and conditioner | Wood, tools, wire, fire | |
| Water-repellent spray or wax bar | Restores a dead shell | |
| Mothproof storage | Wool's real enemy | Store clean |
| Darning wool, needles, patches | → 11-07 | |
| Chainsaw chaps, safety glasses and face screen, hearing protection, saw gloves, safety-toe boots | Saw and wood work | → 22-01 |

**No gear?** Wool is the top clothing scavenge: sweaters, blankets, coats, army surplus, felt. Fleece
blankets as mid layers (→ 11-07). Nylon from tents, tarps, packs and jackets for shells and repairs.
Down from comforters and old sleeping bags. Canvas from tarps, awnings and work clothes. Leather from old
coats, boots, belts and furniture. Insulation in a pinch: newspaper between layers, dry grass or cattail
fluff inside a shell, bubble wrap. **Skip** cotton for the winter system and synthetics for fire work.

## When it runs out

- **Fabric lasts a long time,** and every house nearby is full of it. In a long outage you are unlikely
  to run out of fabric if you can rework it (→ 11-07, → 16-04).
- **What does run out:** waterproof coatings (re-treat with wax), elastic (perishes), zippers (fail and
  are hard to replace: **stock spares**), thread. **Thread and needles are tiny, cheap and almost
  impossible to improvise. Stock them heavily.**
- **Protect the good things:** wear cheap layers for dirty work and save merino and the good shell for
  cold work.
- **Substitute up the chain:** wool blanket into socks, mittens, liners, poncho; sweater into children's
  clothes (→ 11-02); tarp into a shell; comforter into a parka.
- **The very long term:** hides from animals you process, and one day wool from sheep where land allows.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** sort by fibre, purge cotton from the winter kit, stock wool, set aside fire clothing.
- **B (moving):** wool and synthetics only; nothing cotton. A windproof shell is worth more than an extra
  insulation layer in wind. Keep the spare dry base layer sealed in a bag.
- **C (established somewhere else):** more abrasion, more fire work, more wet: canvas, leather and wool
  earn their keep, and reworking fabric becomes routine.
