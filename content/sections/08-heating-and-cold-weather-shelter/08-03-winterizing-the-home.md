# Winterizing the home and preventing frozen pipes

**What this page is for.** The cheap, boring work done in autumn that does more for a winter
outage than any gadget: protecting the pipes, sealing draughts, insulating, and knowing how to
drain the house if it has to go cold. A burst pipe in an unheated house wrecks the building and
your water supply at the same time.

> **CAUTION.** **Never thaw a pipe with an open flame** (Red Cross; → 03-06). A snow-buried
> exhaust vent pushes carbon monoxide back into the house: clear vents after every storm
> (→ 08-02). **Never use automotive antifreeze in drains or toilets** (below).

## Do this now (before the cold)

- **Outdoor taps:** drain and store hoses. Close the inside shutoff valve to each outdoor tap,
  then **open the outdoor tap and leave it open** so trapped water can expand without splitting
  the pipe (Red Cross).
- **Insulate pipes** in the basement, crawlspace, garage and along outside walls, hot and cold
  lines both (Red Cross).
- **Seal leaks:** weatherstrip doors, caulk window gaps, draught stoppers, foam gaskets behind
  outlet covers on outside walls.
- **Insulate the attic.** Most heat leaves through the top of the house.
- **Find the main water shutoff**, keep the tool with it, and practise turning it in the dark.
- **Stage the warm-room kit now** (→ 08-01): blankets, hooks, window film.

## Pipes

Water expands as it freezes and splits metal or plastic pipe. The breaks happen first in
**unheated space:** outside walls, crawlspaces, garages and cold basement corners. In deep cold,
**let vulnerable taps drip** (moving water resists freezing) and **open the cabinet doors under
sinks** so house heat reaches the pipes (Red Cross).

**In a winter outage the whole house becomes unheated space.** Either keep enough heat
everywhere to protect the plumbing, which costs a great deal of fuel, or **drain the system on
purpose** (→ 03-06) and live without running water. For a long outage, draining is usually
right, but **fill every container first** (→ 03-02).

{{#if has.water_heater_tank}}### The water heater when the house is drained

A tank water heater ({{ dwelling.water_heater_gallons }} gallons on file) is the biggest volume of
water left to freeze and split, and it is **drinkable reserve water first** (→ 03-02). In order:

1. **Turn its heat off.** {{#if has.gas_any}}Gas: control knob to **OFF**, not "vacation" or
   "pilot". The gas shutoff procedure is on → 16-12.{{else}}Electric: its breaker off.{{/if}}
2. **Close the cold-water inlet valve** on top of the tank.
3. **Wait.** The water stays scalding for hours. Once it is safe to touch, draw it off through the
   drain valve into clean containers, or run a hose from the drain valve to a floor drain and
   open a hot tap upstairs so air can enter.
4. **Restart only after it is full again,** with the inlet open, and only by the instructions on
   its label. Heating an empty tank destroys it.
   Relighting a gas water heater's pilot yourself when the gas company cannot come is a
   decision card: {{choice DC-28}} → 30-29.

{{/if}}### Antifreeze for drains and toilets

When a house is drained, pour antifreeze into sink traps and toilet bowls so the water left in
them cannot freeze and crack them. **Use only pink RV or marine antifreeze, which is propylene
glycol.** **Never automotive antifreeze (ethylene glycol):** a small amount is deadly to a child
or a pet, and it tastes sweet (ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center). A toilet bowl or floor drain
is exactly where a child or a cat will find it.

## The building envelope, biggest gains first

| Fix | Effort | Payoff |
|---|---|---|
| Attic insulation | High | The largest single saving |
| Window film, plastic sheeting, bubble wrap | Low | Very high per dollar |
| Weatherstripping and door sweeps | Low | High: draughts are constant loss |
| Caulking gaps, outlet gaskets | Low | Moderate but adds up |
| Heavy curtains, closed at night | Low | Real and immediate |
| Blankets over unused doorways and stairs | Low | Essential for the warm room (→ 08-01) |
| Rugs and foam on bare floors | Low | Comfort and real loss reduction |

Storm windows and thermal curtains pay for themselves in a normal winter and matter a great deal
in an abnormal one. **Bubble wrap pressed onto glass misted with water** is an effective, cheap
substitute.

## Snow and the house

- **Clear exhaust vents after every storm:** furnace, water heater, dryer. A buried vent pushes
  CO inside (→ 08-02).
- **Watch the roof load** after heavy wet snow, especially low-slope sections and porch roofs.
- **Ice dams:** heat leaking into the attic melts snow that refreezes at the eaves and backs water
  under the shingles. Good attic insulation and ventilation prevent them.
- **Keep paths clear** to the woodpile, the generator spot and the vehicle. In a blizzard, the
  path you did not clear yesterday is the one you cannot use today (→ 17-01).

## Before a forecast storm

1. Fill every water container and the bathtub (CDC; → 03-02).
2. Charge everything, medical devices first (→ 09-06).
3. Bring several days of firewood under cover.
4. Stage the warm room (→ 08-01).
5. Check CO and smoke alarm batteries (→ 08-02).
6. Move medicines that must not freeze or overheat to the safe spot (→ 29-07).

## Gear

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Pipe insulation sleeves; heat tape listed by a testing lab | Prevents the costly failure | Hot and cold lines |
| Window film kits or bubble wrap | Cheapest large gain | Enough for the warm room at least |
| Weatherstrip, door sweeps, caulk, outlet gaskets | Stops constant loss | Autumn job |
| Thermal curtains, blankets and hooks | Night insulation and closing off rooms | Also the warm-room kit |
| Main water shutoff tool, kept at the valve | Drain fast when needed | |
| **RV or marine antifreeze, pink, propylene glycol only** | Protects traps and toilets in a drained house | Never automotive (ethylene glycol) |
| Roof rake | Snow load and ice dams | Long handle, used from the ground |
| Two or more snow shovels | Vents, wood, ways out | One stays inside by the door |

**No gear?** Plastic sheeting, cardboard and tape over windows; foam pool noodles and rags as
pipe wrap; rolled towels as draught stoppers; furniture against outside walls; any blanket on a
few nails becomes a door curtain.

## When it runs out

- **Insulation does not run out.** That is why it is the best investment. The consumables here
  (tape, film, antifreeze) are minor.
- **Short of materials?** Insulate the **warm room first**, then the pipes that matter, then the
  rest.
- **Resupply:** cardboard, cloth and plastic sheeting are everywhere and all work.
- **Every bit of insulation permanently cuts the fuel you burn** (→ 06-04).

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** do all of it in autumn. It is the highest return per dollar and hour in the
  binder.
- **B (moving):** the skill that travels is insulating a small space fast: the same tricks work in
  a vehicle or a borrowed room.
- **C (established somewhere else):** on day one, check the building: where the wind gets in,
  where the pipes run, and where the snow drifts.
