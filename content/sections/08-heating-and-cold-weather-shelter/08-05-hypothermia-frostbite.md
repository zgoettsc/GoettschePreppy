# Hypothermia and frostbite: prevent, spot, treat

**What this page is for.** How to recognise and treat the two cold injuries: **hypothermia**, when
the whole body gets too cold, and **frostbite**, when skin and the tissue under it freeze. In an
outage both happen indoors as well as out. Keep the "Do this now" list where everyone can see it.

> **SAFETY.** Hypothermia is a **medical emergency**. A body temperature below **95 °F** is
> hypothermia (CDC). **Confusion is a symptom,** so the person affected is the least able to
> notice it: someone else has to watch. **Severe hypothermia can look like death: handle the
> person gently, keep warming, and do CPR anyway** (CDC). Never rewarm frostbite with a fire,
> stove, heater, heating pad or electric blanket: numb skin burns without feeling it (CDC).

## Do this now

1. **Watch others, not yourself.** Adults: shivering, exhaustion, confusion, **fumbling hands**,
   memory loss, slurred speech, drowsiness. Babies: **bright red, cold skin and very low energy**
   (CDC). A quiet, floppy baby in a cold house is an emergency, not a good sleeper.
2. **Hypothermia:** into warmth, **wet clothes off**, warm the **centre first** (chest, neck, head,
   groin) with dry layers and **skin-to-skin contact**. Warm sweet drinks only if fully awake. **No
   alcohol.** Nothing by mouth if not alert (CDC).
3. **Frostbite: do not rub it,** do not rub snow on it, do not warm it at a fire. Use **warm, not
   hot, water: 98.6 to 102 °F (37 to 39 °C) by thermometer,** or, with no thermometer, water an
   unhurt hand can stay in for 30 seconds without discomfort; or body heat such as an armpit.
   Rewarming is done at **about 30 minutes,** when the part is red or purple and soft again
   (Wilderness Medical Society guideline, as summarised by AAFP).
4. **Both at once? Treat the hypothermia first.**
5. **No pulse or breathing you can find? CPR, and keep warming** (CDC; → 12-18).
6. **The stop line:** when your NWS office ({{ region.home.nws_office }}) issues an **Extreme Cold
   Warning**, nobody goes outside except for essential minutes, and children stay in (→ 29-10).
   Your office's criterion: wind chill at or below ______ °F.

## Hypothermia

The body loses heat faster than it makes it. Below 95 °F core temperature, thinking and
coordination go first, then consciousness. **Wet speeds it up:** CDC notes hypothermia can happen
even above 40 °F if someone is chilled by rain, sweat or cold water. That is why sweating while
hauling wood in winter is dangerous (→ 11-03), and why a soaked child on a cool spring day is at
risk too.

**CDC lists older people with poor food or heating, babies sleeping in cold bedrooms, people who
stay outdoors a long time, and people who drink alcohol among those most at risk** (CDC,
Preventing Hypothermia). A grid-down winter house fits that list.

**Treatment, in order (CDC):**

1. Get the person into a warm room or shelter.
2. **Take off wet clothing.**
3. **Warm the centre of the body first** (chest, neck, head, groin) with dry blankets, clothing,
   towels, or **skin-to-skin contact** under loose dry layers.
4. Warm, sweet, non-alcoholic drinks **only if the person is fully awake.**
5. Afterwards, keep them dry and wrapped, head and neck included.
6. **Get medical help** if any exists.

**Do not:** rewarm fast in a hot bath, give alcohol, give anything by mouth to someone not alert,
or let them "walk it off".

**Severe hypothermia: do not assume death.** CDC says a person with severe hypothermia may be
unconscious and **may not seem to have a pulse or to be breathing.** Handle them gently, get help,
and **do CPR even if the person seems dead**, continuing until they respond or help arrives, and
**keep warming them during CPR** (CDC). Cold and apparently dead is not the same as dead.

## Frostbite

Freezing of skin and the tissue under it, usually the **nose, ears, cheeks, chin, fingers and
toes**. First signs: **redness or pain**. Then numbness, skin that feels waxy or firm, and loss of
colour (CDC). **Numbness is the danger:** people keep working and make it far worse. How fast
exposed skin freezes depends on temperature and wind together; read it from the NWS wind chill
chart (→ 11-01).

**Treatment cautions (CDC):**

- **Do not rub** the area or rub snow on it.
- **Do not warm it with a fireplace, heat lamp, radiator, stove, heating pad or electric
  blanket.** It is numb and burns easily.
- **Do not walk on frostbitten feet or toes** unless you must.

**Rewarming (Wilderness Medical Society guideline, as summarised by AAFP):**

- Water at **98.6 to 102 °F (37 to 39 °C).** The injured person cannot judge "warm", so **check the
  water with a thermometer, or test it for 30 seconds with an unhurt hand** (it should feel warm,
  never hot). Top it up as it cools.
- **About 30 minutes** is the usual end point: the part turns red or purple and becomes soft.
  Expect real pain as feeling returns.
- Afterwards: dry, loose, clean dressing, gauze between fingers or toes, raise the limb, no walking
  on it.
- **Do not thaw if it might freeze again.** Refreezing does far worse damage than staying frozen
  until you can rewarm for good. This is a real choice when travelling (→ 01-03).
- **If hypothermia and frostbite are both present, treat the hypothermia first.** Core before
  extremities.

## Prevention is the whole game

- **Layer and stay dry** (→ 11-01, → 11-03). Sweat is the enemy; vent before you overheat.
- **Cover the extremities:** hat, a scarf or knit mask over face and mouth, mittens (warmer than
  gloves), water-resistant coat and boots, several loose layers (CDC; → 11-04).
- **Eat and drink.** Food makes heat; people get dehydrated in winter because they do not feel
  thirsty (→ 03-01).
- **Work in shifts and check each other's faces** every few minutes in hard cold. You cannot see
  your own white cheek.
- **A hard rule for children:** a time limit outside set before going out, and inside at once if
  any clothing gets wet.

## Gear

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Thermometer that reads **low** temperatures | Ordinary fever thermometers may not read the hypothermia range | Needed to confirm below 95 °F |
| A thermometer for water | Frostbite rewarming water must be 98.6 to 102 °F | A kitchen or bath thermometer |
| Wool blankets, cold-rated sleeping bags | Rewarming and sleeping warm | Wool insulates when damp |
| Chemical hand and body warmers | Heat with no fire | **Over clothing only, never against skin, never on numb skin, never inside a small child's mittens or boots:** they get hot enough to burn skin that cannot feel or pull away |
| A dry change of clothes per person | "Take off wet clothing" needs something dry to put on | |
| Emergency reflective blankets or bivvies | Hold body heat | Cheap and tiny |
| Room thermometer | Know how cold the house really is | Especially where a baby sleeps |
| Spare hats, mittens, face masks | Extremity cover is the first defence | |
| Makings for warm sweet drinks | Rewarming a conscious person | Also morale |

**No gear?** Another person's body heat is the most reliable rewarming tool there is: skin to skin
under dry layers. Newspaper, cardboard and dry leaves are real insulation inside clothing and
bedding.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** hand warmers and dry clothing are the consumables here; fuel is the deeper one,
  because a warm room prevents all of this (→ 08-01).
- **Ration:** when fuel is short, heat the **people**, not the space: layers, shared body heat, a
  small sleeping area (→ 08-06). Never let a baby's sleeping space go cold.
- **The quiet failure:** everyone gets a little colder each day, judgment slips, and nobody notices.
  In a cold house, take temperatures and look at each other's faces daily.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** most outage hypothermia happens indoors. Keep one warm room (→ 08-01) and check
  children and elders often.
- **B (moving):** the highest-risk activity in winter. Sweat, wind and wet feet are the killers;
  do not thaw frostbite if it may refreeze.
- **C (established somewhere else):** more exposure, more distance from help, harder work. Face
  checks and dry spares become routine.
