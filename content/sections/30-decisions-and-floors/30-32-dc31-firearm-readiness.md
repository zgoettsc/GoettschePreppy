# DC-31. How the household keeps firearms ready

**What this page is for.** A household that keeps firearms for protection has to decide how
quickly an adult can reach one, and how that fits with children in the house. Faster access
and child safety pull against each other. This card lays out the facts, the three common ways
households answer, and the rules each one needs so that children still cannot reach a
firearm. The household decides; every firearms page points here. New to firearms? Read → 29-11
first.

> **SAFETY.** **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine
> products.** That is a floor (→ 30-01), not part of this card: every option below must meet
> it, every hour of every day. **A loaded firearm loose in a drawer, under a pillow, on a shelf,
> in a vehicle or in a bag is not one of the options.**

## 1. The thing, in plain words

**Ready** means how many seconds stand between an adult and a usable firearm. **Loaded** means
a cartridge is in the firearm; a firearm with its magazine out can still have one in the
**chamber** (→ 29-11). A **gun safe** or locked cabinet holds firearms behind a lock. A
**quick-access lockbox** is a small locked box that opens fast with a keypad, a fingerprint
reader or a key. A **holster** is a fitted case worn on the body that covers the trigger and
holds the firearm in place.

## 2. The rule, and why it was set

- **AAP:** the safest home for a child is one without guns. If you keep guns, all of them
  should be **locked and unloaded, with ammunition locked separately**; children and teens must
  not be able to reach the keys or combinations; and no loaded, unlocked gun in the car or
  anywhere else on the property (American Academy of Pediatrics, Guns in the Home).
- **NSSF:** "Firearms should be loaded only when you are in the field or on the target range or
  shooting area, ready to shoot." When not in use, "firearms and ammunition should be secured in
  a safe place, separate from each other", and "It is your responsibility to prevent children
  and unauthorized adults from gaining access to firearms or ammunition" (NSSF, Firearm safety:
  rules for safe gun handling, rule 2).

**Why:** a study of shootings by children and teens who got hold of a household gun found that
keeping the gun locked, keeping it unloaded, storing ammunition separately and locking the
ammunition were **each** linked to a lower risk of suicide and accidental injury, for handguns
and long guns alike (Grossman and others, JAMA, 2005). Each layer counts on its own.

**What the rule costs.** A locked, unloaded firearm with its ammunition elsewhere takes time to
reach and load in the dark. Some owners keep one ready for that reason. NSSF's storage guide
lists quick-access electronic lock boxes among its storage options for that need (NSSF Project
ChildSafe, Gun Storage for Your Lifestyle).

## 3. How it actually hurts people

CDC studied 1,262 accidental shooting deaths of children aged 0 to 17 in the US from 2003 to
2021 (CDC, MMWR, Unintentional Firearm Injury Deaths Among Children and Adolescents, 2023):

- Where storage was known, the gun had been stored **loaded in 74%** of deaths and **unlocked
  in 76%**. Handguns were 74% of the guns.
- The unlocked gun was most often taken from **a nightstand, under a mattress or pillow, or on a
  bed (30%)**, a shelf or closet (18.6%), **a vehicle (12.5%)**, and **a handbag, backpack, gym
  bag, purse or clothing (8.8%)**.
- About two thirds of the shooters were playing with the gun or showing it to someone. About
  one in five thought it was unloaded, the safety was on or the magazine was out. About one in
  ten mistook it for a toy, more than one in four among children aged 5 and under.
- Nearly half of the guns belonged to a parent of the child who fired. 7% of deaths happened
  while a gun was being loaded, unloaded or cleaned.

AAP adds that even toddlers can find an unlocked gun and are strong enough to pull the trigger,
that teaching children not to touch a gun is not enough, and that **teenagers** are at risk too:
youth suicide is four times higher in homes with guns, and about nine in ten youth gun suicides
use a gun from their own or a relative's home (AAP, Guns in the Home). In a long crisis, teens
are home, frightened and grieving (→ 12-15).

**Lockboxes fail too.** CPSC recalled a line of fingerprint gun safes because people whose
prints were not enrolled could open them; it reports 39 such incidents and a lawsuit alleging a
12-year-old died from a gun taken from one (CPSC, Fortress biometric gun safe recall, 2023).

**Carrying has its own failure.** NSSF warns that people have left a firearm where others
could reach it "because they forgot the firearm was in their bag", and that "off your body"
does not mean "out of mind"; a carried handgun should be in a holster designed to protect the
trigger, especially in a pocket or purse where pens and other objects can catch it (NSSF, Let's
Go Shooting, Safe Storage: More Than a Steel Box and Combination Lock).

## 4. What makes it safer

**Removes most of the risk**
- **Every firearm unloaded and locked, ammunition locked separately**, keys and combinations
  out of children's reach (AAP; NSSF). Practise reaching and loading from the safe (→ 14-15).

**Reduces it a lot**
- **Only one firearm is ever ready.** All the others are locked and unloaded, with ammunition
  apart, whatever option is chosen.
- **Ready means locked or on an adult's body, never in between.** No nightstand, no shelf, no
  pillow, no glovebox, no bag, no "for a minute" on the counter.
- **Load and unload only at the safe or box**, muzzle in a safe direction, with no child in the
  room.
- **Lockboxes:** fixed down, out of sight and reach, always shut; a code that is not a birthday;
  children never watch it opened; backup key hidden from children; test monthly that an
  unenrolled finger does not open it and that a child's shaking, prying or random button-pushing
  does not; batteries on a schedule (→ 14-14).
- **Carried:** a holster that covers the trigger and holds the firearm firmly on a belt, worn by
  one named adult; never off-body in a bag or coat; never handed over or set down; at sleep,
  bath, illness, drink or visitors, it goes straight into a locked box.
- **In a vehicle, the same two states:** a ready firearm is in a locked box fixed to the
  vehicle or on the adult's body; never in a door pocket, console, glove box, under a seat or in
  a bag (CDC: a vehicle was the source in 12.5% of the deaths above). Every other firearm travels
  unloaded, cased and locked, ammunition apart (→ 14-11, → 22-05).
- **Warning layers** (→ 14-12) so that a slower option is fast enough: dogs, alarms, a
  neighbour watch.

**Reduces it a little**
- Teach every child the stop rule: stop, don't touch, leave the area, tell a grown-up
  (→ 14-14, → 20-04). Useful, but AAP says it is not enough on its own.
- Chamber flags and dry practice with an empty, checked firearm (→ 14-15).
- When someone in the house is in crisis, especially a teen, move every firearm to the most
  locked state, or out of the house, until it passes (→ 12-15).

## 5. The options

Each option keeps every other firearm locked, unloaded, with ammunition locked separately.

**`locked-safe`: All firearms locked, unloaded, ammunition apart.**
*Child-exposure rules:* safe or cabinet always locked; ammunition in a second locked place; keys
and combinations kept from children and never written in this binder; nothing left out after
cleaning or practice.
You give up: speed. Reaching and loading takes the longest of the three. You accept: that the
warning layers and practice must buy that time.

**`quick-access-lockbox`: One loaded in a quick-access lockbox, the rest locked.**
*Child-exposure rules:* the box is always shut and locked, fixed down, out of sight and out of
reach; children never see the code or watch it opened; it is tested monthly against an
unenrolled finger and against a child's tampering; batteries changed on a schedule; any backup
key hidden from children; one box per adult who needs it, never a box left open "for tonight".
You give up: the AAP and NSSF rule that firearms are stored unloaded. You accept: that one
loaded firearm in the house is guarded only by the box, its code and the adults' habit, and that
boxes can fail.

**`carried-on-person`: One carried on an adult's body, the rest locked.**
*Child-exposure rules:* one named adult carries at a time, in a holster that covers the trigger
and holds the firearm firmly on the belt; never in a bag, purse, pocket or coat that can be
taken off; never set down, handed to a child or "shown"; whenever it comes off the body (sleep,
bathing, illness, alcohol, visitors), it goes straight into a locked box or the safe; loading
and unloading only at that box, with no child in the room.
You give up: the AAP and NSSF rule, and the adult's freedom to take it off casually. You accept:
that a loaded firearm is within arm's length of every child the adult hugs, carries or plays
with, guarded only by the holster and the adult's attention, all day, when adults are tired.
