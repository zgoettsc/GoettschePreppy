# DC-29. One traction attempt on a fractured limb with no pulse below it, when no care is coming

**What this page is for.** The usual first-aid rule for a broken bone is "splint it as you find
it". This card is for one narrow case: a deformed broken arm or leg whose hand or foot has **lost
its blood supply**, when **no care is coming**. It explains what published first-aid and
wilderness-medicine guidance says, the risks either way, and what the household decides. It is a
reminder for trained hands, not the training.

> **SAFETY**
> - **If any care can be reached, go now** (→ 12-16). This card is for when it cannot.
> - **Never on a suspected spine injury**: "DO NOT try to reposition a suspected spine injury"
>   (MedlinePlus, Broken Bone). **Do not move a person with an injured hip, pelvis or upper leg
>   unless it is absolutely necessary** (MedlinePlus). **Bone showing through the skin:** cover it,
>   and "don't try to line up the fracture unless you are medically trained to do so" (MedlinePlus;
>   → 12-09); this card does not cover it.
> - **Stop any serious bleeding first** (→ 12-02). Never loosen a tourniquet once placed while
>   help can still come; mark the time. If no help will ever come, the conversion decision is
>   DC-25 (→ 30-26).

## 1. The thing, in plain words

- A **fracture** is a broken bone. A **deformed** fracture is one where the limb is bent, kinked
  or shortened where it should be straight.
- **Circulation** is the blood reaching the hand or foot. The check (MedlinePlus): press firmly on
  the skin beyond the break; it should go white, then pink up again in about 2 seconds after you
  let go. **Signs that circulation is poor:** pale or blue skin, numbness or tingling, loss of
  pulse.
- A **pulse** is the beat you feel where an artery is near the skin: the thumb side of the wrist,
  or the top of the foot and behind the inner ankle bone. Practise finding them on a healthy
  person now.
- **In-line traction** is a gentle, steady pull along the long line of the limb, which lets the
  broken ends ease back toward their normal position.

## 2. The rule, and why it was set

- **MedlinePlus** (Broken Bone, US National Library of Medicine): "DO NOT attempt to straighten a
  bone or change its position unless blood circulation appears hampered and no medically trained
  personnel are nearby." And: "If circulation is poor and trained personnel are NOT quickly
  available, try to realign the limb into a normal resting position. This will reduce swelling,
  pain, and damage to the tissues from lack of blood."
- **Wilderness first aid** (Minimum Guidelines and Scope of Practice for Wilderness First Aid,
  December 2012 version, published in Wilderness & Environmental Medicine, the Wilderness Medical
  Society's journal, 2013): treat unstable injuries with "gentle traction into position for
  angulated long bones" (bent breaks of the long bones of the arm or leg), and splints that "allow
  for ongoing monitoring of perfusion" (blood flow). The same standard lists "persistently
  impaired CSM" (circulation, sensation, movement) as a high-risk problem that needs evacuation.
- **Military prolonged-care guidance** (Joint Trauma System, Prolonged Casualty Care, 2021):
  "Re-check all pulses after splint placement."

**Why the normal rule is "splint as found":** with care nearby, a trained team with pain relief,
X-rays and a surgeon does the moving. Moving a broken bone hurts, and without training it is easy
to move the wrong thing. The exception exists because a limb with no blood is being damaged every
hour, and no team is coming to save it.

## 3. How it actually hurts people

**Not doing it:** the leg is splinted carefully, bent as it was found. The foot stays white, cold
and pulseless. With no blood, the tissue is damaged further every hour (MedlinePlus), and with no
surgeon a dying limb cannot be treated and becomes a source of infection (→ 12-04).

**Doing it badly:** a hard, fast yank instead of a gentle pull; a second and third try; a pull on
a hip, a pelvis, a spine or a joint injury; pulling on a break with bone showing, which drags dirt
back into the wound. Each adds pain and can add damage.

**Doing it as published:** one gentle pull, the pulse comes back, the limb is splinted in the
better position. Or the pulse does not come back, and the limb is splinted as it now lies.

## 4. What makes it safer

**Removes the question**
- **Reaching care.** Any clinic, surgeon or trained responder who can be reached (→ 12-16).

**Reduces it a lot**
- **Hands-on training.** Wilderness first-aid and first-responder courses teach traction into
  position on a person, not from a page. Take one now (→ 24-01).
- **Check circulation before and after**, every time: colour, warmth, the press-and-release test,
  pulse, feeling, wiggle. Write it down with the time (→ 12-09).

**Reduces it a little**
- Pain relief first, if any is available (→ 12-13).
- A second person to hold the limb above the break steady while the pull is made.
- A splint ready to go on immediately afterwards, padded, with room to re-check the pulse.

### What the card's option means in practice

**One** gentle, slow, steady pull along the line of the limb, toward its normal position. If it
meets firm resistance, stop. Then re-check colour, warmth and pulse, splint the limb in the
position it is now in, and re-check the pulse again after splinting (Joint Trauma System). If the
pulse has not come back, do not try again: splint it as it lies, keep the person warm (→ 08-05),
and make the move-toward-care decision now (→ 12-16).

The sources say only "gentle" (WFA) and "try to realign" (MedlinePlus). **The limits of one pull
and stopping at firm resistance are this card's own, not a published rule**: they are there to
keep an untrained hand from doing more harm.

## 5. The options

**`splint-as-found`: Splint as found: never pull on a broken limb.**
You give up: the chance of restoring blood to the limb. You accept: that the hand or foot may be
lost, in return for never risking added injury from moving the break.

**`one-gentle-traction`: One gentle traction attempt, then splint.**
You give up: the simple "never move it" rule. You accept: pain and a risk of added injury from one
gentle pull, made only in the case on this card, by the best-trained person present.
