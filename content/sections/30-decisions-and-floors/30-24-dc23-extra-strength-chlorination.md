# DC-23. Chlorinating very dirty water beyond the published dose

**What this page is for.** Some emergency water is clear; some is brown. The published chlorine
doses assume you clear the water first. This card explains why, what happens when people try to
make up for dirt with more chlorine, and what the household decides. The published doses
themselves are on → 03-04.

> **SAFETY.** Bleach or pool shock is never mixed with ammonia, acids or other cleaners (floor,
> → 30-01). Water that may hold fuel or toxic chemicals cannot be made safe by chlorine or boiling
> (CDC); see → 30-14. Concentrated pool-shock solution is itself a poison: label it, keep it away
> from children (floor 9).

## 1. The thing, in plain words

Read → 29-05 and → 29-06 first if these words are new.
- **Chlorine disinfection** means adding household bleach, or a solution made from pool shock, to
  water, then waiting so the chlorine kills germs.
- **Turbidity** is cloudiness: mud, silt, plant bits floating in the water.
- **Chlorine demand** is the chlorine the dirt uses up before any is left to kill germs.
- **Residual** is chlorine left over after the wait; you can smell it. No smell can mean the dirt
  used it all.

## 2. The rule, and why it was set

- CDC and EPA: if water is cloudy, **let it settle and filter it** through cloth, paper towel or a
  coffee filter first; then add the published dose of unscented bleach; **double the dose if the
  water is still cloudy, coloured or very cold**; stir and wait 30 minutes. EPA adds: the water
  should have a slight chlorine smell; if not, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes (CDC, How
  to Make Water Safe in an Emergency; EPA, Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water).
- Follow the bleach label if it gives drinking-water directions.

**Why:** chlorine works on what it can reach. Germs inside or behind dirt particles are shielded,
and dirt reacts with chlorine and uses it up. Clearing the water first is what lets the published
dose work.

## 3. How it actually hurts people

- **Under-disinfection**: muddy water gets a big dose, smells of chlorine for a while, and is
  drunk, but germs inside particles survived. Diarrhoea follows; in a baby, an elder or someone with
  diabetes, dehydration becomes an emergency (→ 12-05).
- **Parasites**: Cryptosporidium survives ordinary chlorine doses; boiling or a fine filter is
  needed (CDC Yellow Book, Water Disinfection for Travelers).
- **Too much chlorine**: water that tastes and smells harsh, which people then avoid drinking, and
  stomach upset. Large mistakes with concentrated pool-shock solution burn the mouth and stomach
  (CDC/ATSDR, Calcium Hypochlorite).

## 4. What makes it safer

**Removes most of the germ risk**
- **Boil** water that has been settled and filtered (CDC), when fuel allows.
- **Choose cleaner water**: rain, snow, or the clearest source available (→ 30-14).

**Reduces it a lot**
- **Settle, then filter**: let a bucket stand for hours, pour off the clear top, run it through
  cloth and then a fine filter (→ 03-09).
- **Then the published dose**, doubled only if still cloudy, coloured or very cold, with the full
  wait and the smell check (→ 03-04).
- **Measure**: a dropper or measuring spoon, not a guess; write the bleach strength on the bottle.

**Reduces it a little**
- Pour treated water between two clean containers, or let it stand uncovered a few hours, to soften
  the taste.
- Chlorine test strips, if you have them (→ 03-07).

## 5. The options

**`label-dose-and-filter-first`: Clear it first, then the published dose.**
You give up: time and filter material; settling takes hours. You accept: a slower supply.

**`double-dose-and-wait`: Heavy dose and a longer wait when it cannot be cleared.**
You give up: the clearing step. You accept: harsh-tasting water and the risk that germs hidden in
the dirt, and Cryptosporidium, survive.
