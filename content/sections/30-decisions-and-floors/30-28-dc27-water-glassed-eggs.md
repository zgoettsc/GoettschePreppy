# DC-27. Water-glassing fresh eggs (storing them in lime water)

**What this page is for.** Hens lay most in summer and least in winter, which is when a household
with no fridge most wants eggs. Water-glassing is the traditional way of carrying summer eggs into
winter. The published food-safety advice says do not do it. This card explains why, what the
household gives up either way, and what it decides.

> **SAFETY.** **Never taste food to decide whether it is safe.** Children do not have access to
> firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products (floor, → 30-01); keep pickling lime sealed,
> labelled and out of reach as well. **Egg is one of the nine major food allergens** (FDA, Food
> Allergies): no stored egg goes to anyone allergic to egg (→ 02-04).

## 1. The thing, in plain words

- **Water-glassing** means putting fresh, unwashed eggs in a container filled with a solution of
  water and **pickling lime** (calcium hydroxide, sold for pickling), or in the older version,
  sodium silicate ("water glass"), and keeping them there for months.
- A freshly laid egg has a natural coating called the **bloom** that seals the shell. Washing
  removes it, which is why the method uses only unwashed eggs (→ 04-15).
- ***Salmonella*** is a germ that causes food poisoning. It can be inside an egg that looks
  perfect.

## 2. The rule, and why it was set

- **Utah State University Extension** (Storing Eggs Safely, 2023): water-glassing "is not
  considered safe, nor is it a recommended method to store eggs for the long term." Its
  conclusion: "do not use water glassing for egg storage."
- **Kansas State University Research and Extension** (Storing Eggs Safely, 2020): storing eggs in
  lime water "is not recommended."
- **Penn State Extension** (How to Safely Handle and Store Eggs from Your Home Flock, 2024): water
  glassing is "not recommended."
- **FDA** (What You Need to Know About Egg Safety): keep eggs at **40 °F or below**, cook eggs
  until **both yolk and white are firm**, and cook egg dishes to **160 °F**.

**Why they say no:**
- The method was popular when people thought eggs were sterile inside and all germs came through
  the shell after laying. Research shows *Salmonella* can get into the egg while it forms inside
  the hen, and "there is no way a person can know if it is there" (Utah State; Kansas State).
- Unwashed eggs usually come from small flocks, and a Penn State study found small home flocks
  have a much higher rate of *Salmonella* than large commercial ones (Utah State; Kansas State).
- The membrane under the shell lets water pass, so the solution and any germs in it can get into
  the egg (Penn State). The lime itself can seep through the shell (Kansas State; Utah State).
- Studies from the 1940s found water-glassing did not keep egg quality at room temperatures of
  40 to 95 °F, and a home pantry or basement is unlikely to stay at 40 °F or below, so germs
  already in the egg have time to grow (Penn State).

## 3. How it actually hurts people

Months into winter, an egg from the bucket looks and smells normal. It is used in soft scrambled
eggs or a batter tasted before baking. Between 12 and 72 hours later: diarrhoea, fever, stomach
cramps and vomiting, usually for 4 to 7 days. Most people recover without treatment, but in some
the infection spreads into the blood, which "can cause death unless the person is treated quickly
with antibiotics" (FDA). Children, older adults, pregnant women and people with weak immune
systems (including people with diabetes) are at greatest risk (FDA). With no hospital, the
dehydration alone is dangerous for a small child or an elder (→ 12-05).

**The other side:** with no fridge and no freezer, refusing to store eggs may mean no eggs at all
from late autumn to spring, and the loss of a good source of protein and fat.

## 4. What makes it safer

**Removes the storage risk** (stored eggs are still cooked thoroughly: freezing and freeze-drying
do not kill *Salmonella*, Utah State)
- **Freeze eggs out of the shell** while you have a freezer or measured hard-freeze cold
  (→ 04-09). The National Center for Home Food Preservation's method: break each egg separately
  into a clean saucer and check it, mix yolk and white without whipping, add sugar, corn syrup or
  salt against graininess as its page lists, and leave ½ inch of headspace (NCHFP, Freezing Eggs).
- **Cook first, then dry** (→ 04-06). Cooking settles the *Salmonella* question before storage.
- **Refrigerate** at 40 °F or below while you can (FDA).

**Reduces it a lot**
- **Cook every stored egg until yolk and white are firm**, and egg dishes to 160 °F (FDA). Utah
  State says the same for frozen and freeze-dried eggs: cook them thoroughly, never in uncooked
  foods.
- **Check every egg** before use: candle it, crack it into a separate bowl, smell it, and throw out
  any that is cracked, floats, or is off (→ 04-15).
- **Keep stored eggs from the people at greatest risk** named above, and from anyone allergic to
  egg.

**Reduces it a little**
- Only clean, unwashed, uncracked, fresh eggs.
- The coolest spot available, checked with a thermometer, and the shortest storage time you can
  manage. None of the Extension sources gives a "safe" storage time, because they do not consider
  the method safe at any length.

## 5. The options

**`do-not`: Do not water-glass: follow the Extension position.**
You give up: eggs through the winter when there is no cold storage. You accept: relying on
freezing, drying, the hens' winter laying, trade, or doing without.

**`water-glass-with-rules`: Water-glass with rules.**
You give up: the published safety position; there is no tested recipe and no tested storage
time. You accept: that some stored eggs may carry *Salmonella*, that thorough cooking is the main
protection, and that every rule in the summary above is kept every time.
