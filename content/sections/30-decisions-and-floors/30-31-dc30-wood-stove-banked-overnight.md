# DC-30. Keeping a wood stove or fireplace burning while people sleep

**What this page is for.** On a winter night with no power, a wood fire that lasts until
morning is tempting: the house stays warmer, and nobody has to relight a fire at dawn. The US
Fire Administration says put the fire out before you sleep. Many stove makers explain how to
run an overnight burn. This card explains both sides and what the household decides. Every
wood-heat page points here.

> **SAFETY.** Never run a generator, charcoal, or any engine indoors, in a garage, or near an
> open window (floor, → 30-01). That is not this card: this card is only about a wood stove or
> fireplace with a working chimney. Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications
> or chlorine products (floor, → 30-01). If a CO alarm sounds, or anyone wakes with a headache,
> dizziness or nausea: everyone out into fresh air, → 08-02. If the chimney roars or glows: →
> 06-09.

## 1. The thing, in plain words

**Banking** a fire means setting it up to burn slowly for many hours: a bed of coals, a large
load of wood, and less air so it burns slower. In the morning there are still live coals to
restart from (→ 06-02).

A **wood stove** is a closed metal box with a door and an **air control**, a slide or knob that
sets how much air reaches the fire. A **damper** is a plate in the chimney or stovepipe that
opens or closes the flue. An **open fireplace** has no door; a **fireplace insert** is a stove
built into a fireplace opening.

**Creosote** is the black or brown tar that smoke leaves on the inside of the chimney. It
burns, and a chimney fire can reach about 2000 °F (CSIA, Preventing Chimney Fires). **Carbon
monoxide (CO)** is a gas you cannot see or smell that stops the blood from carrying oxygen
(→ 29-03).

## 2. The rule, and why the agency set it

- The US Fire Administration's fireplace and wood stove safety card says: **put the fire out
  before you go to sleep or leave your home** (USFA, Fireplace and Wood Stove Safety). The same
  card says keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet away, have the chimney inspected and
  cleaned each year, and keep a stove's doors closed unless loading or stoking.
- EPA says build hot fires: for most appliances **a smouldering fire is not safe or efficient**,
  and keep the doors closed unless loading, because CO can be released into the home (EPA Burn
  Wise, Best Wood-Burning Practices).

**Why:** a sleeping household cannot notice smoke rolling into the room, a chimney fire
starting, or a log rolling against the door. The agency cannot see your chimney, your wood or
your stove, so it draws the line where nobody is asleep beside a live fire.

**What the stove makers say.** Some manuals treat an overnight burn as normal use. One stove
maker's manual gives steps "to achieve an overnight burn": run the stove hot for about 15
minutes, load as much wood as possible in large pieces, burn on high for 15 minutes more, then
turn the air control to low; in the morning the stove should still be hot with embers (Travis
Industries, Lopi Parlor wood stove owner's manual). Another maker's manual says the stove has a
**manufacturer-set minimum low burn rate that must not be altered**, that the wood must be
flaming brightly before the air is turned down or the fire can smoulder, that smouldering,
smoky fires can quickly build a thick layer of creosote, and that a CO detector and a smoke
detector are required in the room where the stove is installed (Stove Builder International,
Wood Stove Owner's Manual, part 1). The same manual says a smaller fire giving three or four
hours of heat is preferable to fully loading the firebox for a much longer burn.

So the agency and some makers differ. Your stove's own manual is the one that applies to it.

## 3. How it actually hurts people

**Chimney fire.** CSIA names the conditions that build creosote: restricted air, unseasoned
wood, a cool chimney, closing a stove's air inlets "too soon or too much", and "overloading the
firebox with wood in an attempt to get a longer burn time" (CSIA, Preventing Chimney Fires).
That is a description of a careless overnight burn. Night after night the deposit grows; then
one hot fire lights it. CSIA says a chimney fire can crack flue tiles and open a path for
flames to the wooden frame of the house, and that most chimney fires go unnoticed.

**Carbon monoxide.** A stove sends its smoke up the chimney only while the chimney draws. A
blocked flue, a damper closed on live coals, a downdraft, or a door left ajar can send smoke
and CO into the room. CDC says people who are sleeping can die from CO before they have any
symptoms, and infants, older adults and people with heart disease, anaemia or breathing
problems are at higher risk (CDC, Carbon Monoxide Poisoning: Basics).

**An open fireplace** gives little overnight warmth for the risk. With the damper open, which it
must be while coals are live, a traditional fireplace can draw as much as 300 cubic feet a
minute of warmed room air up the chimney (University of Missouri Extension, Wood Fuel for
Heating, G5450).

**The other side is real too.** A house with no heat overnight in deep cold is hard on babies
and frail elders: CDC lists babies sleeping in cold bedrooms and older adults with inadequate
heating among those most often hit by hypothermia (CDC, Preventing Hypothermia). {{#if is.cold_winters}}Your
home's January average low is about {{ region.home.jan_low_f }} °F, so this is a real question for your
household.{{/if}}

## 4. What makes it safer

**Removes the risk**
- **Let the last load burn down before bed**, never choke it, and leave the damper open while
  any coals are live. Take the night on bedding: one warm room, sleeping bags, wool, shared
  warmth, hot-water bottles that come out before a child gets in (→ 08-01, → 08-06, → 11-02).
  Relight in the morning (→ 06-02).

**Reduces it a lot**
- **Only a closed stove or insert whose manual describes an overnight or low burn**, run exactly
  the manual's way. Not an open fireplace.
- **Dry wood only**: under 20% moisture (EPA; → 06-03). Wet wood smoulders.
- **Never below the maker's minimum air setting**, and the load flaming brightly before the air
  is turned down (stove maker's manual).
- **Damper open while any coals are live**; door closed and latched.
- **A tested battery CO alarm and smoke alarm** in the room with the stove, plus CO alarms on
  every level and outside each sleeping area (→ 08-02).
- **The chimney checked this season** and swept when it needs it (USFA; → 06-09). Whether to
  light an uninspected stove at all is another card: {{choice DC-21}} (→ 30-22).
- **One adult checks during the night**: the fire, the alarm, the smell of smoke.
- Nothing that can burn within 3 feet (USFA); the hearth barrier in place for children
  (→ 08-08, → 20-02).

**Reduces it a little**
- A small hot fire for the evening and a modest load at bedtime rather than a crammed firebox.
- A stove thermometer on the pipe or top, so a smouldering (too cool) or over-fired (too hot)
  stove is seen.
- An extinguisher by the hearth, not behind it (→ 29-12).

## 5. The options

**`put-out-before-sleep`: Fire out before anyone sleeps (USFA).**
You give up: overnight heat and a warm start to the morning. On the coldest nights the house
cools and you rely on bedding and bodies. You accept: a cold night and a relight every morning.

**`bank-with-rules`: Banked low, with the maker's method and checks.**
You give up: the agency's rule, and part of one adult's sleep every night it runs. You accept:
that the alarms, the chimney check and the night check are what stand between a slow fire and
a sleeping household, and that a careless "all-nighter" builds the creosote that ends wood heat.
