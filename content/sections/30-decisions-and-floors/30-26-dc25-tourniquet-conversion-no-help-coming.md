# DC-25. Converting a tourniquet to a pressure dressing when no help will ever come

**What this page is for.** The binder's rule is that a tourniquet stays on. That rule is written
for a world where an ambulance or a hospital takes over within hours. This card is only for the
case where **no help will ever come**: no ambulance, no clinic, no trained person you can reach or
who can reach you. It explains what military guidance for long, remote care says, what can go
wrong either way, and what the household decides. It is a reminder for trained hands, not the
training.

> **SAFETY**
> - **Never loosen a tourniquet once placed while help can still come; mark the time. If no help
>   will ever come, the conversion decision is DC-25 (→ 30-26).** Re-bleeding and clot release
>   kill (floor, → 30-01).
> - **"No help will ever come" is a high bar.** If you can drive, carry or radio the person to
>   any care at all, that is the answer, not this card (→ 12-16).
> - **Never convert a tourniquet on an amputation, or on anyone in shock** (TCCC Guidelines,
>   2026). TCCC's signs of shock from blood loss: confusion or drowsiness without a head injury,
  and/or a weak or absent pulse at the wrist. Pale, cold, clammy skin goes with it.

## 1. The thing, in plain words

- A **tourniquet** is a tight strap with a twisting handle (a windlass) that squeezes an arm or
  leg until the bleeding below it stops (→ 12-02).
- **Hemostatic gauze** is gauze treated to help blood clot. **Packing** means pushing gauze firmly
  into a wound until it is full.
- A **pressure bandage** is a thick pad with an elastic wrap that holds hard pressure on the wound.
- **Tourniquet conversion** means replacing the tourniquet's job with packing and a pressure
  bandage, then slowly letting the tourniquet off while watching for bleeding. The tourniquet
  stays on the limb, loose, so it can be tightened again in seconds.

## 2. The rule, and why it was set

- **Stop the Bleed** teaches: place the tourniquet 2 to 3 inches above the wound, between the
  wound and the body; twist until the bleeding stops; add a second one above the first if it has
  not; then "continue to monitor and comfort the injured person until help arrives. Do not loosen
  or remove the tourniquet" (Stop the Bleed just-in-time instructions, Uniformed Services
  University). TCCC adds: mark the tourniquet with the time it went on. Both are built for the
  time before help arrives.
- **Military guidance for long, remote care** says the opposite once hours pass. The Tactical
  Combat Casualty Care (TCCC) Guidelines (Committee on TCCC, 01 May 2026) say limb tourniquets
  "should be converted to hemostatic or pressure dressings as soon as possible if three criteria
  are met: the casualty is not in shock; it is possible to monitor the wound closely for bleeding;
  and the tourniquet is not being used to control bleeding from an amputated extremity." They add:
  "Every effort should be made to convert tourniquets in less than 2 hours if bleeding can be
  controlled with other means. Do not remove a tourniquet that has been in place more than 6 hours
  unless close monitoring and lab capability are available."
- The same guidelines limit who does it: people trained only to the basic first-aid levels
  "should not attempt tourniquet conversion beyond 2 hours post-application" without direction
  from a medic, and otherwise keep the tourniquet in place. The Joint Trauma System's Prolonged
  Casualty Care guideline (2021) places conversion with medics and repeats: do not remove a
  tourniquet that has been on more than 6 hours, and do not remove one to relieve pain unless a
  higher medical authority directs it.
- The wilderness first-aid standard (Minimum Guidelines and Scope of Practice for Wilderness First
  Aid, 2012) lists "releasing tourniquets placed to control life-threatening bleeding" as **outside**
  what a wilderness first-aider does.

**Why:** loosening a tourniquet on someone who has already lost blood can start a second bleed
they cannot survive. The time limits exist because a limb without blood is being damaged every
hour, and because after about 6 hours letting blood back into it needs hospital monitoring and
laboratory tests, which a household does not have.

## 3. How it actually hurts people

**Converting:** the bandage looks good, the tourniquet is eased off, and a few minutes later the
dressing turns red and drips. If the tourniquet is not tightened again at once, the person can
bleed to death in minutes. Converting someone already in shock, or an amputation, is the version
that kills most surely, which is why both are excluded. Converting late, after 6 hours, adds the
harm the guideline warns about and that only a hospital can manage.

**Not converting:** the tourniquet holds, the person lives, and the arm or leg below it has no
blood. Tissue survives only a limited time without blood (MedlinePlus, Broken Bone). With no
surgeon, a dead limb cannot be removed safely and becomes a source of infection over the
following days (→ 12-04). The household is choosing between a bleeding risk now and a limb (and
later an infection) risk.

## 4. What makes it safer

**Removes the question**
- **Reaching care.** If the person can be moved to any surgeon, clinic or medic within a few
  hours, the tourniquet stays on and goes with them (→ 12-16).

**Reduces it a lot**
- **Hands-on training that teaches conversion by name.** Ask for "tourniquet conversion" in a
  wilderness first responder or tactical medicine course now, while courses exist (→ 24-01).
- **The kit to do it:** hemostatic gauze, plain gauze for packing, two pressure bandages, a second
  tourniquet, gloves, a marker (→ 12-01, → 22-06).
- **Meeting every criterion, not most of them.** Not in shock, wound in plain view, not an
  amputation, and early: TCCC aims for under 2 hours for everyone, and tells people below medic
  level not to convert after 2 hours without a medic's direction. **On this card the 2-hour
  limit applies to everyone, including a household member with medic training:** the longer
  medic window in the guidelines assumes medic direction and, after 6 hours, laboratory
  monitoring, which a household does not have. After 2 hours, the tourniquet stays on.

**Reduces it a little**
- Time written on the strap and the skin, so everyone knows the clock.
- Good light, a warm patient (→ 08-05), and a second person whose only job is to watch the
  dressing.

### The published steps, as printed for medics

From the TCCC skill instructions for combat medics (Tourniquet Conversion, 2021), in order:
expose the wound; **pack it tightly** with hemostatic gauze (clean, dry cloth if none) while
keeping firm pressure; keep **firm hand pressure for at least 3 minutes** after packing; put the
pressure bandage pad over the wound and wrap it tightly; then **"slowly release the tourniquet
(over one minute); observe the bandage for bleeding."** **"If bleeding reoccurs, retighten the
original tourniquet, ensuring bleeding is controlled and the distal pulse is absent."** Write every
time down: when it went on, when it was converted, when it was retightened.

This card does not describe a second attempt. If the bleeding comes back, the tourniquet goes
back on and the household is in the `never-loosen` situation.

## 5. The options

**`never-loosen`: Never loosen: keep it on and accept the limb may be lost.**
You give up: the chance of saving the limb. You accept: that the arm or leg below the tourniquet
may die, and the infection risk that follows, in return for never risking the bleeding again.

**`convert-by-criteria`: Convert by the published prolonged-care criteria.**
You give up: the certainty that the bleeding is stopped. You accept: that the bleeding may
return and must be stopped again at once, that the person doing it should be trained, and that
it is done only when every criterion above is met, no help will ever come, and less than 2
hours have passed since the tourniquet went on. The 2-hour limit is the same for every
household member, medic training or not; after 2 hours this option becomes `never-loosen`.
