# DC-15. Giving someone else's prescription medication

**What this page is for.** When a person runs out of a medicine and nobody can write a new
prescription, the bottle on the next shelf is tempting. This card explains why prescriptions
are personal, what goes wrong when they are shared, and what the household decides. It never
tells you which drug or dose to give.

> **SAFETY.** Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products
> (floor, → 30-01). If someone has taken a medicine not meant for them and becomes very sleepy,
> confused or is breathing slowly, treat it as a poisoning: → 12-17.

## 1. The thing, in plain words

A **prescription medicine** is one a clinician chose for a particular person, at a particular
strength (the milligrams or units on the label), on a particular schedule. **Sharing** means
anyone else taking it. The label on the bottle (→ 29-14) gives the name, strength, the person
it was for and the directions.

## 2. The rule, and why it was set

- Pharmacists and clinicians say take prescription medicine only as prescribed for you, and never
  share it. CDC says it in plain words for antibiotics: "Do not share your antibiotics with
  others" and "Do not take antibiotics prescribed for someone else" (CDC, Healthy Habits:
  Antibiotic Do's and Don'ts).
- For insulin in disasters, FDA says switching insulin "should always be done in consultation
  with a physician and requires close medical supervision, and if possible, close monitoring of
  blood glucose" (FDA, Insulin Storage and Switching Between Products in an Emergency; ADA,
  Switching Between Insulin Products in Disaster Response Situations).

**Why:** the right drug and dose depend on what is actually wrong, the person's allergies,
everything else they take, their kidney and liver function, their weight and age. A medicine
that is safe for one person can be dangerous for another.

**The strongest case for sharing** is an emergency drug given once: for example, epinephrine
from another person's auto-injector in anaphylaxis, which allergy plans treat as life-saving
(AAAAI, Anaphylaxis Emergency Action Plan; → 02-03). This card is about regular prescriptions.

## 3. How it actually hurts people

- **Allergy**: a person allergic to a drug family (penicillin is the common one) takes a shared
  pill and has a severe reaction.
- **Interaction**: a shared painkiller or antibiotic interacts with a blood thinner, a heart
  medicine or a seizure medicine the person already takes.
- **Too strong**: a strong painkiller or a sleeping or anxiety medicine that one person
  tolerates can stop the breathing of someone who has never taken it, especially a child, an
  elder, or anyone who has also had alcohol. Pain medicines and prescription medicines are among
  the common causes of poisoning (MedlinePlus, Poisoning first aid).
- **Wrong insulin**: a fast-acting insulin taken as if it were a long-acting one, or a different
  strength, causes severe low blood sugar: shaking, confusion, seizures, unconsciousness.
- **Wrong diagnosis**: treating a problem with the wrong medicine hides it while it gets worse.

## 4. What makes it safer

**Removes most of the risk**
- **Each person's own supply, and a written plan**: ask every clinician now for extra supply
  where allowed and for written instructions on what to do if a medicine runs out (→ 12-07).
- **A written list** of every household member's medicines, strengths and allergies
  (→ 25-09).

**Reduces it a lot**
- **Same drug, same strength, already prescribed to that person**: they take it as their own
  prescription said, nothing else changes.
- **Check allergies and every other medicine** they take before the first dose.
- **Never** give a child a medicine from an adult's supply, and never give a new drug for a new
  problem.

**Reduces it a little**
- Watch the person closely for the first hours after the first dose.
- Write down who took what and when.

## 5. The options

**`never`: Never.**
You give up: the chance to bridge a gap with medicine that is in the house. You accept: that
someone may go without their medicine until a source is found.

**`same-drug-same-strength-only`: Same drug and strength, already prescribed to them.**
You give up: the pharmacist's check. You accept: the risk of an unchecked allergy or
interaction and of a label misread, controlled by matching drug and strength exactly and
checking the person's allergy and medicine list.
