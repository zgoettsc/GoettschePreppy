# The floors: nine rules that are not choices

**What this page is for.** Most hard calls in this binder are trade-offs, and the household
makes them on the decision cards (→ 30-02 to → 30-30). The nine rules on this page are
different. Each one guards against something that kills or maims **without warning and with no
fix afterwards**. No mitigation makes the risky version safe every time, so there is no card,
no options box and no "our choice" line. Other pages copy the rule sentences below word for
word.

> **SAFETY.** If you are reading this in an emergency, the nine bold sentences are the whole
> page. Everything else explains why.

## 1. Engines and charcoal stay outside

**Never run a generator, charcoal, or any engine indoors, in a garage, or near an open window.**

**Why there is no choice.** Burning fuel makes carbon monoxide (CO), a gas you cannot see,
smell or taste (→ 29-03). It takes the place of oxygen in your blood. The first signs feel
like flu: headache, dizziness, sick stomach. People lie down and do not get up. CDC says
people who are asleep or drunk can die before they ever feel ill, and more than 400
Americans die from it each year (CDC, Carbon Monoxide Poisoning: Basics). A generator is the
worst source: CPSC says one portable generator makes as much CO as hundreds of cars (CPSC,
Carbon Monoxide Information Center), and that using one indoors can kill in minutes, even with
doors and windows open (CPSC, What to Know About Generators and CO). CO alarms help, but
batteries die and alarms get placed wrong, and an alarm built to the home standard stays silent
at low levels and sounds only after CO has built up for a while (UL 2034 alarm points, as
printed in a First Alert CO alarm manual; → 29-03). Nothing you can add makes an engine indoors
safe every time. The same applies to charcoal and to camp stoves: CDC says never burn charcoal
indoors and never use a portable gas camp stove indoors (CDC, Carbon Monoxide Poisoning: Basics).

**Do this instead.** Run the generator outdoors, at least 20 feet from the house, with the
exhaust pointed away from every house (CPSC); CDC says more than 20 feet from any window, door
or vent. Cook on charcoal and camp stoves outdoors. Keep battery CO alarms on every level and
where people sleep (CPSC; CDC). → 08-02, → 09-04.

## 2. A generator never feeds the house wiring by itself

**Never connect a generator to house wiring except through a transfer switch, or an interlock kit with the main breaker off. Never backfeed through an outlet with a double-male ("suicide") cord.**

A cord-and-plug conversion is not a connection to house wiring: the appliance is unplugged from
the house outlet and plugged into the generator cord, so nothing reaches the panel (whether to do
that conversion yourself is DC-07, → 30-08).

**Why there is no choice.** "Backfeeding" means pushing generator power into the house
wiring, usually with a cord that has a plug on both ends (→ 29-04). That power does not stay
in your house. It flows out through the meter to the transformer on the pole, which raises it
to the voltage of the power line. A line worker who believes the line is dead, or a
neighbour served by the same transformer, is electrocuted. CPSC calls plugging a generator into
a wall outlet "extremely dangerous": it is an electrocution risk to utility workers and
neighbours served by the same utility transformer, and it bypasses some of the house's built-in
circuit protection (CPSC, Safety Alert: Portable Generator Hazards). OSHA's rule is never to
attach a generator to a building's wiring without a properly installed transfer switch, because
of the electrocution risk to utility workers (OSHA, Portable Generator Safety QuickCard). A
double-plug cord also has bare live pins in someone's hand. There is no way to backfeed
"carefully".

**Do this instead.** Run extension cords from the generator to single appliances. If you need
a furnace or well pump on generator power, either have a transfer switch or interlock kit
installed for your panel (with an interlock, the main breaker is off whenever the generator
feeds the panel), or give the appliance its own plug by a cord-and-plug conversion so it can be
unplugged from the wall and plugged into the generator cord (→ 09-04). Who does the conversion
work is a decision card (→ 30-08). Backfeeding is never on that card.

## 3. A tourniquet stays on

**Never loosen a tourniquet once placed while help can still come; mark the time. If no help will ever come, the conversion decision is DC-25 (→ 30-26).**

**Why there is no choice.** A tourniquet is a tight band that stops blood flow to a bleeding
arm or leg. Loosening it "to check" or "to give the limb some blood" lets the bleeding start
again. The person has already lost blood, so the second bleed can be the one that kills, and
the clots that had begun to form are washed away. Stop the Bleed's instructions end: "Do not
loosen or remove the tourniquet" (Stop the Bleed just-in-time instructions, Uniformed Services
University). Writing the time lets anyone who takes over know how long it has been on; military
guidance records the time of application and marks it on the tourniquet (TCCC Guidelines, 2026).

The rule assumes someone will take over: an ambulance, a clinic, a trained medic. When no help
will ever come, military guidance for long, remote care (TCCC Guidelines, 2026) describes
converting a tourniquet to wound packing and a pressure bandage under strict conditions, and
warns against removing one that has been on more than 6 hours without close monitoring and
laboratory support. That is a trade-off, not a floor, so it is a decision card (→ 30-26). While
any help can still come, the tourniquet stays on.

**Do this instead.** Place it 2 to 3 inches above the wound, between the wound and the body,
not over a joint, and tighten until the bleeding stops. If it still bleeds, add a second one
above the first, closer to the body (Stop the Bleed, Uniformed Services University). Write the
time on the strap or the skin. Take a Stop the Bleed course while courses exist. → 12-02,
→ 30-26.

## 4. Low-acid food is pressure-canned or not canned

**Low-acid foods are pressure-canned to the published time and pressure, or not canned.**

**Why there is no choice.** Low-acid foods are red meats, seafood, poultry, milk and all fresh
vegetables except most tomatoes (→ 29-08). In a sealed jar with no air, botulism spores grow
and make a toxin that paralyses the muscles, including the ones you breathe with. USDA says the
spores are very hard to destroy at boiling-water temperatures, so all low-acid foods are
processed at 240 to 250 °F, which only a pressure canner reaches (USDA, Complete Guide to Home
Canning, 2015). The times and pressures in the USDA tables are research-based; guessing them is
not the same thing. You cannot see, smell or taste the toxin, so you cannot catch the bad jar at
the table (CDC, Botulism Prevention). Treatment needs a hospital: antitoxin, sometimes a
breathing machine, and for some people weeks or months in hospital (CDC, Treatment of
Botulism). With no hospital, there is no treatment.

**Do this instead.** Use a real pressure canner (not a pressure cooker) and the USDA time and
pressure for that food, jar size and your altitude. If you have no canner, dry, salt, ferment
by a tested recipe, or eat the food fresh. Throw out any can or jar that is bulging, leaking,
spurting or smells wrong when opened; USDA warns that spoiled low-acid food may show very little
evidence at all (USDA, Complete Guide). → 04-05, → 04-04.

## 5. Wild plants and mushrooms: identification or nothing

**No wild plant or mushroom is eaten on a "test"; identification or nothing.**

**Why there is no choice.** Some survival books, including the military handbook reprinted in
Section 31, describe an "edibility test": touch it, taste a little, wait. That test does not
protect you from the plants that kill. Only a small amount of water hemlock is needed to poison
a person (USDA Agricultural Research Service, Poisonous Plant Research Laboratory); a 2 to 3 cm
(about 1 inch) piece of root can kill an adult, symptoms start within 15 to 90 minutes, and in
fatal cases severe seizures follow (CDC MMWR, Water Hemlock Poisoning, Maine, 1992). The
deadliest mushrooms, death caps, cause no symptoms for 6 to 24 hours, then seem to get better,
and the liver fails 3 to 5 days after the meal (CDC MMWR, Amanita phalloides Mushroom
Poisonings, 2017). Taste tells you nothing about either. Where Section 31 describes an
edibility test, this floor governs.

**Do this instead.** Eat only what you can name with certainty: several matching features,
the right habitat and season, checked against two sources. Leave all mushrooms alone. Use the
plant plates for your region. → 05-06.

## 6. Fuel and flame never meet

**Fuel is never stored or transferred near an open flame or a running appliance.**

**Why there is no choice.** Gasoline gives off vapour that can burn even in deep cold: its
flash point is −45 °F (NIOSH Pocket Guide, Gasoline; → 29-01). The vapour is heavier than air
and can travel along the floor to an ignition source (Maryland Department of the Environment,
Gasoline Safety): a pilot flame, a furnace igniter, a spark or a hot engine across the room.
CPSC warns that vapour escaping a fuel can that meets a spark or flame can ignite and flash back
into the container, causing an explosion that can severely burn those nearby (CPSC, Fuel
Container Safety, 2023). Propane does the same thing: it is heavier than air and settles low
(Flame King, Training Program 1A).

**Do this instead.** Pour and refill outdoors, away from any flame, with engines and heaters
turned off and cool. Store fuel away from water heaters, furnaces, space heaters and anything
that sparks (Maryland Department of the Environment). Where your household stores it is a
decision card (→ 30-06). → 06-06, → 06-07.

## 7. Frozen insulin is thrown away

**A frozen-then-thawed insulin vial or pen is discarded.**

**Why there is no choice.** Insulin is a protein, and freezing can damage it for good
(→ 29-07). It can look completely normal afterwards, so you cannot tell how much strength is
left. A dose that does too little, given to someone with type 1 diabetes, can lead to a
dangerous buildup of acids in the blood (diabetic ketoacidosis, which CDC calls potentially
life-threatening) without anyone noticing in time (CDC, Diabetic Ketoacidosis). FDA and the
American Diabetes Association both say do not use insulin that has been frozen (FDA, Insulin
Storage and Switching Between Products in an Emergency; ADA, Switching Between Insulin Products
in Disaster Response Situations), and ADA says never store insulin in the freezer (ADA, Insulin
Storage and Syringe Safety). Heat is different: heat-exposed insulin is a decision card
(→ 30-09).

**Do this instead.** Keep insulin from freezing: a barrier between insulin and any ice, a
thermometer in the box, the box in the warm room in winter. → 02-01, → 29-07.

## 8. Bleach and pool shock are never mixed

**Bleach or pool shock is never mixed with ammonia, acids or other cleaners.**

**Why there is no choice.** Bleach and pool shock (calcium hypochlorite) are chlorine
products (→ 29-05). Mixed with an acid (some toilet and rust cleaners, vinegar), they release
chlorine gas. Mixed with ammonia (glass cleaners, urine), they release chloramine gas
(CDC/ATSDR, Medical Management Guidelines for Calcium Hypochlorite and Sodium Hypochlorite).
CDC says never mix bleach with any other cleaner or disinfectant, because the vapours can be
very dangerous to breathe (CDC, Cleaning and Disinfecting with Bleach). Chlorine gas burns the
eyes, throat and lungs (CDC/ATSDR, Medical Management Guidelines for Chlorine). Pool shock is
also an oxidizer: with combustible material it can start a fire, it can react explosively with
ammonia, and it gives off chlorine when it gets wet (CDC/ATSDR, Calcium Hypochlorite).

**Do this instead.** Use one cleaning product at a time, with water only. Store pool shock
dry, sealed, off the floor, below 120 °F and away from fuel, acids and ammonia (CDC/ATSDR).
→ 03-04, → 29-05.

## 9. Children cannot get to the dangerous things

**Children do not have access to firearms, fuel, medications or chlorine products.**

**Why there is no choice.** Every source agrees on this one. Young children explore by
putting things in their mouths. Young children are most often poisoned by medicines and
household products, and fuels such as gasoline, kerosene and lamp oil are among the things that
cause the most serious harm (AAP, Poison Prevention). Lamp oil swallowed by a child can be
breathed into the lungs and cause a chemical pneumonia, the main cause of death and injury from
it (CDC MMWR, Near Fatal Ingestion of Household Lamp Oil, 1997). Even toddlers can find an
unlocked gun and are strong enough to pull the trigger, and the AAP says teaching children not
to touch a gun is not enough (AAP, Guns in the Home; NSSF, Project ChildSafe). In a crisis the adults are busier and the supervision is thinner,
which is exactly when storage has to do the work.

**Do this instead.** Lock firearms unloaded, with ammunition locked separately, and keep the
keys and combinations from children (AAP). Store fuel, medicine and chlorine locked, out of
sight and reach, in their original labelled containers, never in drink bottles (AAP; CDC MMWR).
Teach the rule as an absolute, but let storage do the work. Which jobs children may help with under supervision is a worksheet
(→ 30-25). → 20-02, → 14-14.

## Lines inside the cards that are also fixed

A few cards contain one fixed line that is a floor, not an option:

- **Backfeeding** is never an option on the generator card (→ 30-08); see floor 2.
- **Frozen insulin** is never an option on the heat-exposed medication card (→ 30-09); see
  floor 7.
- **Bulging, leaking or spurting cans and jars** are thrown out; the questionable food card
  (→ 30-15) covers everything else.
