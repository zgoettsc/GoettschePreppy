# Solar cookers

**What this page is for.** Cooking with sunlight: no fuel, no smoke, no carbon monoxide. What a
solar cooker does well, what it cannot do, and how to build one from boxes and foil.

> **CAUTION.** Solar cooking is **free but slow and depends on the weather**, and it does not
> excuse you from food safety: meat must still reach safe internal temperatures, checked with a
> thermometer (165 °F poultry, 160 °F ground meat, venison and rabbit, 145 °F whole cuts of beef,
> pork, lamb and veal with a 3-minute rest; FoodSafety.gov). Do not make a solar cooker your only plan for anything that must be cooked
> through.

## Do this now

1. **Use it all summer** for anything slow (beans, grains, stews, bread, rehydrating dried food)
   and save every stick of wood for winter (→ 06-04).
2. **Start early.** Solar cooking needs the middle of the day; food goes in by mid-morning.
3. **Use dark, thin-walled pots with lids**, under a clear cover.
4. **Turn it to face the sun every hour or two**, unless the design is very wide-angle.
5. **Check meat with a thermometer**, always.

## Why / how it works

Sunlight passes through a clear cover, is soaked up by dark surfaces, and the heat is trapped:
a greenhouse in a box. **Reflectors** bounce extra light in; **insulation** keeps the heat from
escaping. A decent box cooker reaches **slow-oven temperatures** on a clear day (check it
with an oven thermometer). Plenty for stews, beans, rice, bread and casseroles; not searing heat.

### What it is good for

| Great | Poor |
|---|---|
| Beans, lentils, grains, rice | Searing or frying |
| Stews, soups, casseroles | Fast cooking on demand |
| Bread and baked goods | Cloudy days, winter, evenings |
| Rehydrating dried food (→ 07-07) | Large amounts quickly |
| **Heating water** (below) | Anything you need in 20 minutes |
| Keeping food hot | |

### Your sun

- **Summer** in most of the US has long days and strong sun: from late spring to early fall a
  solar cooker can carry a real share of the cooking. Your home gets about
  **{{ region.home.sun_hours_jun }} peak sun hours a day in June** and
  **{{ region.home.sun_hours_dec }} in December** ("peak sun hours" are explained on → 09-02).
- **Winter** is weak almost everywhere north of the southern tier: short days, low sun, cold air
  fighting you. Treat solar as a **seasonal fuel-saver**, not a year-round method. The further
  south you are, the longer its season.
- **The point is shifting fuel across the year:** every meal cooked by sun in summer is wood or
  propane you still have in winter.

### Water: an important second use

Heat makes water safe, and a solar cooker can heat water without fuel. Keep the methods apart
(→ 29-06 explains the water words):
- **CDC's solar disinfection method (SODIS)** uses **clear plastic bottles laid on their side in
  full sun for 6 hours (sunny) or 2 days (cloudy)**, and works on **clear water only** (CDC, How to
  Make Water Safe in an Emergency; → 03-04).
- **A solar cooker** heats water faster and hotter. If you rely on heat rather than a rolling boil,
  use a **water pasteurisation indicator (WAPI)**: a small tube of wax that melts when the water
  has been hot enough. Do not guess.
- **When in doubt, boil** (→ 03-04). The fuel saving is not worth a sick child.

### Types

- **Box cooker:** an insulated box with a clear lid and reflectors. The best all-rounder; holds
  heat.
- **Panel cooker:** reflective panels aimed at a dark pot inside a clear bag. Cheap, folds flat.
- **Parabolic cooker:** a dish focusing sunlight to a point. Hottest and fastest, but needs
  constant aiming and is **an eye and burn hazard**. Not for use around small children.

### Build one

A working box cooker: **a cardboard box inside a larger box**, the gap packed with crumpled
newspaper or wool; the inside lined with **aluminium foil**; a **dark pot** inside; and a **clear
plastic or glass lid**. Add foil-covered flaps as reflectors. It costs almost nothing and works,
and it is a good project to build with children (→ 20-03).

**Practical notes:** black pots with lids; do not open it to look (each peek loses heat); put an
oven thermometer inside so you know what you are getting; expect two to four times the usual
cooking time.

**Have it now (buy toward this):**

| Foil, cardboard, clear plastic sheet | Build and repair reflectors and covers | |
| Oven bags or a clear dome | Traps heat around the pot | Big gain |

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- **Two cardboard boxes, newspaper, foil and a clear lid** make a working oven.
- A **car windshield sunshade** becomes a panel cooker with a little shaping.
- Any dark pot; a glass bowl or clear plastic bag as the cover.
- Reflective emergency blankets make good reflectors.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** nothing runs out but the sun and the season. Foil and plastic covers wear out in
  sunlight; keep spares.
- **Ration:** nothing to ration. Use it whenever the sun is out, so you can save wood and propane
  for when it is not.
- **Resupply:** the rocket stove (→ 07-02) and the open fire (→ 07-01) on cloudy days and in
  winter.
- **The yearly rotation:** solar in summer, wood in winter, propane held in reserve. That is how
  a limited fuel supply lasts a year rather than a season.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** a yard or balcony with sun is enough, and it makes **no smoke and no smell**,
  which draws less attention than a fire (→ 14-03).
- **B (moving):** a folding panel cooker weighs almost nothing, but you rarely have the hours
  while moving.
- **C (establishing):** excellent: space, sun, and a strong reason to save wood.
