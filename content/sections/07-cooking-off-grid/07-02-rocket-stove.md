# Rocket stoves, hayboxes and fuel-efficient cooking

**What this page is for.** How to cook more food on less wood: the rocket stove, the "haybox"
that finishes cooking with no fuel at all, the vacuum flask, and the small habits that add up.

> **CAUTION.** A rocket stove is a **fuel-burning appliance**: it makes carbon monoxide (CO), an
> invisible gas that kills (→ 29-03). It stays **outdoors**, never in a closed room, tent or
> garage, unless it is a stove designed, installed and vented for indoor use (CDC, Carbon Monoxide
> Poisoning; → 08-02). It gets extremely hot and tips easily: with children around, where you put
> it matters as much as how well it burns. And **dry kidney-type beans must be boiled hard before
> any slow or haybox cooking** (below).

## Do this now

1. **Get or build a rocket stove.** For everyday cooking it uses **roughly half the wood** an
   open fire does: the biggest fuel saving you can make (→ 06-04).
2. **Feed it small:** twigs, sticks and split scraps, fuel you would not bother putting on a
   fireplace.
3. **Always put a lid on the pot.** It is the biggest free saving there is.
4. **Set it on a level, non-combustible surface**, out of the wind, away from where children run.
5. **Make a haybox** (an insulated box for a hot pot) this week. It costs nothing.

## Why / how it works

### Why a rocket stove is so much better

A rocket stove is an **insulated L- or J-shaped burn chamber**. Three things happen:
1. **The fire is contained**, so heat goes into the pot instead of the sky.
2. **The insulated chamber runs very hot**, so it burns the smoky gases from the wood too: more
   complete burning, less smoke, more heat from the same wood (the principle EPA describes for
   efficient wood burning; EPA, Burn Wise).
3. **The short vertical chimney makes a strong draft**, pulling air through the fuel; that is the
   "rocket" roar.

A handful of sticks boils a pot of water. An open fire loses most of its heat to the air. Expect
**roughly half the wood** of an open fire in practice: a large saving, but not the "ten times"
that gets repeated. Plan your woodpile on that basis (→ 06-04). With a lid, a windscreen and a
haybox on top, the saving grows further.

### Where the fuel goes: the efficiency stack

Every one of these is free and they add up:

| Habit | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Lid on the pot** | Large: the biggest single free saving |
| **Rocket stove instead of open fire** | Very large |
| **Windbreak** | Large, especially in winter |
| **Dry, seasoned, split-small fuel** | Large (wet wood wastes its heat boiling off water; University of Missouri Extension, Wood Fuel for Heating; → 06-03) |
| **Pot sized to the stove** | Moderate |
| **One fire, many jobs** | Large |
| **Soak beans and grains first** | Large: cuts boiling time a lot |
| **Haybox finishing** (below) | Very large for slow-cooked food |
| **Turn it off early**: let held heat finish the job | Moderate |

### The haybox: the cheapest trick there is

Bring food to a **full rolling boil and hold it there for at least 10 minutes**, then move the
covered pot into a heavily insulated box: blankets, straw, crumpled paper, or a cooler stuffed
with towels. It keeps cooking for hours **with no fuel**. Stews, grains, soups and soaked beans
finish well this way. **Boil hard, then insulate.** It also keeps food hot for people eating at
different times.

{{figure fig-haybox}}

**Two rules the box does not forgive:**
- **Dry kidney beans, and any red, white, pinto or black bean: soak at least 5 hours, THROW AWAY
  the soak water, then boil hard for at least 10 minutes (FDA advises 30) BEFORE the box. Never
  cook dry beans from raw in a haybox or any slow, low-heat method.** Raw or undercooked kidney
  beans contain a natural toxin (phytohaemagglutinin): **4 or 5 raw beans can cause hours of
  violent vomiting and diarrhoea**, and slow, low-temperature cooking, which is what a haybox is
  after the first minutes, can make it **worse** (FDA, Bad Bug Book). Canned beans are already
  safe; dry beans from storage (→ 04-03) are not until boiled.
- **The box must keep food above 140 °F.** Food that has sat in the box **more than about
  4 hours**, or is no longer too hot to touch, is **boiled again before serving**. An overnight
  haybox has spent hours in the 40 to 140 °F range where bacteria grow (FoodSafety.gov). It is
  not breakfast.

### The vacuum flask: the haybox's twin

A good stainless vacuum flask, preheated with hot water and filled full, keeps water **hot** for
many hours, less if it is part-empty or was not preheated. The label states how hot after how
long. Boil once in the morning, fill the flasks, and pour from them all day:
- Freeze-dried pouches with a longer rest (→ 07-07), hot drinks, instant foods, hot-water bottles.
- **Flask water is hot, not boiling.** Not for directions that require boiling water, and not
  for sanitising.

One boil instead of three is a large saving with any heat source: kettle (→ 07-06), rocket stove,
propane or fire.

### Buying or building

- **Buying:** a manufactured rocket stove is inexpensive and sturdy.
- **Building:** the classic homemade version is a large can with a smaller can or pipe as the burn
  chamber and wood ash or vermiculite packed between them as insulation; masonry versions use
  brick or block. What matters: **insulation around the burn chamber**, a **short chimney**, and a
  **shelf** so fuel feeds in sideways while air passes underneath.
- **Have both** if you can: one bought, one improvised, and the knowledge to build another.

### Safety with children

- **Stability first.** A rocket stove with a full pot is top-heavy and very hot. Put it on level,
  non-combustible ground where nobody runs past.
- **The chimney is very hot and does not look it.** Teach the boundary (→ 20-02).
- **Never indoors, never in a tent, never in a garage** (→ 08-02).

## Gear / items

**Have it now (buy toward this):**

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Manufactured rocket stove | The biggest fuel saving you can buy | Steel, with a pot support |
| Pots with **tight lids** | Free efficiency | Sized to the stove |
| Windscreen | Big saving outdoors | Sheet metal or foil-covered board |
| Haybox: a cooler or box and blankets | Fuel-free finishing | Any cooler works |
| Stainless vacuum flasks | One boil covers the day | Preheat before filling |
| Hatchet or small saw for kindling | Rocket stoves eat small fuel | → 16-01 |
| Heat-proof gloves and a stable base | Burns and tipping | Non-combustible surface |
| Materials for a second stove (cans, insulation) | Spare and skill | Practise building one now |

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- **Cans, buckets and ash or vermiculite** build a working rocket stove for almost nothing.
- **Bricks or concrete blocks** stack into a rocket-style stove in minutes with no tools.
- A **hobo stove** (a large can with air holes) is far better than an open fire, if cruder.
- Any cooler, box or pile of blankets becomes a haybox; any insulated jug or wrapped pot stands in
  for a flask, for shorter times.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** the stove does not run out; steel slowly burns through with heavy use, which is why
  a spare or the skill to build one matters.
- **Ration:** this page **is** the rationing plan for wood (→ 06-04). Stove, lid, windbreak and
  haybox together make everyday cooking cheap in fuel.
- **Resupply:** solar cooking (→ 07-03) in summer costs nothing; the open fire (→ 07-01) when you
  want the heat as well.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** everyday outdoor cooking; the open fire for batch cooking and boiling volume. A
  rocket stove also makes far less smoke, so it draws less attention (→ 14-03).
- **B (moving):** a small rocket or twig stove is ideal: it burns sticks you gather, so you carry
  no fuel.
- **C (establishing):** build a masonry version and a haybox. Fuel efficiency matters more when you
  cut all your own wood.
