# Dutch-oven cooking and baking without an oven

**What this page is for.** The cast-iron Dutch oven: how to control its heat with coals, how to
bake bread with no kitchen oven and no yeast, and how to keep cast iron for a lifetime.

> **CAUTION.** **Never run a generator, charcoal, or any engine indoors, in a garage, or near an
> open window** (→ 30-01). Charcoal for Dutch-oven baking is **outdoor only**: it gives off carbon
> monoxide, an invisible gas that kills (CDC, Carbon Monoxide Poisoning; → 29-03). Cast iron full of
> hot food is **heavy and stays hot a long time**: a serious burn and crush risk around children.
> And baking does not exempt meat from safe temperatures (→ 07-01).

## Do this now

1. **Own at least one cast-iron Dutch oven**, the "camp" style with three legs and a flat, rimmed
   lid: the most versatile single cooking pot for off-grid life.
2. **Learn one bread you can make from stored staples** and practise it now, while a failure is
   only a bad loaf.
3. **Coals on the lid, not just underneath.** That is what turns a pot into an oven.
4. **Dry and oil your cast iron** after every use; rust is the only thing that kills it.
5. **If anyone has a food allergy**, bake their food first, in their own marked pan (→ 07-01,
   → 02-04).

## Why / how it works

### Why cast iron

It works on coals, in a fireplace, on a rocket stove, on a grate or on a stove; it holds and
evens out heat; it lasts generations; and it needs no electricity, no non-stick coating and no
spare parts. **Cast iron is the cookware that outlives the crisis.**

A **camp Dutch oven** (three legs, flat lid with a raised rim) is the key piece: the legs hold it
above coals, and the rimmed lid holds coals **on top**, so heat comes from both directions, like a
real oven.

### Controlling the temperature with coals

The traditional **"rule of 3"** (a camp-cooking rule of thumb, not an agency figure; check with a
thermometer):
- **Total charcoal briquettes = 2 × the oven's diameter in inches ≈ 350 °F.** A 12-inch oven →
  **24 briquettes**; a 10-inch → 20.
- **For baking, put the diameter + 3 on the lid and the diameter − 3 underneath.** 12-inch oven →
  **15 on the lid, 9 underneath.** The other way round burns the bottom before the top browns.
- **Each briquette added or removed ≈ 10 to 15 °F.** For 400 °F in a 12-inch oven, about 28.
- **In deep cold or wind, add about a quarter more.**

| Job | Heat pattern |
|---|---|
| **Baking** (bread, biscuits, cake) | Most coals **on the lid**, few underneath |
| **Roasting** | Even, top and bottom |
| **Simmering, stewing** | Mostly underneath, few or none on top |
| **Frying** | All underneath |

**Turn it to avoid hot spots:** every 10 to 15 minutes, turn the oven a third of a turn one way
and the lid a third of a turn the other. That habit is the difference between burnt and even.

**Keep coals coming:** briquettes give about 45 to 60 minutes of useful heat; wood coals less.
For long bakes, keep a second fire making fresh coals (→ 06-02).

**Use a thermometer** rather than trusting coal counts: an oven thermometer inside the pot, an
instant-read for meat.

### Bread without an oven, or without yeast

Bread matters more than people expect: calories, morale, and a use for the stored grain and flour
(→ 04-03).
- **Yeast breads** need yeast: stock it, or keep a **sourdough starter** alive (a living mix of
  flour and water that leavens bread indefinitely and can be shared with neighbours).
- **Soda breads and quick breads** need only baking soda or baking powder: cheap and long-lasting.
- **Flatbreads:** flour, water, salt, a hot skillet or flat stone. No leavening, fast, reliable.
  **This is the bread to know.**
- **Bannock and ash cakes:** dough cooked on a stick, in a skillet, or straight in clean embers.

### Other ways to bake

- **A skillet and lid** with coals on top stands in for a Dutch oven.
- **Two skillets** clamped rim to rim make an improvised oven.
- **A metal box or tin in the coals** bakes well.
- **The haybox** (→ 07-02) will not bake, but it finishes anything simmered.

### Care

- **Season:** a thin coat of oil, heated until it bonds to the iron; repeat. Well-seasoned iron is
  nearly non-stick.
- **Clean** with hot water and a brush or chain-mail scrubber rather than heavy soap scrubbing;
  dry at once over heat, then wipe with oil.
- **Never store it damp.**
- **Wood ash** is a good scouring powder for greasy iron (→ 06-02).

## Gear / items

**Have it now (buy toward this):**

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Camp Dutch oven (legs, rimmed lid), 10 to 12 inch | The core off-grid oven | Bigger for a larger household |
| Cast-iron skillet(s) with a lid | Everyday cooking, flatbread | 10 to 12 inch |
| Lid lifter and heavy gloves | Lifting a lid covered in coals | A hook is safer than pliers |
| Long tongs or a small shovel for coals | Placing coals | |
| Oven thermometer and instant-read thermometer | Real temperatures | Cheap |
| Charcoal briquettes, stored dry | Predictable, countable heat | **Outdoor only** |
| Lid stand or trivet | A place to set a hot lid | Keeps ash out of food |
| Flour, salt, baking soda and powder, yeast | Bread from stored staples | Rotate (→ 04-03) |
| Sourdough starter | Renewable leavening | |
| Marked cookware, if anyone has a food allergy | Cross-contact control | → 02-04 |

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- Any **heavy pot with a tight lid** works as a Dutch oven: bury it in coals with coals on the lid.
- **Flat, dry stones** as griddles for flatbread.
- A **metal biscuit tin or ammunition can** as a small oven in coals.
- Green sticks for bannock; children can cook their own with an adult at the fire (→ 20-02).

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** cast iron does not run out. The consumables are **fuel** (→ 06-04), **charcoal** and
  **leavening**.
- **Ration:** bake in batches while the coals are hot: bread, then a stew, then wash water, all
  from one fire.
- **Resupply:** a sourdough starter replaces bought yeast for good; flatbread needs no leavening
  at all.
- **Morale:** fresh bread in a hard winter is worth more than its calories (→ 21-02).

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** a Dutch oven in fire-pit or fireplace coals is your oven. Practise one loaf now.
- **B (moving):** cast iron is **heavy**: one skillet at most; flatbread on a hot stone travels.
- **C (establishing):** a Dutch oven and an outdoor fire pit cover all baking; a masonry oven later
  if you settle long-term.
