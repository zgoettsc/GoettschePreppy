# Cooking on an open fire: fire pit, outdoor fireplace or hearth

**What this page is for.** How to cook real meals over wood: on a fire pit, an outdoor fireplace,
or an indoor fireplace or wood stove built for fire. Coals, heat zones, safe temperatures, and
how to keep allergens and children away from the fire.

> **CAUTION.** **Never run a generator, charcoal, or any engine indoors, in a garage, or near an
> open window** (→ 30-01). A fire pit, a grill and charcoal are **outdoor equipment** and never come
> inside (→ 08-02, → 29-03). An indoor fireplace or wood stove is the only place fire cooking
> happens inside, and that is a decision card (below). Cooking over live fire with small children
> nearby needs a hard boundary. And **undercooked meat is a serious risk when there is no
> emergency room**: use a thermometer; FoodSafety.gov is clear that colour is not a reliable sign
> of doneness.

## Do this now

1. **Cook over coals, not flames.** Flames scorch the outside and leave the middle raw. Burn down
   to a deep bed of glowing coals first; that is the cooking surface.
2. **Use a thermometer:** **165 °F for all poultry, 160 °F for ground meat, 145 °F for whole cuts
   of beef, pork, lamb and veal with a 3-minute rest** (FoodSafety.gov).
3. **Make every fire do several jobs:** cook the meal, boil water (→ 03-04), heat wash water, dry
   boots. Never light a fire for one job (→ 06-04).
4. **Mark a child line** around the fire and enforce it every time (→ 20-02).
5. **If anyone has a food allergy, cook their food first**, in their own marked cookware (procedure
   below, → 02-04).

## Why / how it works

### Coals, not flames

A wood fire has three useful stages:
1. **Flames:** for boiling water fast and for light. Bad for food.
2. **Coals:** steady, controllable heat. **This is where you cook.**
3. **Embers and ash:** dying heat for simmering, keeping food warm and banking the fire (→ 06-02).

Build the fire, let it burn down for 20 to 45 minutes to a glowing bed, then rake the coals into
zones. Burn only dry, seasoned, untreated wood; wet wood smokes and gives little heat (EPA, Burn
Wise; → 06-03).

### Heat zones: the trick that makes fire cooking work

Rake the coals into:
- **A hot zone** (deep coals) for searing and boiling.
- **A medium zone** (thinner coals) for most cooking.
- **A cool zone** (few or no coals) to move food to when it is getting ahead of you.

Move the **food**, not the fire. That is your stove dial.

### Methods

| Method | What it needs | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Grate over coals** | A grill grate | Everyday cooking, boiling, searing |
| **Dutch oven in or over coals** | Cast iron with a lid | Stews, beans, bread, anything long (→ 07-04) |
| **Tripod and hanging pot** | Chain or tripod | Soup, stock, boiling water in bulk |
| **Directly on coals** | Nothing | Potatoes, onions, foil-wrapped food |
| **Flat stone** | A dry, flat stone | Flatbread; a hot stone is a griddle |
| **Skewers** | Green sticks or metal | Quick protein |
| **Ash baking** | Coals and ash | Root vegetables buried in embers |

### Cold-weather realities

{{#if is.cold_winters}}Your January lows average {{ region.home.jan_low_f }} °F, so outdoor
cooking in deep cold is the normal case, not the exception.{{else}}Outdoor cooking in cold, wet or
windy weather needs planning.{{/if}}
- **Everything takes longer.** Cold pots, cold food, wind stripping heat. Plan for double.
- **Wind is the enemy.** A windbreak of split wood, blocks or sheet metal cuts cooking time and
  saves real fuel.
- **Prepare inside, cook outside.** Chopping with numb hands is miserable and dangerous.
- **Cook once, eat twice.** Cook big in the cold, reheat on a smaller fire or the kettle (→ 07-06).
- **Keep water off your gloves.** Wet gloves in deep cold are a cold injury (→ 08-05).
- **Light the path.** It is dark early in winter (→ 10-01).

### Cooking on an indoor fireplace or wood stove

A built, chimneyed fireplace or wood stove is the one place fire cooking can happen indoors.
It runs under the wood-heat rules on → 08-08, not this page's:
- **CO alarm in the room, working, before the first cooking fire** (→ 29-03). The damper stays
  open for the burn and is **never closed on live coals**: coals make carbon monoxide for hours
  after the flames die (→ 08-08).
- **Cook on coals, not flames:** burn down to a bed, set a Dutch oven on and among the coals
  (→ 07-04) with coals on the lid. Flames up the flue are heat and creosote (tar that builds up in
  chimneys and catches fire, → 06-09), not cooking.
- **Keep the child line.** An indoor hearth tempts small children more, not less.

### Food safety without a fridge

- **Use a thermometer.** Appearance and colour do not reliably show doneness (FoodSafety.gov).
- **Poultry 165 °F. Ground meat 160 °F. Whole cuts 145 °F plus a 3-minute rest.**
- **Never partly cook food to finish later.** That is how you grow bacteria.
- **Cool leftovers fast.** In winter, use the cold as a refrigerator (→ 04-09), with a
  thermometer, and do not let food freeze and thaw over and over.
- **Wash hands and surfaces** with what water you have (→ 13-02). A stomach illness in a baby, an
  elder or someone with a chronic condition is an emergency with no medical care (→ 12-05).

### Food allergies at the fire: the procedure

One fire, one grate, one pot is the classic cross-contact setup ("cross-contact" means a trace
of an allergen getting onto safe food). With no ambulance, it is the biggest cooking risk for an
allergic person. Run it the same way every time, numbered so anyone at the fire can follow it:
{{#if has.condition.CL-04}}This household has a food allergy on record (→ 02-04).{{/if}}

1. **The allergic person's food cooks FIRST**, in clean cookware from their own **colour-marked
   pot-and-utensil set**, before anything else touches the heat.
2. **Serve it and cover it** before any of the household's allergens enter the cooking area.
3. **Their utensils are washed and racked separately.** The stirring spoon is never shared, not
   once.
4. **When shared heat cannot be avoided** (one grate, one lid), lay **foil** as a barrier between
   the shared metal and their food.
5. **Wash order: their gear first, in clean water**, never after allergen cookware has been
   through it.
6. **When one pot must feed everyone**, the whole meal is built **only from ingredients safe for
   the allergic person**. There is no "take their portion out before the sauce goes in."
7. **Hands are washed** between handling any allergen and handling their food.

The zero-contact alternative, a verified pouch prepared in its own pouch, is on → 07-07.

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- **An oven rack makes a good grill grate. Not a refrigerator shelf:** many are plated with
  metals that give off toxic fumes over fire.
- **Flat stones as griddles, dry ones only:** wet or porous rock can crack or burst when heated.
- Green hardwood sticks as skewers and pot supports.
- Foil for wrapping food in coals; a metal sheet as a windbreak.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** this is a firewood question (→ 06-04). An open fire is the **least** fuel-efficient
  way you have to cook.
- **Ration:** move everyday cooking to a **rocket stove** (→ 07-02), which uses **roughly half the
  wood** of an open fire for the same cooking; keep the open fire for
  big-batch cooking, boiling volume, and when you want the heat anyway.
- **Resupply or substitute:** solar cooking in summer (→ 07-03) costs no fuel; propane (→ 07-05)
  for speed and bad weather; the kettle (→ 07-06) wherever there is stored power.
- **The efficiency stack:** windbreak, a lid on every pot, coals not flames, and batch cooking
  together can cut wood use by more than half, which is the same as doubling your woodpile.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** set up the outdoor spot properly: grate, windbreak, work surface, child line.
  Smoke and cooking smells advertise that you have food (→ 14-03).
- **B (moving):** small, contained fires only; a grate and a pot travel well. Check fire danger
  before lighting anything in dry country (→ 17-05).
- **C (establishing):** build a roofed outdoor kitchen with a windbreak, then work toward a vented
  indoor stove for winter.
