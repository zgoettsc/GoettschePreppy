# Propane and camp stoves, and why they stay outdoors

**What this page is for.** Choosing and using camp stoves and grills safely, making a limited
fuel supply last, and lighting a gas range when the power is out. It starts with the rule that
keeps people alive in winter outages.

> **SAFETY.** **Never run a generator, charcoal, or any engine indoors, in a garage, or near an
> open window.** Carbon monoxide kills in minutes with no warning; alarms fail; there is no
> mitigation that works every time (→ 30-01). **The same applies to camp stoves and grills built
> for outdoor use:** CDC says never use a portable gas camp stove indoors, never burn charcoal
> indoors, and **never use a gas oven or range to heat your home** (CDC, Carbon Monoxide
> Poisoning; CDC, Winter Weather). Carbon monoxide (CO) has no smell and no colour (→ 29-03).
> "Cracking a window" does not make it safe. **Fuel is never stored or transferred near an open
> flame or a running appliance** (→ 30-01).

## Do this now

1. **Cook outdoors** with any camp stove, grill or charcoal, including in winter. Build a
   sheltered outdoor spot (windbreak, roof or tarp, light) so weather is never the reason to bring
   it inside.
2. **Choose propane or white gas over butane** where winters are cold: butane canisters lose
   pressure badly in the cold and may barely work; propane keeps working, more weakly.
3. **Store cylinders outdoors**, upright and secured, away from doors, windows and anything that
   sparks (→ 06-06).
4. **Keep battery CO alarms** on every level and outside each sleeping area (→ 08-02).
5. **Count what you have:** {{ gear.fuel.propane_20lb }} 20-lb cylinders and
   {{ gear.fuel.propane_1lb }} 1-lb cylinders on hand. If the same tanks also feed a heater or a
   generator, decide now how much is for cooking (→ 06-05).
6. **Save propane for what it does best:** fast boiling, bad weather, illness, and cooking when
   the kettle (→ 07-06) is down and a fire is not practical.

## Why / how it works

### The single rule

Every appliance that burns fuel makes carbon monoxide. Appliances built for **outdoor use** have
no venting and no oxygen-depletion sensor, because they assume unlimited fresh air. Indoors, in a
closed room, in winter, with a family asleep, that assumption kills.

**The tempting scenario:** it is bitterly cold, the power is out, and cooking outside is
miserable. "Just ten minutes, with the window open." That is how people die, and it is worth
deciding **now**, in advance, that the answer is always no. Set things up so the temptation never
comes: a sheltered outdoor cooking spot with a windbreak, a roof or tarp, and a light. An
afternoon's work in fall removes the risk in winter.

### Choosing a stove

The fuels are explained on → 29-01.

| Type | Fuel | Cold performance | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Two-burner propane camp stove** | 1-lb cylinders, or a 20-lb tank with an adapter hose | **Fair to good** | The best all-round backup for a family |
| **Single-burner propane** | 1-lb cylinders | Fair to good | Compact, cheap, good as a spare |
| **Butane canister stove** | Butane cartridges | **Poor in the cold** | Fine in summer; unreliable exactly when you need it |
| **Liquid-fuel (white gas) stove** | White gas or multi-fuel | **Excellent** | Best in deep cold; needs priming and care |
| **Alcohol stove** | Denatured alcohol | Moderate | Simple, quiet, low output |
| **Wood or twig stove** | Sticks | Good | No fuel to carry (→ 07-02) |

A two-burner propane stove with a **20-lb tank and adapter hose** gives a great many cooking
hours cheaply, **outdoors**. If the same tank also runs a heater or a generator, one tank cannot
serve both on the same night.

### Getting the most from the fuel

- **Lids on pots**, always (→ 07-02).
- **A windscreen:** outdoors, wind can double fuel use.
- **Size the flame to the pot:** flames licking up the sides are wasted fuel.
- **Simmer once it is up to temperature;** do not keep it at a hard boil.
- **A 20-lb tank and adapter** is cheaper per meal than 1-lb cylinders.

### Propane in deep cold

Propane feeds a stove by its vapour pressure, and vapour pressure falls as it gets colder. A
**1-lb cylinder** chilled well below freezing, and chilling itself further as it is used, loses
pressure: the flame weakens, and a part-full cylinder can sputter out while still holding fuel. A
20-lb tank does better (more mass, more liquid) but weakens too in hard cold. Keeping a cylinder
warmer (inside a coat, never near a flame) helps a little.

**Do not plan a bitter-cold night around a camp stove.** In deep cold, the better answers are an
electric kettle indoors (→ 07-06: no flame, no CO) or a wood fire outdoors. Propane is the
mild-weather and shoulder-season fast burner; in deep cold it is a backup, with a warmed cylinder,
a windbreak and short boil-only sessions. White-gas stoves are the cold-weather exception.

### Grills

A propane or charcoal grill is a real outdoor cooking appliance, and most households have one.
**Same rule: outdoors only, always.** A grill in a garage with the door open has killed whole
families.

### Cylinders: the three decisions

- **A cylinder larger than 1 lb inside the house** is a decision card (→ 30-03). Propane is
  heavier than air: a leak pools at floor level and finds a flame. The household's choice:
  {{choice DC-02}}.
- **Refilling 1-lb cylinders from a bigger tank** is a decision card (→ 30-04). Makers say
  disposable cylinders are never refilled. The household's choice: {{choice DC-03}}.
- **Storing propane or other fuel in an attached garage** is a decision card (→ 30-06). The
  household's choice: {{choice DC-05}}.

> **Before the collapse:** fire codes limit how much propane may be stored in or near a home
> (NFPA 58, Liquefied Petroleum Gas Code), and many rental agreements forbid it indoors entirely.

### Lighting a gas range when the power is out

{{#if has.gas_any}}This household has natural gas or propane service, so this matters to you.{{else}}This
matters at any destination or shelter with gas service.{{/if}} In most electrical outages **natural
gas keeps flowing**, and a propane tank on site keeps feeding the house, so a gas range is often
a working stove when the lights are not.
- **The electric igniters will be dead. Light the burner with a match: flame FIRST.** Strike the
  match or click the lighter, hold it at the burner, and **only then** turn the gas on. Gas on
  first while you fumble for a light pools gas around your hand. If a burner does not light within
  a couple of seconds, gas off, wait for it to clear, try again.
- **Most modern gas ovens will not work without power.** Their gas valve depends on an electric
  igniter and control board, and they cannot be safely lit by hand. Plan on **stovetop only**;
  bake in a Dutch oven outdoors (→ 07-04). Check your range's manual.
- **A range is for cooking, never for heat** (CDC). Keep sessions to cooking length, open the
  kitchen to the rest of the house, and keep the CO alarm on that level working.
- **Open-flame cooking indoors** (a match-lit range, an indoor hearth) is a decision card
  (→ 30-21). The household's choice: {{choice DC-20}}.

### Safe temperatures

A camp stove does not change the numbers: **165 °F poultry, 160 °F ground meat, venison and
rabbit, 145 °F whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb and veal with a 3-minute rest** (FoodSafety.gov). Use a thermometer (→ 07-01).

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- **Grill tanks** from abandoned grills are a common find, often part-full. Check the valve and
  the date stamp; treat any that hiss as leaking.
- A **rocket stove** (→ 07-02) does everything a camp stove does, on free fuel, more slowly.
- **Alcohol stoves** can be made from cans. **Yellow-bottle fuel-line antifreeze is methanol. It
  burns, with three cautions: the flame is nearly invisible in daylight (never refuel a stove that
  might still be lit; pour water on it first); its vapour and fumes are toxic (outdoors only); and
  swallowed, as little as 2 tablespoons can kill a child, and blindness is common and often
  permanent** (MedlinePlus, Methanol Poisoning). If anyone swallows it: Poison Control,
  1-800-222-1222, and do not make them vomit. **Children do not have access to firearms, fuel,
  medications or chlorine products** (→ 30-01): locked, labelled, never in a drink bottle
  (→ 12-17). Red-bottle (isopropyl) antifreeze is a poor, sooty fuel.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** count cooking days against the share of propane that is really for cooking, and
  write it on → 23-02. If heat or a generator also needs the tanks, cooking's share may be days,
  not a season.
- **Ration:** propane for **speed and weather**: a hot meal in a blizzard, boiling water when it
  is dangerous to be outside long, cooking for someone sick. Everyday boiling goes to the kettle
  (→ 07-06) or wood (→ 07-02).
- **Hold a reserve.** Decide now how much propane you will **never** spend on convenience.
- **Resupply:** wood (→ 06-04) is renewable; propane is not. Plan the change before you are forced
  into it.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** kettle for everyday boils, propane for speed and weather, wood for volume, solar
  in summer. Build the sheltered outdoor spot so winter never forces a bad decision.
- **B (moving):** a small propane or white-gas stove; **never** run it inside a vehicle or tent,
  including to warm up. White gas wins in deep cold.
- **C (establishing):** propane becomes precious and hard to replace; wood becomes the main fuel.
  Bring the adapter hose and any remaining tanks.
