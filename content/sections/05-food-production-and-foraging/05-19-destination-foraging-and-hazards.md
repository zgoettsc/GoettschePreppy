# Foraging and field hazards at each destination

**What this page is for.** What is different about wild food and outdoor hazards at each place
this household might go: the plants, the snakes, the ticks, and the allergy risks that lie on the
ground. Everything on → 05-06 (killers first, identification or nothing, no mushrooms) applies in
full. This page adds what changes when the ground is new to you.

> **SAFETY.** **No wild plant or mushroom is eaten on a "test"; identification or nothing.**
> The deadly ones are not detectable by taste tests and the dose is small (→ 30-01). **Snakebite:
> do not cut the wound, suck the venom, apply a tourniquet or ice, or give alcohol; keep the
> person still and get them to medical care, because antivenom is the treatment and only
> hospitals have it** (CDC/NIOSH, Venomous Snakes). **A fever in the weeks after a tick bite is a
> tick-borne disease until proven otherwise** (CDC, Ehrlichiosis).

## Do this now

1. **Look at each destination's plates in Section 32** (→ 32-00) before your next visit: the
   deadly plants first, then the edibles.
2. **Find out which venomous snakes live at each destination** (the state Extension service
   publishes a guide) and write them in the table below.
3. **Treat outdoor clothing with permethrin** before tick season, and pack fine-tipped tweezers in
   every kit (below).
4. **Write down the nearest hospital** to each destination that can treat snakebite and severe
   allergic reactions (→ 25-03, → 01-07).
5. **Set the household's ground rules for children and food allergies** before the first walk,
   and brief every adult who will watch the children there, including the hosts.

### Nuts underfoot and food allergies

Much of the country, especially wooded land in the East and South, drops **hickory nuts, walnuts,
pecans and acorns** every fall. Hickory nuts, walnuts and pecans are **tree nuts**, a major food
allergen. At a wooded destination the allergen is not in a labelled package; it is **on the
ground, at a small child's height**, disguised as the easiest food on the property.

If anyone in the household has a tree-nut allergy (→ 02-04, → 02-03):
1. **No ground-snacking.** Nothing picked up from the ground or off a plant goes in any child's
   mouth.
2. **Adults check everything.** Any wild or gathered food is identified, checked against the
   allergy list and handed over by an adult.
3. **Nut processing happens away from the allergic person entirely**, with dedicated tools,
   washed hands and the debris cleaned up. Consider not gathering nuts at all.
4. **Epinephrine goes on every walk** if it has been prescribed (→ 02-03).
5. **Fall is the danger season:** the ground is thickest with nuts when everyone is outside.

Whether a food-allergic person eats any wild, gathered or traded food is the household's decision
card (→ 30-19): {{choice DC-18}}.

### Mushrooms: the honest rule, again

**Do not forage mushrooms in a survival situation** (→ 05-06). Some regions make the temptation
worse because **morels** are famous and locally plentiful in spring. If anyone hunts them in normal
times, with an expert, know the killers:
- **False morels** look wrinkled or brain-like rather than honeycomb-pitted, and are chambered or
  stuffed inside rather than hollow. They **can be deadly.** A true morel is **hollow from cap tip
  to stem base**, with the cap joined to the stem.
- **Destroying angels:** clean, all-white gilled mushrooms of the woods. **Symptoms are delayed,
  seem to improve, then the liver fails.** This is why "a white mushroom in the woods" is never
  food.
- **Chanterelle versus jack-o'-lantern:** jack-o'-lanterns (violently sickening) grow in clusters
  on wood and have true gills.

**In a crisis: no mushrooms.** Nothing about a crisis makes the odds better.

### Venomous snakes

The venomous snakes of the US are the **pit vipers** (rattlesnakes, copperheads and cottonmouths)
and the **coral snakes** of the South (CDC/NIOSH, Venomous Snakes). Which ones live where varies by
state; write yours in the destination notes above.

| Snake | What to look for | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Copperhead** | Coppery-tan with **hourglass-shaped crossbands** seen from the side; superbly camouflaged in leaf litter | Woods in the East and South; the one people most often step on |
| **Rattlesnakes** | Heavy body, blotches or chevrons, a **rattle** on the tail; usually rattles, not always | Many species across the country |
| **Cottonmouth (water moccasin)** | Heavy, dark, blocky head; threatened, it holds its ground and gapes a **white mouth**; swims with its **whole body on the surface** (harmless water snakes swim mostly submerged and flee) | Streams, ponds and swamps in the South and Southeast |
| **Coral snake** | Red, yellow and black rings | Southern states |

**Avoidance is nearly everything:** boots and long trousers off mowed ground; **never put hands
or feet where you cannot see** (woodpiles, rock ledges, stepping over logs); a light at night in
warm months; children on cleared paths; leave every snake alone. **Many bites happen to people
trying to kill or handle the snake** (University of Arkansas Extension, Education and caution key
to avoiding venomous snakes). Clear brush, rock piles and lumber near the house and play areas.
Look before you step down a bank or reach into brush at the water's edge (→ 05-08).

**Snakebite response (CDC/NIOSH):**
1. **Move away from the snake.** No second bite, no revenge hunt; a dead snake's head can still
   bite by reflex.
2. **Do NOT cut the wound, suck the venom, apply a tourniquet or ice, or give alcohol.** Suction
   "extractor" kits do not work.
3. **Remove rings, watches and tight clothing** near the bite before swelling starts.
4. **Keep the person calm and still**; keep the bitten limb still, roughly at heart level.
5. **Get to medical care. Antivenom is the treatment and only hospitals have it.** Mark the time
   of the bite and draw a line at the edge of the swelling with a pen, with the time.
6. **If care truly cannot be reached:** rest, fluids, wound care and watching for infection
   (→ 12-04) are what you have. That is why **avoidance is the real medical plan.**

### Ticks

Ticks carry several serious diseases, and some regions have very heavy tick loads (the lone star
tick across the South and East above all). Two to know by name:
- **Alpha-gal syndrome:** a lone star tick bite can cause an **allergy to red meat** (beef, pork,
  venison, rabbit and other mammals), with reactions **delayed 2 to 6 hours** after eating: hives,
  stomach symptoms, up to anaphylaxis (CDC, Alpha-gal Syndrome). For a household whose protein
  plan is game and livestock, this is a serious threat.
- **Ehrlichiosis:** **fever, chills, severe headache, muscle aches, sometimes a rash, about 1 to
  2 weeks after a bite.** Untreated it can be severe; **doxycycline is the treatment** (CDC,
  Ehrlichiosis; → 12-04).

**The routine (CDC, Preventing Tick Bites):**
- **Permethrin on clothing and boots** (0.5 % clothing treatment, sprayed on fabric and dried
  before wearing; **never on skin**). **Wet permethrin is highly toxic to cats**: keep cats away
  until it is dry (→ 20-09). Retreat as the label says. An EPA-registered repellent (DEET or
  picaridin) on skin.
- **Trousers tucked into socks; light-coloured clothes; stay in the middle of paths.**
- **Daily full-body tick checks on everyone** after any time outdoors. Children at bath time:
  hairline, ears, waistband, groin, behind the knees. Check pets too.
- **Removal: fine-tipped tweezers, grip as close to the skin as possible, pull straight up with
  steady pressure.** No matches, no petroleum jelly, no twisting. Wash the site; write the date
  down and watch for fever or rash for the next weeks.
- **Clothes into a hot dryer for 10 minutes** after field days kills ticks that rode home.

### Chiggers and poison ivy

- **Chiggers:** tiny mites in brush and tall grass that leave intensely itchy welts at the
  waistband and sock line. Permethrin-treated clothing helps; a soapy shower soon after exposure
  helps. The risk is scratched-open skin getting infected (→ 12-04).
- **Poison ivy** is far heavier in some regions, including **thick, hairy climbing vines** on the
  trees you would cut for firewood. Three leaflets; leafless winter vines still carry the oil;
  **never burn it** (the smoke injures lungs, → 17-05). Wash skin, tools and clothes after
  clearing; pets carry the oil on fur.

**Scavenge or substitute:** the hosts and neighbours at a destination know its snake spots, tick
seasons and berry patches. **Local knowledge is the best field guide** (→ 18-03).

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** permethrin and repellent are consumables. Stock several seasons; clothing treatment
  goes furthest.
- **Ration:** when repellent is gone, clothing discipline, path discipline and daily checks do
  most of the work, and cost nothing.
- **There is no substitute for antivenom.** Avoidance is the plan.

## Phase notes

- **B (moving):** the tick routine applies from the moment you leave the vehicle on any leg
  through tick country; treat travel clothes before you leave.
- **C (at a destination):** set the children's and allergy ground rules the first day; clear brush
  and rock near the house the first week; start the tick-check routine the first night.
