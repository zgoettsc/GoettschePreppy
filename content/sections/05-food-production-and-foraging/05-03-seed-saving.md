# Seed saving and a survival seed stock

**What this page is for.** How to build a seed stock that keeps a garden going for years, and
how to save your own seed so it never runs out.

> **CAUTION.** **Hybrid (F1) seed does not breed true.** "Hybrid" or "F1" on the packet means
> the plant is a cross of two parent lines; seed saved from it grows into a scattered, often
> useless mix. For a renewable garden you must buy **open-pollinated** or **heirloom** seed
> (varieties that come back true from their own seed). Separately, **treated seed** (coated
> with a fungicide, usually dyed pink, blue or green) **is not food**: never eat it and never
> sprout it (→ 04-16).

## Do this now

1. **Buy open-pollinated or heirloom seed**, not hybrids, for anything you mean to save.
2. **Buy a real seed stock now** and store it cool, dark, dry and sealed.
3. **Learn the easy three first: beans, peas and tomatoes.** They pollinate themselves and save
   reliably with almost no technique.
4. **Label everything** with variety and year. Unlabeled seed is a guess.
5. **Split the stock.** Keep a working stock at home and a sealed duplicate somewhere else
   cool and dry: with family, at a destination{{#if has.destinations}} (→ 05-18 lists the
   varieties suited to each of yours){{/if}}, or in a second building. One cache can be lost;
   two rarely are.
6. **Test germination** on older seed each spring before you rely on it.

## Why / how it works

### One season or many

A stored seed packet feeds you once. **A saved-seed cycle feeds you indefinitely.** In a long
emergency, seed becomes one of the most valuable things a household can have, for food and for
trade (→ 18-02).

Most modern commercial seed is hybrid, bred for uniformity in the first generation only.
Renewable gardening means choosing open-pollinated varieties **before** you need them.

### Easy to hard, for saving

**Pollination** is how a flower is fertilised so it makes seed. **Self-pollinating** crops
fertilise themselves inside one flower. **Cross-pollinating** crops are fertilised by pollen
carried by insects or wind from another plant, so two varieties growing near each other mix.

| Difficulty | Crops | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Easy**: self-pollinating | **Beans, peas, tomatoes, lettuce** | Little or no crossing; just dry and store |
| **Moderate**: needs isolation | **Peppers**, squash, cucumbers, melons, corn | Cross readily. Sweet and hot peppers in one yard **will** cross, and the next generation is a hot/sweet lottery. Isolate, bag the blossoms, or grow one variety |
| **Hard**: biennial (two-year cycle) | **Carrots, beets, cabbage, onions** | Flower only in their second year. Where the ground freezes hard, dig them in fall, store them cool (→ 04-09) and replant in spring; left in frozen ground they die. In mild-winter climates they can stay in the ground |
| **Specialist** | Potatoes | Grown from saved tubers ("seed potatoes"), not true seed |

**Start with the easy tier.** Beans and peas self-pollinate, dry on the plant, store for years,
and are rich in calories and protein.

**Keeping varieties true, in one table:**

| Crop | To keep it true |
|---|---|
| Beans, peas, lettuce, tomato | A few feet between varieties is enough |
| **Peppers** | **Bag the blossoms** (tie a scrap of row cover over a flower cluster until fruit sets) **or grow one variety** |
| Squash, cucumber, melon | One variety per species, or hand-pollinate at dawn and tape the flower shut (→ 05-12) |
| Corn | One variety, or keep it far from any other corn: wind carries corn pollen, and popcorn crosses with sweet corn (University of Minnesota Extension) |
| Cabbage family, beets, chard, onions, carrots | One variety flowering per year (beets and chard cross; so do all the cabbage-family crops) |

### Saving the easy three, and peppers

- **Beans and peas:** let the pods dry brown and rattly on the plant (up to a month after you
  would pick them to eat). Dry them indoors at least two weeks before shelling, dry further
  indoors, store.
- **Tomatoes:** scoop the seeds and gel into a jar with a little water, swirl it twice a day and
  let it ferment until the seeds sink, within about five days (University of Minnesota
  Extension, Saving Vegetable Seeds). Pour off the liquid, rinse, then dry thoroughly on a
  plate.
- **Peppers:** from a bagged blossom or your only variety, let the fruit ripen fully, scrape the
  seed, dry it.

**Dry is everything.** Seed stored with any moisture moulds or dies. Dry it longer than feels
necessary, then store it with a desiccant (a drying agent such as silica gel packets).

### Storing seed

- **Cool, dark, dry and steady.** Between 32 and 41 °F is ideal, so a refrigerator works,
  sealed in a glass jar (University of Minnesota Extension). Otherwise a basement or the coldest
  corner of the house, sealed.
- **Sealed jars with silica gel.** Silica gel can be dried out again in a warm oven and reused.
- **Freezing extends life a lot** for thoroughly dried seed, if it stays sealed against
  moisture. A winter outbuilding or a freezer works.
- **Label:** variety, year saved, where it came from.

### How long seed lasts

Approximate seed life, stored cool and dry (University of Illinois Extension, Will My Seeds
Still Grow?):

| Seed | Approximate life |
|---|---|
| Onion, parsnip, parsley | 1 year |
| Leek, sweet corn, pepper, okra | 2 years |
| Bean, pea, carrot, broccoli, kohlrabi, spinach, celery | 3 years |
| Tomato, beet, cabbage, cauliflower, kale, squash, pumpkin, watermelon, chard, eggplant | 4 years |
| Cucumber, muskmelon, radish, collards, endive | 5 years |
| Lettuce | 6 years |

The University of Minnesota advises using your own saved seed within a year where you can; older
seed sprouts less and grows weaker plants.

**Germination test** (University of Illinois Extension): put 10 seeds on a damp paper towel,
fold it into a bag or box so it stays damp, label it, and leave it somewhere warm (no light
needed) for the days to sprouting on the packet. **All 10 sprout:** sow as normal. **7 to 9:**
sow more thickly. **6 or fewer:** replace the seed if you can; the plants it makes will be
weak.

### Building the stock

Buy for **calories, storage and reliability in your season** (bands on → 05-01):
- **Beans (dry and green), peas**: protein, easy saving.
- **Seed potatoes**: highest calorie return in most gardens. Buy certified seed potatoes;
  grocery potatoes may be treated to stop sprouting.
- **Winter squash**: calories and long storage.
- **Carrots, beets, cabbage, onions**: storage crops.
- **Tomatoes, cucumbers**: preserving crops (→ 04-04).
- **Kale, chard, spinach, lettuce**: fast greens and season extension (→ 05-02).
- **Long-season bands only:** sweet potato slips, okra, southern peas (cowpeas).
- **Herbs**: flavour matters more than you think over months (→ 21-02).

**Leave out any crop that is one of your household's food allergens** (→ 02-04).

### Seed as currency

Seed is close to an ideal trade good (→ 18-02): light, storable, valuable, divisible, and **it
multiplies**. Giving seed away costs you almost nothing and builds goodwill and a wider local
food supply.

## Gear / items

**Have it now (buy toward this):**

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Open-pollinated or heirloom seed**, several years' stock | The renewable foundation | **Not** hybrid |
| Certified seed potatoes (each spring) | Highest calorie crop | Not grocery potatoes |
| Airtight jars and **silica gel** | Dryness is everything | Re-dry the gel with heat |
| Envelopes, labels, marker | Variety and year | Unlabeled seed is useless |
| Fine screens or plates for drying | Processing saved seed | Improvise |
| Paper towels and bags | Germination tests | Free |
| **A printed seed-saving reference** | Isolation distances, techniques | Keep with your documents (→ 19-01) |

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- **Dry beans from the grocery store usually grow.** So do whole dried peas and popcorn.
- **Neighbours and local gardeners** are the best source of varieties that already suit your
  climate (→ 18-01).
- Any dry jar in any cool corner beats no storage.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** seed life is measured in years (table above), but **a saved-seed cycle never runs
  out**.
- **Ration:** always save more seed than you think you need, and **never eat the seed stock**.
  That is the classic famine mistake.
- **Resupply:** trade with neighbours (→ 18-02), grocery dry beans, and perennials that need no
  replanting (→ 05-13).
- **Why it matters:** seed is the only food input in this binder that **multiplies**. A handful
  of beans becomes pounds.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** buy the stock, learn the easy three, save seed this season, and place the
  duplicate while trips are ordinary.
- **B (moving):** seed is light and it is next year's food: **take it** (→ 01-03). With a
  duplicate already placed, a lost load is not a lost garden.
- **C (establishing):** more space means easier isolation of the harder crops. A longer season
  at a destination ripens seed from biennials and late crops more reliably (→ 05-18).
