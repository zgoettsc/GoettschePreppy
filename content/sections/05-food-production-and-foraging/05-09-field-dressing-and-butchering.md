# Field dressing and butchering

**What this page is for.** Turning a large animal, usually a deer, into safe meat: the order of
work, the disease checks, the cuts, and how to keep it. It is a sequence reminder, not a
substitute for hands-on teaching or an illustrated guide.

> **SAFETY.** Three hazards. **Spoilage:** body heat ruins meat within hours. **Contamination:**
> a cut gut spreads bacteria over the carcass. **Disease:** chronic wasting disease (CWD) in
> deer, elk and moose is caused by a prion, an infectious protein, and **cooking does not
> destroy it**: never eat meat from an animal that tested positive or looked sick, however it is
> cooked (CDC). **Low-acid foods are pressure-canned to the published time and pressure, or not
> canned** (→ 30-01): meat is low-acid. And **sharp knives and tired hands cut people**; with no
> emergency room a deep hand cut is a serious injury (→ 12-02).

## Do this now

1. **Have the preservation plan ready before you hunt** (→ 05-07): jars, lids, salt and fuel.
2. **Field dress immediately.** The clock starts the moment the animal is down.
3. **Keep it clean and keep it cool.** Those two rules cover most of meat safety.
4. **Where CWD is known, test every deer before anyone eats it, while testing exists**, and hold
   the meat until the result is back. **Bone it out; do not saw through the spine.**
5. **Practise on small game first** (→ 05-16). Rabbits and birds teach the anatomy cheaply.

> **Before the collapse:** many state wildlife agencies run CWD testing in the hunting season,
> often free, with head drop-off sites. Use it while it exists.

## Why / how it works

### The three rules

1. **Speed.** Body heat spoils meat from the inside. Gut it fast.
2. **Cleanliness.** Do not cut the stomach, intestines or bladder. If you do, wash the mess
   away at once and trim generously.
3. **Cooling.** Get the carcass cool quickly. In cold weather this is easy; in a warm early
   season it is the whole problem.

### Field dressing, in outline

"Field dressing" means removing the guts where the animal fell.

1. **Position** the animal, ideally head uphill.
2. **Open the belly carefully:** a shallow cut through the hide only, with your fingers guiding
   the blade so it does not cut the gut.
3. **Free and remove the guts** without cutting the bladder or intestines.
4. **Remove the diaphragm, heart and lungs.** Save the **heart and liver** if you want them.
   **Not the spleen and not the lymph nodes** (CWD, below).
5. **Drain and wipe out** the body cavity: clean water if you have it, otherwise cloth or grass.
6. **Prop the cavity open** and get air moving through it.
7. **Hang it, skin it, let it cool.** In cold weather, hanging for a few days improves the meat.

**Save the fat** (→ 04-12): deer fat renders for tallow, candles and soap even if it is not
prized for cooking. **Save the bones** for broth, cooked on a fire that is already lit. **Save
the hide** if you have the skill and time.

### Disease and safety

- **Chronic wasting disease** is in deer in many states, and **cooking does not destroy the
  prion.** CDC's advice: **test before eating** where CWD is known; **bone out the meat**, do not
  saw through spine or skull; **trim away the lymph nodes**; do not eat brain, spinal cord, eyes,
  spleen or lymph nodes; use dedicated knives, not the kitchen's; wear gloves. Never eat an
  animal that tested positive or looked sick.
- **Lead fragments** (→ 05-07): a lead-core bullet sheds fragments **12 to 18 inches from the
  wound channel**, too small to see. Trim that far and throw out bloodshot meat. Children under 6
  and pregnant or nursing women were the groups advised to avoid venison from lead-shot deer
  (Minnesota DNR, Lead in venison). Copper bullets remove the problem.
- **Wear gloves.** Nitrile gloves are cheap and sensible.
- **Do not eat an animal that looked or behaved sick:** thin, confused, or with abnormal tissue.
- **Tularemia** ("rabbit fever") is a real risk in rabbits and hares. **The field check:** a wild
  rabbit whose **liver or spleen shows small white or yellow spots, or whose liver is enlarged,
  is thrown out whole**: bury it, eat none of it, wash up (CDC). **Gloves when dressing rabbits,
  no rubbing eyes or face, clean the knife after**: the bacterium enters through skin and through
  dust from skinning. Cold weather after hard frosts reduces but does not remove the risk. (The
  same spotted liver in a **domestic** rabbit is coccidiosis, → 05-05.)
- **Wash hands and tools thoroughly**, and keep the work away from your water supply (→ 03-08).

### Butchering into usable cuts

- **Break it down** into backstraps (loins), tenderloins, shoulders, hindquarters, ribs, neck
  and trim, **boning out, not sawing through the spine** (CWD, above).
- **Prime cuts** (backstraps, tenderloins, whole hindquarter muscles) for roasting or steaks.
- **Everything else** (shoulders, neck, ribs, trim) becomes **ground meat, stew meat, jerky or
  canned meat**. In a survival setting this is most of the animal. From a lead-shot deer this is
  also the meat most likely to hold fragments.
- **Cooking:** FoodSafety.gov gives **160 °F for ground meat** and **145 °F with a 3-minute rest
  for whole cuts**. Cooking all game to 160 °F adds a margin when there is no medical care.
- **Trim silver skin** (the tough shiny membrane) and connective tissue.
- **Keep it cold throughout.** Work in batches so the rest stays cool.

### Preserving: decide before you start

| Method | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Pressure canning** (→ 04-05) | **The most reliable without a freezer** | NCHFP has game procedures; shelf-stable and ready to eat |
| **Winter cold / freezing** (→ 04-09) | Everything, when reliably below freezing | Free but temporary; measure it with a thermometer |
| **Drying / jerky** (→ 04-06) | Lean cuts | Follow the tested procedure; meat drying is not fruit drying |
| **Curing and smoking** (→ 04-07) | Traditional | Needs the correct cure and salt |
| **Fresh** | Straight away | Eat the organs and trim first |

**Pressure canning is usually the right answer for a deer** in a long outage: shelf-stable, no
ongoing cold, and the meat comes out ready to eat with no more fuel (National Center for Home
Food Preservation, Canning Meat, Poultry and Game; → 29-08).

## Gear / items

**Have it now (buy toward this):**

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Sharp knives** (skinning and boning) and a steel or stone | The core tools | Sharp is safer than dull (→ 16-02) |
| Bone saw or hacksaw | Quartering | **Not through a deer's spine or skull** (CWD): bone out instead |
| **Nitrile gloves**, by the box | Disease protection | Use them |
| Game bags | Keeping meat clean while it cools | Reusable |
| Gambrel, rope, or block and tackle | Hanging a deer | A tree limb and rope work |
| Large cleanable cutting surface | Butchering | |
| Coolers and ice, or reliable winter cold | Cooling is a safety step | Measure it |
| **Preservation gear ready** | Jars, lids, salt, canner, fuel | → 04-05, → 04-07 |
| Instant-read thermometer | Cooking temperatures | → 07-01 |
| **Illustrated butchering guide, printed** | Anatomy under pressure | → 19-01 |

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- One good sharp knife does everything; the rest is convenience.
- Rope over a branch replaces a gambrel.
- Clean bedsheets as game bags; a door on sawhorses as a table.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** the consumables are **gloves, jars and lids** (→ 04-05) and **salt** (→ 04-13).
  Knives last if you keep them sharp (→ 16-02).
- **Ration:** use every part: organs, bones for broth, fat for rendering, hide if you can.
- **Resupply:** salt and canning lids are the items to stock now.
- **Why it matters:** a deer is only food if you can **keep** it. Butchering skill without
  preserving capacity turns a whole deer into a few days of eating and a lot of waste.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** butchering in a town backyard raises visibility and sanitation problems
  (→ 14-03, → 13-03). Plan where, and how to get rid of the remains.
- **B (moving):** you are not butchering on the move; carry preserved meat.
- **C (establishing):** far easier: space, distance from neighbours, cold outbuildings for
  hanging.
