# Foraging wild plants, and the deadly look-alikes

**What this page is for.** The rules for eating wild plants anywhere in the US, the pairs of
plants that kill people who confuse them, and the few starter plants that are hard to get
wrong. **It is about risk, not a field guide.** The plates on this page exist so you can
recognise the plants that kill, not so you can identify food.

> **SAFETY.** **No wild plant or mushroom is eaten on a "test"; identification or nothing.**
> The deadly ones are not detectable by taste tests and the dose is small (→ 30-01).
> **Water hemlock is the most violently toxic plant that grows in North America** (USDA
> Agricultural Research Service); a piece of root 2 to 3 cm long (about an inch) can kill an
> adult (CDC MMWR, Water Hemlock Poisoning, 1994). It grows in wet ground in every region and
> resembles plants people forage. There is no antidote.

> **If someone has eaten a suspect plant:** call **Poison Control, 1-800-222-1222**, while
> phones work, and keep a piece of the plant. **Do not make them vomit.** Water hemlock sickness
> starts within 15 to 90 minutes of eating (CDC MMWR, 1994), and seizures can follow within the
> hour (USDA Forest Service). In a seizure: lay the person on their side, keep the airway clear, give
> nothing by mouth, and protect them from injury until the seizure passes. Wash skin that
> touched the sap; the poison (cicutoxin) is absorbed through skin (CDC MMWR, 1994). → 12-17.

## Do this now

1. **Study the plates for your home region below**: the hemlocks first, then their
   look-alikes. Twenty minutes now.
2. **Buy two regional field guides with photographs**, for your own part of the country, and
   learn plants **now**, in your own yard, while a mistake costs nothing.
3. **Learn the poisonous plants first**, before any edible one.
4. **Never use a "universal edibility test."** It does not protect against the plants that
   kill. Where the military handbook in Section 31 (→ 31-17) describes one, the floor above governs.
5. **Positive identification or nothing:** several matching features, the right habitat, the
   right season, checked against two sources.
6. **Write the household's rule for anyone with a food allergy** (below, → 30-19).

## Why / how it works

### What foraging is worth

Wild plants are mostly water and fibre. A day of foraging yields greens and a few calories:
worth it for **vitamins and variety**, not for keeping a family alive. Its real value:
- **Micronutrients** in spring, when stored food is nutritionally thin (→ 04-18).
- **Variety and morale** (→ 21-02).
- **Seasonal fruit and nuts** you can dry and keep (→ 05-17).

Trapping (→ 05-15), fishing (→ 05-08) and hunting (→ 05-07) return far more calories per hour,
at far lower risk. The garden (→ 05-01) is foraging with the risk removed.

### The killers that grow almost everywhere: learn these first

| Plant | Where | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Water hemlock** (*Cicuta* species) | **Wet** ground: ditches, stream banks, wet meadows, springs, pond edges | The most violently poisonous plant in North America. Resembles wild carrot and parsnip family plants |
| **Poison hemlock** (*Conium maculatum*) | **Drier** disturbed ground: fields, **garden beds**, ditches, fence lines, roadsides | Deadly to eat, even a small amount. **Its first-year rosette comes up in garden beds and is mistaken for parsley or carrot tops** (→ 05-01) |
| **Death camas** (several species) | Grassland, open woods, mostly in the West and Plains | Looks like wild onion **but has no onion smell**. All species are deadly |
| **Nightshades** | Disturbed ground, gardens, fence lines | Berries attractive to children |
| **Wild mushrooms** | Everywhere | Treat as off limits (below) |

**Two that hurt without being eaten:**

| Plant | Where | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Wild parsnip** | Roadsides, ditches, old fields | **Sap burns and blisters skin in sunlight.** Yellow flowers: the easy tell from the white-flowered hemlocks |
| **Poison ivy** (and poison oak, poison sumac) | Edges, fence lines, woods, riverbanks | Blistering rash on contact. Poison ivy has **three leaflets**. The oil stays active on clothes, tools and pets' fur, and **burning it puts the oil in your lungs** (→ 17-05) |

### The hemlocks, side by side

Both hemlocks are **tall** (water hemlock up to about 6 feet, USDA Forest Service; poison
hemlock up to 2 to 3 metres, about 6 to 10 feet, USDA ARS), with **hollow stems that branch at
the top**, often **mottled or streaked with purple**. **Smell proves nothing, either way.** Crushed poison
hemlock usually smells musty or mousy; water hemlock's root smells of carrot or parsnip. A
carrot-family plant that smells "right" can still be hemlock. **Do not crush and sniff unknown
plants as a test.** Use stem, leaf and habitat.

| | Poison hemlock | Water hemlock |
|---|---|---|
| **Ground** | **Dry** disturbed ground | **Wet** ground |
| **Stem** | Smooth, hairless, **purple blotches** | **Purple streaks**, stout |
| **Leaf** | Very finely divided, **fern-like lace** | **Coarsely toothed** leaflets whose **side veins run to the notches between the teeth, not to the tooth tips**: the one leaf feature that separates water hemlock from the edible carrot-family plants (USDA ARS; USDA Forest Service) |
| **Root** | Long white taproot | **Chambered** rootstalk with poisonous liquid. Confirmation only; **never cut one open to check** |

**The carrot family (Apiaceae) is the family to avoid entirely.** It contains wild carrot
(Queen Anne's lace), wild parsnip, and both hemlocks. Unless you are genuinely expert, **eat
nothing from this family.** The downside is death; the upside is a poor root vegetable you
could have grown.

### The look-alike pairs

These are the pairs where people die. Learn the dangerous half first.

| You are looking for | It is confused with | The difference |
|---|---|---|
| Wild carrot, wild parsnip, wild celery | **Water hemlock, poison hemlock**, fool's parsley | Do not eat this family at all (above) |
| Wild onion, wild garlic | **Death camas**, fly poison, star-of-Bethlehem, daffodil bulbs | **Onion smell from every bulb, checked with clean hands** (onion on your fingers carries to the next bulb). If any bulb in a clump does not smell of onion, throw out the whole clump |
| Ramps (wild leeks, eastern woods) | **Lily-of-the-valley, false hellebore**, autumn crocus | Crush a leaf from **each** plant: no onion-garlic smell means poison. False hellebore has pleated leaves on a stem |
| Cattail shoots | **Blue flag iris** (and other irises), toxic to gut and skin | Only harvest shoots or roots from a stand you identified **by its brown spikes the previous summer**. Cattail leaves are flat and spongy with a rounded base; iris leaves fan out from a flat base |
| Wild grape | **Moonseed**, poisonous; also Virginia creeper and pokeweed | Grapes have **tendrils** and several small seeds; moonseed has **no tendrils** and **one crescent-shaped seed**: cut a fruit and look. Virginia creeper has five separate leaflets; pokeweed is an upright plant with berries in long strings on magenta stems |
| Milkweed shoots | **Dogbane**, a heart poison, in the same ground with the same milky sap | Left off the edible list in this binder: the features are too fine for printed text |
| Elderberry (cooked) | **Water hemlock** in the same wet ground; **pokeweed**; red elderberry | Elderberry is a woody shrub with paired leaves; hemlock is a soft, hollow-stemmed plant that dies back each winter. Pokeweed berries hang in long strings on soft magenta stems. Red elderberry has dome-shaped flower clusters and red berries. Raw elderberries and all green parts of elder cause vomiting: cook the berries |
| Dandelion | Common groundsel | Dandelion has one flower per **hollow, leafless** stalk and leaves only at the ground; groundsel has leafy branching stems with many small flower heads |
| Purslane | **Spotted spurge** (milky sap, irritant) | Break a stem: purslane has clear juice and fleshy leaves; spurge has **milky** sap and thin leaves |
| Chokecherry | Common buckthorn | Chokecherries hang in long strings; buckthorn berries cluster tight against the twig, often with a thorn at the twig tip |
| Blueberries | **Nightshades** (deadly nightshade, black nightshade) | Learn blueberries in person first; never let children pick dark berries unsupervised |
| Staghorn sumac (red upright cones) | **Poison sumac** | Poison sumac has **drooping white berries** and grows in swamps |
| Morel mushrooms | **False morels**, which can kill | See mushrooms, below. Not on the edible list |

### Mushrooms: the honest rule

**Do not forage mushrooms in a survival situation.** Deadly species in every region look like
edibles. Symptoms of the worst (amatoxin, in death caps and destroying angels) are **delayed by
hours**, seem to improve, and then the liver fails. There is no treatment available to you, and
the food value is negligible. **It is the worst risk-reward trade in foraging.**

plant in your hand, and ideally a person who knows it.

### Rules that keep you alive

1. **Identification or nothing.** Several features, the right habitat, the right season, two
   sources. No taste test, no edibility test (→ 30-01).
2. **Learn the poisonous plants first.**
3. **Never eat from the carrot family** unless you are expert.
4. **No mushrooms.**
5. **One new plant at a time**, a small amount, then wait and watch before eating more, and only
   for **plants you have already identified for certain**: this checks personal tolerance and
   allergy. It is not a way to identify anything.
6. **Never children first.** Teach "we only eat what a grown-up hands us."
7. **Cover up and wash.** Long sleeves and gloves in brush; wash skin and clothes after, and
   wash pets that have been in it (→ 20-09).
8. **Avoid contaminated ground:** roadsides (exhaust, salt), sprayed lawns and rights-of-way,
   industrial sites, and floodwater ground (→ 03-07).
9. **Cook what can be cooked.** Cooking reduces some toxins and kills parasites. It never makes a
   poisonous plant safe.

### The safe, hard-to-mistake starters

These grow across most of the country, mostly as **weeds in your own yard**. Learn them in
person, from a guide and ideally a person who knows them. Check that each one appears on your
region's safe-starter plates (→ 05-17, → 32-61); not every plant grows everywhere. Each one's
look-alike is in the table above.

- **Dandelion:** leaves, roots, flower buds. Hard to mistake once you check the stalk (hollow,
  leafless, one flower).
- **Broadleaf plantain:** common lawn weed; leaves edible.
- **Lamb's quarters and amaranth (pigweed):** good cooked greens, **with a caution**: both build
  up nitrate and oxalate. Boil them and throw out the water; eat them as part of a varied diet,
  not a staple; skip plants from heavily manured or drought-stressed ground. Nitrate is most
  dangerous to babies and toddlers (it stops blood carrying oxygen). Write the household's rule
  for young children here: ______________________{{#if has.young_child}} (this household has a
  child under 6){{/if}}.
- **Purslane:** succulent garden weed. Clear juice, never milky (spurge, above).
- **Stinging nettle:** cooked only (cooking removes the sting). Wear gloves to pick it.
- **Wild onion and garlic:** **only if every bulb smells of onion** (look-alike table above).
- **Cattail:** only from a stand identified by its brown spikes (above), and beware the water it
  grows in (→ 03-07).
- **Berries you already know:** mulberry, raspberry and blackberry, wild plum. **Chokecherry
  and wild plum pits contain cyanide-forming compounds: never crush or eat the pits.**

### Food allergies and wild food

Wild food is **unlabeled by definition**, and nobody can tell you what it was processed with.
**Any wild nut is a tree nut.** For anyone with a food allergy the risk of a reaction adds to
the risk of misidentification, and in a crisis neither can be treated properly.

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- **Your own lawn and garden weeds** are the highest-value, lowest-risk foraging there is.
- **A knowledgeable local person** is worth more than any book (→ 18-03).
- **The garden** (→ 05-01) is foraging without the risk. Prefer it.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** foraging is **seasonal and local**. In cold regions there is almost nothing from
  the first hard frost to spring, which is when you most need food (→ 05-17).
- **Ration:** taking a whole patch destroys it. Take a fraction; leave the rest.
- **Resupply:** the garden, sprouting (→ 04-16), trapping, fishing and hunting: all higher
  return, all lower risk.
- **The conclusion:** learn foraging as a **supplement and a skill**, practise it now while
  errors are survivable, and never let the food plan depend on it.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** yard weeds and parks. Watch for sprayed ground and for being seen.
- **B (moving):** little value while travelling. Tired, hurried and on unfamiliar ground is
  exactly when people make fatal identification errors.
- **C (establishing):** more ground and better plants, but the same rules, and further from help.
  Check the destination's plates in Section 32 (→ 32-00) and → 05-19 before you forage there.
