# Processing poultry and small game

**What this page is for.** Turning a chicken, a rabbit or a trapped animal into clean, safe meat:
the order of work, the hygiene that protects you, and how to handle it with children around.

> **SAFETY.** **Raw poultry carries *Salmonella* and *Campylobacter*.** Cross-contamination
> during processing is a real illness risk, and with no medical care a bad stomach illness in a
> baby, an elder or someone with a chronic condition is an emergency (→ 12-05). **Cook poultry to
> 165 °F and rabbit to 160 °F** (FoodSafety.gov). **A bird that died on its own is never food.**
> Work **outside**, keep it clean, and keep children away from raw carcasses and the water used.
> **Low-acid foods are pressure-canned to the published time and pressure, or not canned**
> (→ 30-01).

## Do this now

1. **Process outdoors**, away from the kitchen, the garden and any stored water (→ 03-08).
2. **Set up before you start:** kill area, scald or skin area, gutting area, chilling container,
   in that order, so nothing moves backwards.
3. **Plenty of clean water.** This is a water-hungry job (→ 03-01).
4. **Gloves and dedicated tools.** Raw poultry never touches anything used for other food,
   especially any allergy-safe cookware (→ 02-04).
5. **Chill the carcass fast**, then preserve or cook.

## Why / how it works

### First rule: what is never food

**A bird that died on its own is never food**, and neither is any bird from a flock with sudden
multiple deaths, swollen heads or purple combs. Assume bird flu (→ 05-04; USDA APHIS, Defend the
Flock): gloves and a mask, do not compost it, bury it deep, and process nothing from that flock.

### Poultry: the sequence

1. **Hold back feed** for several hours beforehand (water available). A full crop and gut make
   gutting messier and riskier.
2. **Kill quickly and humanely.** A killing cone (a funnel that holds the bird head-down) and a
   very sharp knife is the standard method. Speed and calm handling matter for welfare and for
   the meat.
3. **Bleed out** fully.
4. **Scald or skin.**
   - **Scalding** (a short dip in hot water, well below boiling) loosens feathers for plucking.
     Test by pulling a wing feather: when it comes out easily, the bird is ready. Too hot tears
     the skin; too cool and the feathers hold.
   - **Skinning** is faster and needs no hot water. You lose the skin and some fat, but when fuel
     is short (→ 06-04) you avoid heating a big pot.
5. **Pluck** (if scalded): main feathers, then the small pin feathers.
6. **Gut it** ("eviscerate"): cut carefully around the vent (the rear opening), then remove the
   insides **without cutting the intestines or the gall bladder** (bile ruins meat). Save heart,
   liver and gizzard if you want them.
7. **Rinse and chill at once:** cold water or an ice bath, then cold storage.
8. **Rest** the carcass a day cold before cooking or preserving; freshly killed birds are tough.

### Rabbit: simpler

1. **Kill quickly and humanely.**
2. **Skin:** rabbit hide peels off readily.
3. **Gut it**, avoiding the bladder and intestines.
4. **Rinse, chill, rest.**
5. **Gloves, and check the liver and spleen before keeping the meat.** In a **wild** rabbit,
   **small white or yellow spots on the liver or spleen, or an enlarged liver, mean tularemia:
   throw out the whole animal, bury it, wash up, eat none of it** (CDC; → 05-15). In a
   **domestic** rabbit the same spots are coccidiosis: the meat is fine cooked to 160 °F; throw
   out the liver (→ 05-05). No rubbing eyes or face while skinning; clean the knife after.

### Raccoon and opossum, if the trapline brings them

The same sequence as rabbit, with two hard rules (→ 05-15): **cook every part to 160 °F
throughout** (trichinosis; freezing, smoking and curing do not make wild-carnivore meat safe;
CDC), and treat raccoon **droppings and fur as hazardous** (a roundworm that can cripple or kill
toddlers): gloves, no children anywhere near the carcass or gut pile, burn or deep-bury the
remains, never process near the kitchen.

### Hygiene: the part that protects you

- **One-way workflow.** Dirty (kill, feathers, guts) → clean (rinse, chill). Never carry a clean
  carcass back through the dirty area.
- **Dedicated tools and surfaces** for raw poultry, never the ones used for allergy-safe food.
- **Wash hands** at every change of task (→ 13-02).
- **Get rid of guts and feathers properly:** bury deep away from water and the garden, or compost
  with care (→ 05-11). Left out, they draw predators, rats (→ 04-11) and attention (→ 14-03).
- **Clean and sanitise tools** after: hot soapy water, then a bleach solution if you have it.

### Preserving

- **Pressure canning** (→ 04-05): published procedures exist for poultry and rabbit (National
  Center for Home Food Preservation, Canning Meat, Poultry and Game). The best answer without
  refrigeration.
- **Winter cold** (→ 04-09), where it is reliably below freezing.
- **Broth from the carcass:** bones, feet and backs make nourishing stock on a fire that is
  already lit.
- **Fat:** poultry fat renders easily (→ 04-12), a real advantage of birds over lean rabbit.

### Children

If children helped care for the animals, they may have named them. Handle it deliberately:
- **Be honest and matter-of-fact.** Evasion often frightens children more than the truth.
- **Process out of sight** of small children; involve them in care and cooking, not slaughter.
- **Let them opt in later** as they grow. Whether and when older children take part is on the
  household's worksheet for children and dangerous jobs (→ 30-25): {{choice DC-24}}.
- **Do not force it**, and do not make a spectacle of it (→ 20-03, → 21-03).

## Gear / items

**Have it now (buy toward this):**

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Very sharp knives** and a steel or stone | Humane and safe | Sharp is safer (→ 16-02) |
| Killing cone | Control and humane killing | Can be made from sheet metal |
| Large pot for scalding, and a thermometer | Scalding | Skinning avoids this |
| **Nitrile gloves** by the box | Germ protection | Especially for rabbits |
| Dedicated cutting surface and tubs | One-way clean workflow | Cleanable |
| Cold water or ice bath | Fast chilling is a safety step | → 04-09 in winter |
| **Instant-read thermometer** | 165 °F poultry, 160 °F rabbit | → 07-01 |
| Soap, bleach, brushes | Sanitising | → 13-02 |
| Shovel | Burying remains, away from water | → 13-03 |

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- A traffic cone, a bucket with a hole cut in it, or a cord from a branch all work as restraints.
- **Skinning instead of scalding** saves fuel and equipment.
- Any large tub for chilling; clean snow works in winter.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** the consumables are **gloves, soap and fuel** (for scalding and canning). Knives
  last.
- **Ration:** skin rather than scald to save fuel; process in batches so one setup serves several
  animals.
- **Resupply:** everything here can be improvised except sharp knives and cleanliness.
- **Why it matters:** processing skill turns livestock (→ 05-04, → 05-05) and traps (→ 05-15)
  into food. **Practise now**, on a bird or two, while hot water comes from a tap.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** neighbours, sight lines, smell and disposal all matter (→ 14-03). Work
  discreetly and clean up thoroughly.
- **B (moving):** you are not processing livestock on the road.
- **C (establishing):** set up a dedicated processing area away from the house and water.
