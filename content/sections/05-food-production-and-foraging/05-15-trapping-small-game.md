# Trapping small game and pest animals

**What this page is for.** Why traps beat guns for small game in a long emergency, how to set
them where animals travel, and how to keep children, pets and yourself safe from the traps and
from the diseases wild animals carry.

> **SAFETY.** **Traps injure children and pets:** the trap does not know whose foot it has.
> **Wild rabbits and hares carry tularemia:** gloves, the spotted-liver check, cook to 160 °F
> (CDC; FoodSafety.gov). **Wild meat-eaters (bear, wild boar, fox; treat raccoon and opossum the
> same) can carry trichinosis:** cook every part right through, to at least 160 °F; freezing does
> not reliably make their meat safe (CDC). **Raccoon droppings carry a roundworm whose eggs can
> damage the brain and eyes, mostly in young children** (CDC): handle droppings, carcasses and gut
> piles as hazardous waste.

## Do this now

1. **Learn and practise now**, while a mistake costs nothing. Trap-setting has a real learning
   curve.
2. **Never set traps where children or pets go.** Map every set and show the household.
3. **Wear gloves when handling rabbits and hares, and check the liver** (below).
4. **Understand why it matters:** traps work **while you sleep and use no ammunition**
   (→ 14-08).

> **Before the collapse:** trapping is regulated by your state's wildlife agency: which species
> may be trapped (often only "furbearers" such as raccoon, opossum, muskrat and beaver; often
> **not** cottontail or squirrel), licences, trap types, tags and how often traps must be
> checked. Practise legally now, on the species your state allows. Keep the printed rules with
> your documents (→ 19-01). This never decides a survival procedure.

## Why / how it works

### Why trapping beats hunting for small game

| | Hunting | Trapping |
|---|---|---|
| **Ammunition** | Used per animal | **None** |
| **Your time** | Hours per attempt | Set once, check daily |
| **Works while** | You are there | You do other work, or sleep |
| **Scale** | One shooter, one place | Many sets, many places |
| **Noise** | Announces you (→ 14-03) | **Silent** |

In a long emergency where ammunition is finite, a trapline is the sustainable way to take small
game. A dozen sets checked each morning is a real food system.

### The rules that remain when nobody enforces any

- **Check every set every morning.** A late check means spoiled meat and a suffering animal.
- **Do not strip the ground you live on.** Heavy trapping near a town empties the local rabbit
  and squirrel population in one winter and leaves you with nothing in year two. Move the line
  and leave breeding stock.
- **Children and pets first** (below).
- **Only where you have a right to be.** A trapline on an armed neighbour's land is a security
  problem, not a food system (→ 14-03).

### Target species

Which animals are near you depends on your region; these are widespread.
- **Rabbits and hares:** the main small-game target. Cage traps and snares on their runs.
  **Gloves, the spotted-liver check, 160 °F.**
- **Squirrels:** common, even in town.
- **Raccoon, opossum:** edible **cooked right through, to at least 160 °F** (trichinosis, below). Also pests that
  raid stored food (→ 04-11). **Raccoon droppings and latrine sites are hazardous** (below).
- **Muskrat, beaver** where present: good meat, and **fat**, which matters (→ 04-12).
- **Rats and mice** around food storage: a different job, and always worth doing (→ 04-11).
- **Armadillo** (in the South): can carry the bacterium that causes leprosy (Hansen's disease).
  Gloves and thorough cooking, or leave them.

### Where to set: the actual skill

Trapping is reading animal behaviour, not buying traps.
- **Set on trails, runs and funnels:** fence lines, hedgerows, the edge of cover, under low
  branches, along water. Places animals already travel.
- **Look for sign:** droppings, tracks, chewed plants, worn paths in grass or snow.
- **Snow shows you exactly where animals move.**
- **Baited sets** draw animals in; **blind sets** sit on a trail with no bait.
- **Check every day.**

### Children and pets

- **Never set a trap where children play or pets roam.**
- **Map every set** and show every adult. An unmapped trap is a hazard you made and forgot.
- **Cage (live) traps are the safest** around a household: they hold without injury, and a pet
  can be released unharmed.
- **Body-grip traps** (which close on the body and kill quickly) **and foothold traps are
  indiscriminate**: never near a home with pets or small children.
- **Snares:** use ones with locks and stops that can be released; a snare that cannot be
  released cannot release a cat or dog.
- **Teach children the rule:** do not touch; come and tell an adult (→ 20-02). Whether older
  children help with trapping is part of the household's worksheet on children and dangerous
  jobs (→ 30-25): {{choice DC-24}}.

### Handling and disease

- **Tularemia:** wild rabbits and hares carry it. **The field check:** a rabbit whose **liver
  shows many white spots about the size of a pinhead is thrown out whole**: bury it, wash up, eat
  none of it (Missouri Department of Conservation, Rabbit: Safety and Health). **Nitrile or latex
  gloves when dressing, no rubbing eyes or face, clean the knife**: the germ gets in through skin
  and eyes. Avoid animals that seemed sick or sluggish. **Cook to 160 °F** (FoodSafety.gov). Waiting
  for cold weather to trap rabbits reduces the risk but does not remove it (Missouri Department
  of Conservation; CDC, Tularemia Prevention). Flu-like illness after handling rabbits needs a
  doctor, while there is one: say "possible tularemia". (The same spotted liver in a
  **domestic** rabbit is coccidiosis, → 05-05.)
- **Bear, wild boar, fox and other wild meat-eaters: trichinosis** (CDC, Trichinellosis); treat
  raccoon and opossum the same way. FoodSafety.gov gives no separate figure for these animals:
  cook all of it right through, to at least the **160 °F** it gives for venison and rabbit. Do not
  rely on freezing, smoking, curing or drying: they do not reliably kill the worms in wild meat
  (CDC).
- **Raccoon roundworm (*Baylisascaris*):** eggs in raccoon droppings can damage the brain and
  eyes (blindness, coma); most infections are in young children who put dirt in their mouths
  (CDC).
  **Gloves; no children anywhere near carcasses, gut piles or raccoon latrines** (raccoons use
  the same toilet spots: flat roofs, woodpiles, the base of big trees, decks); **burn or
  deep-bury gut piles; never process near the kitchen; wash hands and tools** (→ 13-02).
- **Rabies:** raccoons, skunks, foxes and bats. **Never handle an animal that acts strangely.**
- **Fleas and ticks** leave a dead animal looking for a new host. Handle accordingly.

### The lean-meat caution again

Wild rabbit is **extremely lean** (→ 05-05). A diet that leans on it without fat causes protein
poisoning. **Pair trapped small game with stored fat** (→ 04-12).

## Gear / items

**Have it now (buy toward this):**

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Cage (live) traps**, several | Safest around children and pets | Also for pest control |
| Body-grip or foothold traps (if you trap furbearers) | Species-specific | Never near the house |
| **Snare cable or wire** with locks, stops and swivels | Light, cheap, effective | Releasable |
| Your state's trapping rules, printed | Pre-collapse practice; species biology outlasts the law | → 19-01 |
| **Nitrile gloves** by the box | Tularemia and general hygiene | Always |
| Stakes, wire, pliers, hatchet | Setting and anchoring | → 16-01 |
| Notebook and **map of sets** | Safety and daily checks | Non-negotiable with children |
| Instant-read thermometer | 160 °F | → 07-01 |

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- **Wire, cord and scrap** make snares and deadfalls: ancient technology that needs almost
  nothing.
- Cage traps can be built from wire mesh.
- **Mouse and rat traps** you already own protect stored food (→ 04-11).

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** traps last; wire and cable are minor consumables. **One of the few food systems in
  this binder with almost no running cost.**
- **Ration:** move the trapline instead of over-trapping one area.
- **Resupply:** wire, cord and knowledge.
- **Why it matters:** as ammunition runs down (→ 14-08), trapping becomes **more** valuable, not
  less.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** in town, neighbours, pets and children limit this heavily. Pest control around
  stored food is always appropriate.
- **B (moving):** a few snares weigh nothing; set them at camp and check at dawn.
- **C (establishing):** a real trapline becomes practical, and may be the most sustainable meat
  source on rural land.
