# Perennials, fruit trees and berries for your zone

**What this page is for.** Food plants that come back every year: what grows in your zone, how
long each takes to bear, and how to keep them alive and multiplying.

> **The most time-sensitive page in Section 05.** Everything here takes **years** to produce. A
> tree planted the week the grid goes down feeds no one for several years. Act on this page
> **now**, while nurseries, water and spare time exist.

## Do this now

1. **Plant something perennial this season.** Rhubarb and raspberries are the fastest
   meaningful returns in most climates, often a partial crop the year after planting.
2. **Check your state Extension service's recommended fruit variety list before you buy.** Your
   zone is **{{ region.home.zone }}**. Many varieties sold nationally are not reliable in very
   cold zones, and many need more winter cold than the warmest zones give. Buying the wrong tree
   costs you years.
3. **Buy fruit trees in pairs.** Most apples, pears and plums need a **second, different,
   compatible variety** nearby to set fruit. A lone apple tree can be a lifetime of leaves.
4. **Site them permanently and correctly:** full sun, out of the frost pocket at the bottom of
   the slope, and where they will not shade your vegetable beds as they grow.
5. **If you have an agreed destination, plant fruit there too, now**, with the owner's
   permission{{#if has.agreed_destination}} (→ 05-18 has each destination's zone){{/if}}. A tree
   planted this year is bearing before you might need it.

## Why / how it works

**Perennials are the opposite trade from the vegetable garden.** They cost years of patience and
permanent space up front. In return they produce **without seed, tilling, transplanting or yearly
inputs**: exactly what matters when supply chains are gone. A vegetable garden fails completely
if you lose a season to illness or a move. A raspberry patch keeps going.

**Hardiness is pass or fail.** Your **USDA hardiness zone** is the average coldest winter
night in your area, in 10 °F bands (USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map). A plant rated two zones
warmer than yours is not stunted by a hard winter; it is dead. In the warmest zones the problem
reverses: many apples, pears, cherries and peaches need a certain amount of winter cold
("chill hours") to flower, and a mild winter leaves them barren. Choose "low-chill" varieties
there.

### What grows by zone band

Rough bands. Your state Extension list is the local authority.

| Band | Reliable choices | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Zones 3 to 4** | Rhubarb, asparagus, raspberries, currants, gooseberries, haskap (honeyberry), aronia, elderberry, very hardy apples, hardy plums, sour cherries, cold-hardy grapes, hybrid hazelnuts | Prefer varieties from northern university breeding programmes; that is what those programmes are for |
| **Zones 5 to 6** | All of the above plus most apples, pears, plums, many grapes, blueberries (in acid soil), strawberries; peaches in sheltered spots | The widest choice for temperate fruit |
| **Zones 7 to 8** | Peaches, pears, apples (fewer in hot humid areas), figs (with winter protection at the cold end), persimmons, blackberries, blueberries, muscadine grapes in the Southeast | Disease pressure from humidity; choose resistant varieties |
| **Zones 9 and warmer** | Citrus, figs, pomegranates, low-chill peaches and apples; avocado and mango in the warmest areas | Winter cold is short or absent; water and heat are the limits |

Your zone, **{{ region.home.zone }}**, puts you in one row. Any perennial you plant should be
rated for your zone or colder.

### Pollination is a design rule

Most apples, pears and plums are **self-unfruitful**: they need pollen from a **different
variety** that flowers at the same time (University of Minnesota Extension, Growing Apples).
Haskaps need a matched partner. Sour cherries, most peaches, and most raspberries, currants and
strawberries are self-fertile. Before you plant, write down which tree pollinates which. In a
small yard, a **multi-graft tree** (several varieties on one trunk) solves pollination in one
planting hole.

**Rootstock decides size and lifespan.** Fruit trees are grafted (joined) onto a root system
chosen for size. Dwarf trees fruit sooner and stay pickable without a ladder, but need
**permanent staking** and more watering. Standard trees take longer, grow big, live longer and
stand on their own. In town, dwarf or semi-dwarf; on open land, standard trees are the legacy
planting.

### Food trees you did not plant

Towns and farm country are full of **abandoned apple trees, wild plum thickets, mulberries,
chokecherries, serviceberries and nut trees**, planted or naturalised decades ago. Map them now
(→ 05-17), the way you would map water.

### The two ways young trees die

- **Rabbits and voles girdle young trunks** (chew the bark all the way round), often under
  snow, and kill the tree. Every young tree gets a **hardware-cloth or spiral trunk guard**,
  from the ground to above the expected snow line, from day one.
- **Deer** rub antlers on young trunks in fall and browse low branches. Cage young trees where
  deer roam.

## Gear / items

**Have it now (buy toward this):**

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Bare-root fruit trees, **in compatible pairs** | Cheapest way to buy trees | Check the Extension list first |
| Raspberry canes, rhubarb and asparagus crowns | Fastest perennial food per dollar | Rhubarb and asparagus can outlive you |
| Hardware cloth and trunk guards | Rabbits and voles kill more young trees than cold does | Check every fall |
| Bypass pruners, loppers, pruning saw | Perennials need yearly pruning | Keep sharp (→ 16-02) |
| Tree stakes and soft ties | Dwarf trees need permanent support | Not bare wire against bark |
| Deep mulch (wood chips, straw) | Moisture, weeds, root insulation | Keep mulch off the trunk |
| Grafting knife and grafting tape | Propagate more trees | The skill that makes fruit renewable (→ 24-01) |
| Wire or panel trellis | Grapes and cane berries | |
| Netting | Birds take the whole berry crop otherwise | |

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- **Free plants:** raspberries, blackberries and elderberry send up suckers; a neighbour's patch
  is a free start. Rhubarb divides. Strawberries send runners. Currants and grapes root from
  **hardwood cuttings** taken while dormant.
- **Wild rootstock:** wild plum, chokecherry and wild apple can be grafted onto.
- **Trunk guards:** split plastic pipe, wrapped window screen, metal flashing.
- **Mulch:** shredded leaves, wood chips from storm debris.

## When it runs out

Perennials do not run out; they **decline**, slowly enough to miss.
- **Water:** a young tree needs deep watering through its first two or three summers. Plant in
  **fall or early spring** so roots establish before heat, and site trees where stored or used
  water can reach them.
- **Renewal:** raspberries need old canes cut out every year; strawberry beds need renovating
  every few years; fruit trees need yearly pruning. **The skill is the consumable**: learn
  pruning now.
- **Propagation is the resupply.** With no nurseries, new plants come from **cuttings, suckers,
  divisions, runners and grafting**: all free, from what you already have.
- **Preserving is the other half.** Fruit arrives all at once and will not wait: dry it
  (→ 04-06), can it (→ 04-04) or make fruit leather. Plan preserving capacity **before** the
  harvest.
- **Nut trees and allergies:** tree nuts are a major food allergen. If anyone in the household
  is allergic to tree nuts (→ 02-04), nut trees may not be worth planting at all; if they are
  planted, harvest, processing and storage happen away from that person's food, with dedicated
  tools.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** plant now along fences and in corners where they do not shade vegetable beds.
  Berries, rhubarb and asparagus give the best return on a small lot.
- **B (moving):** you cannot take a tree. You **can** take dormant cuttings, seed, and rhubarb or
  asparagus crowns wrapped damp.
- **C (establishing):** perennials pay off most here. Old farmsteads often hide apple, plum,
  mulberry and asparagus gone wild along fence lines. Inventory before you plant.
