# Pollination and garden pests without chemicals

**What this page is for.** Which crops need insects to make food, how to pollinate them by hand,
and how to control pests when there is nothing to buy.

> **CAUTION.** **Stinging insects:** encouraging bees into the yard raises sting exposure. Anyone
> can have a first severe reaction to a sting, and epinephrine is the answer (→ 02-03).
> {{#if has.epinephrine}}Make sure every adult knows where the household's epinephrine is before
> you plant a flower strip.{{else}}Know the signs of a severe reaction before you plant a flower
> strip.{{/if}} **Every "natural" pesticide is still a pesticide.** Homemade nicotine tea, sprays
> of unknown strength and borrowed farm chemicals can poison people, pets and soil. Do not
> improvise chemistry near where children play.

## Do this now

1. **Learn which of your crops actually need an insect.** Many do not. If
   pollinators crash, you want to know at once which beds are at risk (table below).
2. **Learn to hand-pollinate squash, cucumbers and melons.** Five minutes a morning, and the vine
   crops no longer depend on insects.
3. **Stop spraying anything broad-spectrum** (anything that kills all insects). In a long outage,
   beneficial insects are the only pest control left, and they come back more slowly than pests.
4. **Walk the garden every morning with a jar of soapy water.** Hand-picking is the most
   effective no-input pest control there is.

## Why / how it works

### Which crops need pollinators

| Needs insects | Pollinates itself (shake or wind is enough) | Wind-pollinated | Eaten before it flowers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squash, zucchini, pumpkin, cucumber, melon (separate male and female flowers) | Tomato, pepper, eggplant, bean, pea | Sweet corn | Lettuce, spinach, chard, kale, radish, carrot, beet, onion, potato, herbs |
| Apple, plum, cherry, pear and most tree fruit (usually needs a **second compatible variety**) | Strawberry (better with insects) | | |
| Raspberry, blueberry (better and bigger with insects) | | | |

Read that twice. **Most of a survival garden's calories (potatoes, roots, greens, beans, corn,
tomatoes) do not depend on bees.** The vine crops and fruit trees do. That tells you where to
spend effort (University of Minnesota Extension, Fruits and Vegetables That Require
Pollination).

**Squash-family crops ("cucurbits") are the special case.** They carry **separate male and
female flowers**, and an insect has to carry pollen from one to the other. Female flowers have a
tiny fruit already formed behind the bloom; males sit on a plain stem. Poor pollination gives
shrivelled, curled or half-formed fruit that rots at the flower end. Bumblebees and native squash
bees are the best pollinators of squash; they work early in the morning, when the flowers are
open.

{{figure fig-squash-male-female-flowers}}

**Hand-pollinating squash, cucumbers and melons (at dawn; the flowers close by midday):**
1. Find an open **male** flower. Pick it and peel the petals back to show the pollen-covered
   centre.
2. Find an open **female** flower (tiny fruit behind the bloom).
3. Dab the male centre onto the centre of the female flower. One male does several females.
4. A soft brush or a cotton swab does the same job without picking the male.

**Tomatoes and peppers** under cover or indoors (no wind): flick or gently shake the flower
clusters daily. They pollinate themselves; they just need the vibration.

### Keeping the pollinators you need

Bees need **flowers from spring through fall**, not one burst. The near-free version: let some
herbs, cabbage-family crops and greens **bolt** (go to flower) instead of pulling them. Dill,
cilantro, basil, mustard, radish and arugula flowers draw pollinators. Add a strip of native
wildflowers for your region along the fence. **Leave a patch of bare, untilled ground:** many
native bees, including squash bees, nest in soil and will not use a bee house (Iowa State
University Extension).

### Pest control with nothing in the shed

Work down this ladder in order. Each step is cheaper and less harmful than the next.

1. **Prevention.** Rotate crops so last year's pest does not wake up under this year's host.
   Clear crop debris in fall (pests spend the winter in it). Space plants for airflow. Water the
   soil, not the leaves: wet leaves breed fungus.
2. **Barriers.** **Floating row cover is the most valuable pest tool in a no-chemical garden**:
   it keeps out flea beetles, cabbage worms, squash vine borer and cucumber beetles. **Row cover
   keeps pollinators out too**, so take it off squash-family crops when they flower, or
   hand-pollinate under it. Collars of cardboard or tin cans around transplants stop cutworms.
   Fine mesh over cabbage-family crops all season nearly ends cabbage worms.
3. **Hand-picking.** Every morning: Colorado potato beetles and their orange egg clusters (check
   under leaves), tomato hornworms, squash bugs and their bronze egg patches, cabbage worms,
   Japanese beetles. Drop them in soapy water. **A board laid flat on the soil overnight**
   collects squash bugs underneath; flip it in the morning.
4. **Encourage predators.** Lady beetles and their alligator-shaped larvae, lacewings, ground
   beetles, tiny parasitic wasps, hoverflies. They come for the same small flowers the
   pollinators want: dill, fennel, cilantro, yarrow. **Learn to recognise the larvae** so you do
   not crush your allies. A hornworm covered in white rice-like cocoons is being eaten by
   parasitic wasps: leave it.
5. **Physical.** A strong water spray for aphids. Beer traps or hand-picking at night for slugs.
   Diatomaceous earth on dry soil only (it stops working when wet and kills beneficial insects
   too): spot use, never on flowers.
6. **Last resort: soap.** Insecticidal soap, or a **very weak** plain-soap spray, kills
   soft-bodied insects on contact only. It also kills beneficial insects it lands on. Spray in
   the evening, on the pest, never the whole garden. Do not make stronger chemistry.

**Companion planting:** the folk claims ("marigolds repel everything", "basil protects
tomatoes") are mostly unsupported by trials. What **is** supported is the underlying idea:
**variety and continuous flowers support predators and pollinators** (University of Minnesota
Extension, Companion Planting). Interplant flowers freely; do not plan food security around a
marigold.

### Common garden pests across most of the US

| Pest | Attacks | What works |
|---|---|---|
| Colorado potato beetle | Potato, eggplant, tomato | Hand-pick adults and crush egg clusters daily; rotate far |
| Squash vine borer | Squash, zucchini, pumpkin | Row cover until flowering; bury vine joints so they root; slit the stem and remove the grub |
| Squash bug | Squash family | Board trap; crush bronze eggs under leaves |
| Cucumber beetle | Cucumber, melon, squash | Row cover early; they spread bacterial wilt, so this one matters |
| Cabbage worm / looper | Cabbage, kale, broccoli | Fine mesh all season |
| Flea beetle | Cabbage family, eggplant | Row cover on transplants until established |
| Aphid | Everything | Water spray; let the lady beetles work |
| Japanese beetle | Beans, raspberry, fruit | Hand-pick into soapy water at dawn; **do not use pheromone traps**: they draw in more than they catch |
| Grasshopper (dry years) | Everything | Physical barriers; poultry (→ 05-04); often you accept the loss |
| Deer, rabbit, raccoon | Everything | Fencing |

Your region will have its own extras; your state Extension service's pest guide lists them
(University of Minnesota Extension, Yard and Garden Insects).

## Gear / items

**Have it now (buy toward this):**

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Floating row cover (100-ft roll or more) and hoops | Best no-chemical pest tool; also frost cover | Light weight for summer, heavier for → 05-02 |
| Fine insect mesh | Season-long cabbage worm control | Lasts years if stored dry |
| Insecticidal soap concentrate | Last-resort soft-bodied pests | Concentrate stores longer |
| Soft brush or cotton swabs | Hand-pollination | |
| 10× hand lens | Telling a pest from a beneficial larva | |
| A printed pest guide for your region | You will not remember 20 insects | |
| Native wildflower seed for your region | Continuous bloom, resident pollinators | Perennial; plant once |
| Diatomaceous earth (food grade) | Spot use for slugs and crawling pests | Useless wet |

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- **Row cover:** old sheer curtains, tulle, window screen, cheesecloth, worn sheets on hoops
  (heavier: watch the heat).
- **Hoops:** plastic pipe, heavy wire, willow branches bent green, metal conduit.
- **Cutworm collars:** toilet-paper tubes, cut cans, cardboard strips, cups with the bottoms cut
  out.
- **Traps:** a flat board for squash bugs; a shallow dish of beer or yeast and sugar water for
  slugs.
- **Pollination brush:** a feather, a paintbrush, or the male flower itself.

## When it runs out

- **Insecticidal soap:** use a **very weak** plain-soap solution (pure soap, not detergent and
  not dish soap with degreasers), sprayed on the pest in the evening, rinsed off food before
  eating. Test on a few leaves first: soap burns leaves in heat. **Do not** move on to homemade
  nicotine, bleach or borrowed farm chemicals: the poisoning risk to children and pets is real
  and the benefit small.
- **Row cover** is the consumable that matters. It tears and breaks down in sun. **Keep spares
  and patch with tape.** When it is gone: hand-picking discipline, and plan as if you will lose
  a share of cabbage-family and squash-family crops.
- **Pollinators** can crash after a hard chemical year, a brutal winter or habitat loss. Your
  hedge is **hand-pollination** plus favouring crops that do not need insects.
- **Bought predators** (lady beetles, nematodes) end when shops do. Habitat is the permanent
  replacement: plant it now; it takes two or three seasons to establish.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** fence out the animals; row cover and morning hand-picking for the insects. Let
  herbs flower along the fence.
- **B (moving):** carry seed, not plants. Wildflower seed weighs nothing.
- **C (establishing):** bigger plantings can absorb losses, and native habitat nearby usually
  means plenty of pollinators. Pest pressure shifts toward grasshoppers, deer and voles: fencing
  and poultry matter more than sprays.
