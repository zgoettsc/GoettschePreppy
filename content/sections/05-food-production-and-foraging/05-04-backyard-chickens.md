# Backyard chickens

**What this page is for.** Whether a small flock of hens suits your household, and how to keep
them through a hard winter, a feed shortage and the diseases that matter.

> **CAUTION.** Three things go wrong with backyard hens. **Bird flu (avian influenza)** kills
> flocks and has infected people who handled sick birds (CDC). **Salmonella** lives on healthy
> birds, their eggs and their coop, and it makes small children seriously ill (CDC, Backyard
> Poultry). And **heat lamps burn coops down**. Each has a rule on this page.
{{#if has.condition.CL-04}}> **This household has a food allergy on record.** If egg is one of the allergens, keeping hens
> means producing that allergen daily at home. Read the allergy section before buying birds.
{{/if}}

## Do this now

1. **If anyone in the household is allergic to egg, decide the allergy plan first**, with the
   allergist while one is available (→ 02-04). Below.
2. **Choose cold-hardy breeds** if your winters are cold
   ({{#if is.cold_winters}}yours are: January lows average {{ region.home.jan_low_f }} °F{{else}}January lows at home average {{ region.home.jan_low_f }} °F{{/if}}).
3. **Build the coop with hardware cloth**, not chicken wire, and a solid latching door.
4. **Plan the feed.** Chickens eat grain. Store it like human food (→ 04-03).
5. **Learn the bird-flu signs now** (below). A bird that died on its own is never food.
6. **Consider rabbits as well or instead** (→ 05-05): comparable protein, better manure, quiet,
   no egg allergen.

> **Before the collapse:** many towns and cities limit backyard poultry: how many birds, no
> roosters, a permit for the coop, a distance from neighbours' houses, permits for ducks,
> turkeys or geese. Check your city or county ordinance before buying birds. This never decides
> how you keep animals to survive.

## Why / how it works

### If someone in the house is allergic to egg

Egg is one of the major food allergens in the US (FDA, Food Allergies). A flock means handling
that allergen every day, on hands, clothes, surfaces and in the kitchen. Many families manage
it, but it is a decision to make deliberately (→ 02-04, → 02-03).

| Consideration | Detail |
|---|---|
| **What you gain** | Renewable protein and fat from scraps and yard forage; manure for the garden (→ 05-11) |
| **What you risk** | Daily handling of the allergen; egg residue on hands, clothes, surfaces and in the kitchen |
| **What helps** | Egg handling entirely outside; dedicated coop clothing and boots; washing before contact with the allergic person; dedicated cookware; eggs stored sealed and out of reach (→ 04-15); no egg cooking while the allergic person is in the kitchen |
| **A middle path** | Keep hens and **trade the eggs** (→ 18-02) instead of eating them. The allergen stays on the property, but the kitchen exposure shrinks a lot |
| **The alternative** | **Rabbits** (→ 05-05): similar protein, no allergen |

### Breeds and space

Where winters are cold, choose cold-hardy, dual-purpose breeds (eggs and meat): **Rhode Island
Red, Plymouth Rock, Wyandotte, Orpington, Australorp, Brahma, Chantecler.** **Small combs are
better:** large single combs get frostbite. Rose-comb and pea-comb breeds handle cold better.
Where summers are hot, lighter breeds and shade matter more than combs.

**Space:** about **4 square feet per bird inside the coop and 10 square feet per bird in the
run**. Six hens need about 24 square feet inside plus a 60-square-foot run. Crowding turns a
winter coop into pecking and disease.

### Winter management

- **Dry and draft-free, but ventilated.** Moisture, not cold, causes frostbitten combs and
  breathing problems. This is the most common beginner mistake.
- **No heat lamps. Ever.** Cold-hardy hens do not need heat; a dry coop and deep litter is the
  method. Heat-lamp fires are a leading cause of lost coops, and they spread to garages and
  houses, especially where fuel is stored nearby (→ 06-06). A lamp over dusty bedding is how
  you lose the birds and the house.
- **Deep litter:** keep adding bedding (straw, wood shavings) on top. The lower layers compost
  in place, give off a little warmth, and become garden material in spring (→ 05-11).
- **Water freezes.** Without power you haul and thaw water at least twice a day, every day, all
  winter (→ 03-06). That is the real daily labour.
- **Laying drops sharply** as days shorten. Without added light, expect few or no eggs in the
  darkest months.
- **Predators:** raccoons, foxes, hawks, dogs and, in a crisis, people. Hardware cloth (welded
  wire mesh with small openings), buried at the edges, and a solid door.

### Feed: the honest constraint

**Chickens eat grain.** In a long emergency:
- Kitchen scraps and garden waste help but will not sustain a flock.
- **Free-ranging** gives real forage in summer and almost nothing in a cold winter.
- **Sprouted grain** (→ 04-16) stretches feed a lot.
- **You turn stored grain into eggs at a loss in calories.** The gain is protein quality and
  fat, not energy.

Plan feed storage the way you plan human food, in rodent-proof bins (→ 04-11), and count it in
the runway (→ 23-02).

### Health and hygiene

- **Salmonella:** wash hands after touching birds, eggs, feeders or the coop. CDC advises that
  children under 5 not handle or touch backyard poultry (CDC, Backyard Poultry). Do not kiss or
  snuggle birds, and keep coop shoes outside the house.{{#if has.young_child}} This household
  has a child under 6: keep them out of the coop and away from bird handling.{{/if}}
- **Bird flu (avian influenza, HPAI H5N1)** has been found in backyard flocks across the
  country, and people have been infected through contact with sick poultry (USDA APHIS, Defend
  the Flock; CDC, Bird Flu in Backyard Flocks). **Signs: sudden death of several birds, swollen
  heads, purple combs or wattles, a sharp drop in laying.** If you see them, assume bird flu:
  **do not eat them, do not compost them**, wear gloves and a mask to handle them, bury them
  deep. While phones work, report it to your state veterinarian or animal health agency.
  **Prevention:** keep feed and water where wild birds, especially ducks and geese, cannot reach
  them, and keep wild birds out of the coop.
- **A bird that died on its own is never food** (→ 05-16).
- **Do not wash eggs until you use them** (→ 04-15). The natural coating ("bloom") is what
  keeps them.
- **Cook poultry to 165 °F and egg dishes to 160 °F** (FoodSafety.gov).
- **Put the coop away from the house**, downwind, and well away from stored water (→ 03-08).

**Have it now (buy toward this):**

| Ordinance checked, permit if needed | Pre-collapse | Your city or county |

| Rodent-proof feed bins | Feed is the real constraint | → 04-11 |
| Waterers and a freeze plan | Daily winter labour | → 03-06 |

| Gloves and masks | Handling sick or dead birds | Keep a box by the coop |
| Instant-read thermometer | 165 °F poultry, 160 °F egg dishes | → 07-01 |

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- **Rabbits** (→ 05-05) instead, if the fit is poor.
- Coops build easily from salvaged lumber and pallets.
- Neighbours with hens may trade eggs for produce or labour (→ 18-02).

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** **feed** is the limit, not the birds. Count it like human food (→ 23-02).
- **Ration:** sprouted grain (→ 04-16) stretches feed; free-ranging helps in summer only.
- **Resupply:** without bought feed, the flock must shrink to what scraps, garden waste and
  forage can support. Realistically that is a very small number of birds.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** ordinance, neighbours, noise and any egg allergy all argue for a deliberate
  decision.
- **B (moving):** **poultry travels badly**: heat stress, water and crates on the road. Caged
  rabbits travel better (→ 05-05). Treat a flock as a home or destination commitment.
- **C (establishing):** easier: space, forage, fewer neighbours. The allergy question and the
  winter water hauling come with you.
