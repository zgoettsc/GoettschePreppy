# Wild edibles by season

**What this page is for.** **When** and **where** to look for wild food through the year, so you
can plan and preserve. It is a calendar, not a field guide: it cannot tell you **what** you found.
Read → 05-06 before you pick anything.

> **SAFETY.** **No wild plant or mushroom is eaten on a "test"; identification or nothing.**
> The deadly ones are not detectable by taste tests and the dose is small (→ 30-01). **Water
> hemlock, the most violently toxic plant in North America (USDA ARS), where a 2 to 3 cm piece of
> root can kill an adult (CDC MMWR, 1994), grows in wet ditches and low meadows in every region,
> and comes up in spring looking like a carrot-family green.** **Mushrooms are
> deliberately left off this page**: deadly look-alikes exist and printed text is not an adequate
> teacher.

## Do this now

1. **Map before you forage.** Walk your neighbourhood, trails, parks and field edges now, with
   fuel and time and no urgency, and mark on a paper map (→ 25-04) where the **mulberries, wild
   plums, serviceberries, crabapples, abandoned fruit trees and nut trees** are. Those are the
   calories.
2. **Buy one good field guide with photographs for your own region.** A national guide is a poor
   substitute.
3. **Learn three plants completely, not thirty badly.** Good first three in most of the US:
   **dandelion, mulberry, and one wild fruit common near you** (wild plum, blackberry or
   serviceberry).
4. **Learn water hemlock and poison hemlock cold** (→ 05-06). The plants that hurt you matter
   more than the ones that feed you.
5. **Look at your region's plates** (below) and at Section 32 for anywhere you might go.

## Why / how it works

**Set your expectations.** Wild plants are a **supplement**, not a food supply. Wild greens are
mostly water and vitamins; berries are seasonal and brief; the real wild calories are **animal**
(→ 05-07, → 05-08, → 05-15) and **starchy roots and nuts**, which take real work. Foraging's honest
role:
1. **Vitamins and variety** against a stored-food diet (→ 04-18).
2. **Seasonal bulk you can preserve:** fruit dried or made into leather (→ 04-06).
3. **Knowledge that scales** when you have more ground.

**Your green window at home** runs roughly from the last spring frost
(**{{ region.home.last_frost }}**) to the first fall frost (**{{ region.home.first_frost }}**):
about **{{ region.home.growing_days }} days**. The productive foraging season is shorter still: a
spring greens flush, an early-summer berry run, a late-summer fruit and nut glut. In cold regions,
**much of the year offers almost nothing wild and green**; that gap is closed by storing, not by
foraging. In mild-winter regions the greens season runs through the cool months and summer heat
is the lean time. **Plan the wild harvest around preserving it.**

### The calendar, by season

Dates shift with latitude, elevation and the year. Use your frost dates as the anchor and your
own notes as the truth. Not every plant grows in every region: check each against your region's
plates and your field guide.

**Early spring (the weeks around the last frost): the greens flush**
Wild greens are tender and worth eating for a short window; most turn bitter and tough once the
plant flowers.
- **Dandelion:** young leaves before flowering, roots, flower buds. Lawns everywhere. The easiest
  plant on this page: one yellow flower on each hollow, leafless stalk. Groundsel, the look-alike,
  has leafy, branching stems with many small flower heads (→ 05-06).
- **Stinging nettle:** cooked only (cooking removes the sting). Moist edges, ditches, old
  farmsteads. Gloves.
- **Lamb's quarters, purslane, plantain, violet, chickweed:** common yard and garden weeds into
  summer. **Lamb's quarters and amaranth build up nitrate and oxalate:** boil and throw out the
  water, eat them as part of a varied diet, skip plants from heavily manured or drought-stressed
  ground, and use the household's rule for young children (→ 05-06). **Purslane** has clear
  juice; spotted spurge, which grows in the same cracks, has milky sap and is not food.
- **Wild onion and wild garlic:** **every bulb must smell of onion, checked with clean hands**;
  death camas, fly poison, star-of-Bethlehem and daffodil have onion-like bulbs, no onion smell,
  and are poisonous (→ 05-06). Ramps grow only in parts of the East; a ramp look-alike in a shady yard is more
  likely lily-of-the-valley.
- **This is peak hemlock season.** Young carrot-family plants (a lacy leaf, a hollow stem, a wet
  spot) are where people die. If you cannot identify it beyond doubt, walk away.

**Late spring to early summer: early fruit, the last greens**
- **Mulberry:** planted and wild trees in towns across most of the country; heavy drops; nothing
  dangerous looks like a mulberry tree with mulberries on it. Dries well.
- **Serviceberry (Juneberry):** wild stands and ornamental plantings.
- **Wild strawberry; black and red raspberry; blackberry and dewberry** (thorny canes on edges
  and old fields).
- **Elderflower:** the bloom tells you where to come back for berries.
- Most greens are now bitter or gone to seed.

**Late summer to early fall: the glut, and the reason to have jars ready**
Almost everything worth preserving comes at once.
- **Wild plum:** thickets on fence lines and field edges. Excellent dried, as sauce or leather.
- **Chokecherry:** astringent raw; good cooked into syrup or jelly. **The pits contain
  cyanide-forming compounds: never crush or grind them.** Chokecherries hang in long strings;
  buckthorn berries, the look-alike, cluster tight against the twig.
- **Elderberry:** **cook them**; raw berries and all green parts cause vomiting. Pokeweed, whose
  berries hang in long strings on soft magenta stems, is poisonous (→ 05-06).
- **Crabapple, wild and abandoned apple and pear:** cook or dry.
- **Wild grape and muscadine:** **moonseed is the poisonous look-alike.** Grapes have tendrils
  and several small seeds; moonseed has no tendrils and one crescent-shaped seed: cut a fruit and
  look before eating any. Virginia creeper berries (five leaflets per leaf) are not grapes.
- **Pawpaw** (eastern woods): eat only ripe flesh; skin and seeds cause stomach upset; try a
  small amount first.
- **Sunflower, amaranth and lamb's-quarters seed:** real calories, tedious to process. **Rinse
  amaranth and lamb's-quarters seed well before using it as grain** (it carries soapy, bitter
  compounds called saponins).
- **Nuts: black walnut, hickory, hazelnut, acorns, pecan in the South.** **The wild calorie crop
  in much of the country.** Acorns need **leaching** (soaking out the bitter tannins): **cold-water
  leaching (repeated changes of water over several days) keeps the starch usable for flour;
  hot-water leaching is faster but cooks the starch, so the meal will not bind.** White-oak-group
  acorns (rounded leaf lobes) need far less leaching than red oaks (pointed lobes): start there.
  Black walnut hulls stain and irritate skin (wear gloves), and **water the hulls soaked in kills
  fish and garden plants**: dump it nowhere near a pond, rain barrel or bed. **Tree nuts are a
  major food allergen** (below).

**Fall into early winter (after the first frost): roots and the last fruit**
- **Burdock, Jerusalem artichoke, cattail roots:** starchy calories, dug.
  **Not wild parsnip, wild carrot or any carrot-family root. Ever.** A first-year parsnip or carrot
  rosette beside a young hemlock is exactly the fatal mistake, and wild parsnip sap burns skin.
  **Cattail grows in the same wet ground as water hemlock**, and its shoots resemble blue flag
  iris: harvest only from a stand identified by its brown spikes the previous summer (→ 05-06).
- **Rose hips:** best after the first frost; high in vitamin C; dry them for tea.
- **Persimmon** (South and East): wait until fully soft and wrinkled, usually after frost; unripe
  fruit is fiercely astringent.
- **Highbush cranberry, hackberry:** frost sweetens some fruit.
- **Nuts continue.** Squirrels are competition: be early.

**Winter: little or nothing in cold regions**
- **Rose hips still on the cane; hackberries on the tree; dried staghorn sumac heads** (red,
  upright, fuzzy cones; soaked for a sour vitamin-C drink). **Poison sumac has drooping white
  berries and grows in swamps.**
- **Inner bark** of pine and some other trees is last-resort survival food, not food.
- **In cold regions, winter is hunting, trapping, ice fishing and stored food** (→ 05-07,
  → 05-08, → 05-15). **Winter is the reason you preserve in late summer.**
- In mild-winter regions, cool-season greens (chickweed, dandelion, wild mustards) return through
  the winter.

### The rules that keep you alive

1. **Identification or nothing.** Not "probably", not "it looks like the picture." Several
   matching features, the right habitat, the right season. No taste or edibility test
   (→ 30-01).
2. **Never eat from a carrot-family plant.**
3. **Never forage where you do not know the spray history.** Roadsides, rail lines, park edges,
   power-line corridors and lawns are often sprayed with weedkiller. Ditches collect road salt
   and runoff.
4. **One new food at a time, a small amount, one person, then wait and watch before eating
   more**, even when the plant is correctly identified: some people react to correctly identified
   wild plants.
5. **Cook what must be cooked:** nettle, elderberry, acorns. (Milkweed is deliberately left off:
   its look-alike, dogbane, is a heart poison, and printed features are not enough to tell them
   apart.)
6. **Food allergies:** wild food is unlabeled and any wild nut is a tree nut. Whether a
   food-allergic person eats it at all is the household's decision card (→ 30-19):
   {{choice DC-18}}. → 02-04.
7. **Children eat only what an adult hands them.** A toddler will eat berries off a bush because
   they saw you do it.
8. **Pets too:** several wild plants are toxic to cats and dogs, and pets chew greens (→ 20-09).

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- **Containers:** any bucket, pillowcase, cut jug, or a jacket tied off at the sleeves.
- **Drying:** window screens on blocks, a car dashboard in late summer, a line in a hot attic.
- **Acorn processing:** a bucket and many changes of water; grind with two stones, a hand mill or
  a meat grinder.
- **What you cannot substitute:** the field guide and the identification skill.

## When it runs out

Wild food does not run out so much as **arrive and vanish**. The failure is being unready during
the short window when a plant is ripe.
- **Runway:** almost none without preserving. A plum thicket is ripe for days, not weeks. **Preserving
  capacity is the limit, not the plants.**
- **Competition:** in a long emergency every wild food near town is stripped fast, by people and
  by deer, birds and raccoons. **Town fruit trees will be picked clean in the first season.**
- **Substitute:** **planted perennials** (→ 05-13): the same fruit, on ground you control.
  Foraging carries the first season; planting carries the years after.
- **The skill does not deplete.** Everything you learn to identify this year, you know for good.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** towns are full of planted mulberry, crabapple, serviceberry and abandoned
  fruit trees. Expect competition in a real event.
- **B (moving):** foraging on the move is a snack, not a meal plan. Do not detour for wild food,
  and do not eat anything unverified while tired and hungry: that is when people make fatal
  mistakes.
- **C (establishing):** more ground and less competition. **Walk the property in each season of
  the first year** and write down what grows where; the destination's plates are in Section 32
  and its hazards on → 05-19.
