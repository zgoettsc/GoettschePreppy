# A small high-yield garden for your climate

**What this page is for.** How to get the most food from a small garden where you live: when
to plant, what to grow first, and how to squeeze a yard. Your frost dates and zone are printed
below from your home region's data. The methods work anywhere in the US.

> **CAUTION.** Be honest about what a small backyard garden does: **it will not feed a family.**
> It supplies vitamins, variety, morale and **food whose every ingredient you know**, which
> matters if anyone has a food allergy (→ 02-04). Calories still come from stored staples
> (→ 04-03) and protein from animals, fish and game. Plan the garden for **nutrition and
> traceability**, not survival calories, and you will be right about it.

## Do this now

1. **Learn your frost window.** At home the median **last spring frost is
   {{ region.home.last_frost }}** and the median **first fall frost is
   {{ region.home.first_frost }}**: about **{{ region.home.growing_days }} frost-free days**.
   "Median" means half of all years are worse. Plan tender crops to be ripe well before the
   first frost date, not on it. Write your own observed dates here each year:
   last spring frost ________ · first fall frost ________.
2. **Start warm-season seeds indoors** 4 to 12 weeks before they go outside (→ 05-14).
3. **Plant cool-season crops 2 to 4 weeks before the last frost.** They sprout in soil as cool
   as 40 °F (SDSU Extension, Vegetable Gardening in South Dakota).
4. **Build the soil now** (→ 05-11). Soil takes years; seeds take days.
5. **Buy and store seed now** (→ 05-03), choosing varieties that ripen inside your season.

## Why / how it works

### Your season, in one table

Words first. A **frost** is a night cold enough (32 °F or below) to kill tender plants.
**Cool-season crops** (peas, lettuce, spinach, kale, cabbage, radish, carrots, onions,
potatoes) tolerate light frost and grow best in cool weather. **Warm-season crops** (tomatoes,
peppers, beans, squash, cucumbers, corn, melons) are killed by frost and want warm soil and
air above about 60 °F (SDSU Extension).

| Milestone | When, at home |
|---|---|
| Start warm-season seeds indoors | 4 to 12 weeks before {{ region.home.last_frost }} (tomatoes and peppers earliest) |
| Sow cool-season crops outside | 2 to 4 weeks before {{ region.home.last_frost }} |
| **Last spring frost (median)** | **{{ region.home.last_frost }}** (a cold year runs later) |
| Transplant warm-season crops | After the last frost, once nights stay warm |
| **First fall frost (median)** | **{{ region.home.first_frost }}** (a cold year comes earlier) |
| Frost-free days | **{{ region.home.growing_days }}** |
| Hardiness zone (for perennials, → 05-13) | **{{ region.home.zone }}** |
| Season extension | Weeks before and after the frost dates, under cover (→ 05-02) |

**Will a crop fit?** Every seed packet prints "days to maturity". Count that many days forward
from the date you plant (or transplant) it. If the answer lands comfortably before
{{ region.home.first_frost }}, it fits. If it lands close, pick a faster variety, start it
indoors, or cover it at the end (→ 05-02). A fenced town yard is often slightly warmer than
open country, and a low spot is often colder; your own written dates beat the median.

**Hardiness zone** (from the USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map) is the average coldest winter
night, in bands of 10 °F. It decides which **perennials** (plants that live for years) survive
your winter. It does not decide when to plant vegetables; frost dates do.

### Crop guidance by zone band

Find the row that holds your zone ({{ region.home.zone }}) and your frost-free days
({{ region.home.growing_days }}). If the two point to different rows, trust the frost-free
days for vegetables. These are rough bands; your state's Extension planting chart is the
local authority.

| Band | What the season is like | What that means in the garden |
|---|---|---|
| **Zones 3 to 4, short season** | Late spring, early fall, one warm stretch | One main planting. Start tomatoes, peppers and squash indoors. Choose short-season varieties (the shortest "days to maturity" on the rack). Potatoes, cabbage, carrots, peas and greens are the reliable crops. Season extension (→ 05-02) adds real weeks at both ends. |
| **Zones 5 to 6, middle season** | One full warm season, cool spring and fall shoulders | A spring cool-season planting, a summer warm-season planting, and a fall cool-season planting sown in late summer. Most of the crop list works. |
| **Zones 7 to 8, long season** | Early spring, late fall, hot summers | **Two full plantings**: a spring garden and a fall garden. Potatoes twice. Heat-tolerant crops such as sweet potatoes, okra and southern peas (cowpeas) thrive. Fungal disease and summer heat, not cold, are the main limits (University of Arkansas Extension, Year-Round Home Garden Planting Chart). |
| **Zones 9 and warmer, mild winter** | Frost rare or absent; summer is the hard season | Cool-season crops grow **through the winter**. Midsummer heat stops many crops from setting fruit, so plant warm-season crops in late winter and again in late summer. Shade cloth and water become the limits. |

destination in → 05-18.

### What to grow in a small space, ranked by usefulness

| Priority | Crops | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Calorie-dense** | Potatoes, winter squash, dry beans, corn (if space); sweet potatoes in long-season bands | The only garden crops that meaningfully add calories |
| **2. Storage crops** | Carrots, beets, onions, garlic, cabbage | Keep for months in a cool place with no processing (→ 04-09) |
| **3. High-yield producers** | Zucchini, tomatoes, cucumbers, kale, chard | Large output per square foot |
| **4. Fast / succession** | Radish, lettuce, spinach, turnip greens | Several harvests per season |
| **5. Preserving crops** | Tomatoes, cucumbers (pickles), cabbage (kraut) | Feed the preserving chain (→ 04-04, → 04-08) |
| **6. Perennial** | Rhubarb, asparagus, berries, herbs | Plant once, harvest for years (→ 05-13) |

**Potatoes deserve a special mention.** In most of the country they are the highest-calorie
crop a small garden can grow, they store well somewhere cool and dark (→ 04-09), and they
contain none of the common food allergens. In the warmest bands, plant them early so they are
dug before summer heat.

### Making a small yard produce

- **Intensive spacing** (blocks or "square-foot" grids) instead of long rows. Rows waste
  space in a small yard.
- **Grow up** (→ 05-10): pole beans, cucumbers, tomatoes and small squash on trellises.
- **Succession planting:** as soon as one crop finishes, another goes in the same spot.
- **Interplanting:** fast crops (radish, lettuce) between slow ones (tomatoes, squash).
- **Containers** on the patio and along the fence add real growing area (→ 05-10).
- **Full sun:** 6 to 8 hours of direct sun a day for fruiting crops. Map your yard's sun before
  you dig: check it in the morning, at noon and in late afternoon.

### One weed to learn before you weed

**Poison hemlock comes up in garden beds** across most of the country. Its first-year rosette
(a low circle of leaves) is the plant gardeners most often mistake for parsley or carrot tops,
and it is deadly to eat (→ 05-06). Before the first weeding each spring, look at the hemlock
plate on → 05-06: finely divided, fern-like, glossy leaves on hairless stems that are often
blotched with purple. Any carrot-family plant you did not sow comes out with gloves on and goes
in the trash: not the compost, not the rabbits (→ 05-05), and never into a child's hand.

### Water, and one important warning

- A vegetable garden needs **roughly 1 inch of water a week** from rain or watering (SDSU
  Extension). In a dry spell that is a lot of hauling.
- **Do not water the garden with saltwater-pool water** (→ 03-03). Salt damages soil and kills
  plants, and the damage lasts for seasons.
- **Rain barrels are the right source** (→ 03-05). Used wash water ("greywater") works with
  care (→ 13-03).
- **Mulch heavily** with straw, leaves or cardboard. It is the cheapest water saving there is,
  and it smothers weeds.
- Your home gets about **{{ region.home.precip_in }} inches of precipitation a year**. In the
  wetter bands, raised beds for drainage matter more than watering.

### Food allergies and the garden

{{#if has.condition.CL-04}}Someone in this household has a food allergy (→ 02-04). {{/if}}Garden
produce is the most traceable food you can have: you planted it, you know what it is, and there
is no label to misread. Two cautions: **do not grow a crop that is one of your household's
allergens** (soybeans as edamame, for example, or sesame), and keep allergen-free produce
handling separate from any allergen-containing food preparation (→ 02-04).

### Keeping it quiet

A visibly productive garden in a hungry neighbourhood is an advertisement (→ 14-03). A fence
helps. Consider growing calorie crops out of sight and ornamental-looking edibles (kale, chard,
herbs) where they show.

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- **Containers:** buckets, totes, feed sacks: anything that holds soil and drains.
- **Trellis:** a fence, a livestock panel, branches, string.
- **Mulch:** leaves, grass clippings, cardboard, newspaper.
- **Compost:** kitchen scraps and leaves. **Cat litter and dog waste never go in food-garden
  compost** (→ 05-11).

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** a garden is a **flow**, not a store, and it is seasonal. Outside your frost-free
  window it produces little or nothing unless covered, which is exactly when stored food
  matters most. In the mildest bands the gap is summer heat rather than winter.
- **Ration:** preserve the surplus rather than eating it all fresh (→ 04-06, → 04-08).
- **Resupply:** seed saving (→ 05-03) makes the garden renewable; sprouts and microgreens
  (→ 04-16, → 05-14) cover the fresh-food gap.
- **The honest limit:** a backyard garden supplies a share of your vegetables for part of the
  year. Treat it as nutrition insurance and morale, and keep the calorie plan resting on stored
  staples and protein.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** small, intensive, vertical, fenced. Start improving the soil this season.
- **B (moving):** carry seed (→ 05-03). It is light, and it is next year's food.
- **C (establishing):** a destination may be in a different band from home. Use its own
  calendar (→ 05-18), not this one.
