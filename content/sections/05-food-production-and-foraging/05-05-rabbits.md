# Meat rabbits in a small yard

**What this page is for.** Why rabbits are often the best-fitting livestock for a small lot,
and how to keep them healthy, fed and productive, including the diseases that wipe out a herd.

> **CAUTION.** Rabbit is **extremely lean**. A diet that leans on rabbit without a fat source
> causes "rabbit starvation" (protein poisoning): nausea, weakness and diarrhoea even while
> eating plenty. **Rabbit must be paired with fat** (→ 04-12). And **two diseases can kill a
> whole herd in days** (below).

## Do this now

1. **Start with a trio:** one buck (male), two does (females). That is a working breeding unit.
2. **Plan a fat source** alongside them (→ 04-12).
3. **Vaccinate the breeding stock against RHDV2 while veterinarians are available**, and
   quarantine every new rabbit for 30 days (below).
4. **Plan for summer heat**, not winter cold. Heat kills rabbits.
5. **Be honest with children** about what the animals are for, before they name them
   (→ 20-03).

> **Before the collapse:** many towns count rabbits with chickens under a backyard-livestock
> limit, with setbacks from neighbours' houses and permits above a certain number. A doe's
> litters can push you past a cap fast; ask your city how young rabbits being raised for meat
> are counted. This never decides how you keep animals to survive.

## Why / how it works

### Why rabbits fit a small lot

| Factor | Rabbits | Chickens (→ 05-04) |
|---|---|---|
| Food allergen | None of the major allergens | Eggs are a major allergen |
| Noise | Silent | Hens cluck; roosters are a neighbour problem |
| Space | Very small; hutches stack | Coop plus run |
| Travel | **Caged rabbits travel**, with care about heat | Poorly |
| Cold | **Excellent**; they tolerate cold far better than heat | Good with the right breeds |
| Feed | Hay, forage, garden waste, pellets | Grain-dependent |
| **Manure** | **Will not burn plants**; still must be composted or autumn-applied (below) | "Hot": must be composted first |
| Output | Meat | Eggs and meat |
| Fat | **Very lean: a real limit** | Eggs supply fat |

**The manure is a serious argument.** Rabbit droppings are low in ammonia, so they do not burn
plants the way chicken manure does. But "will not burn plants" is not "safe to eat from": they
carry what any raw manure carries (*E. coli*, *Salmonella*, coccidia). **Compost them, or spread
them raw only on beds that will not be harvested for 120 days; never side-dress crops eaten
raw.** The 120-day interval is the US organic rule for raw manure on crops whose edible part
touches the soil (7 CFR 205.203). Details on → 05-11.

### Productivity

A trio produces a substantial amount of meat a year. Rabbits breed readily and grow fast. On a
small lot this is the highest protein output per square foot you are likely to get.

**But count the feed.** Rabbits turn feed into meat; they do not make calories from nothing.
Their advantage is that they eat **hay, weeds, garden waste and forage** instead of grain.

### Weather

- **Cold:** rabbits handle it well. Dry, draft-free hutches with deep bedding and wind
  protection.
- **Water freezes** at least twice a day in hard cold (→ 03-06). That is the real winter work.
- **Heat is the real danger.** Rabbits die of heat far more readily than of cold. Shade,
  airflow and water in the hottest weeks.{{#if is.hot_summers}} Your July highs average
  {{ region.home.jul_high_f }} °F: plan shade and airflow before the first heat wave.{{/if}}

### The two diseases that kill meat rabbits

- **Coccidiosis** (a gut parasite) is the main killer of young rabbits in small hutches.
  Prevention is the whole answer: **wire floors, feeders and water off the floor, clean water,
  no droppings in the feed.** At slaughter, **a white-spotted liver in a domestic rabbit is
  liver coccidiosis**: the meat is edible cooked to 160 °F; throw out the liver. **The same
  spotted liver in a wild rabbit means tularemia, and the whole animal is thrown out**
  (→ 05-15, → 05-09). Keep the two facts side by side.
- **RHDV2 (rabbit haemorrhagic disease virus 2)** has spread through wild and domestic rabbits
  in many US states in recent years. It kills **80 to 100 % of a herd in days: sudden deaths, often
  with blood at the nose** (South Dakota Animal Industry Board, RHDV2). If it happens: do not
  eat them, double-bag the carcasses, disinfect hutches and tools with a bleach solution
  (→ 29-05), and, while phones work, report it to your state veterinarian. **Vaccinate the
  breeding stock while veterinarians exist**; the vaccine is sold through vets. **Quarantine any
  new rabbit for 30 days** before it meets the herd.
- **Introduce fresh greens to young rabbits gradually.** A sudden switch to wet greens causes
  the gut illness that kills them. Hay first, greens a little at a time.

### Feeding

- **Hay is the staple.** Grass hay stores well.
- **Garden waste, weeds and forage:** dandelion, plantain, clover, carrot tops.
- **Pellets** while you can buy them; plan for the day you cannot.
- **Grow fodder:** sprouted grain (→ 04-16) and cut greens.
- **Never feed anything from the toxic-plant list** (→ 05-06). The hemlocks kill rabbits as
  surely as people.

### The lean-meat problem

Rabbit is roughly 8 % fat or less. A diet of little but rabbit causes protein poisoning. It
killed explorers and trappers, and it is why rabbit alone is not a survival plan.

**Pair rabbit with:** stored fats and oils (→ 04-12); rendered fat from fattier animals; and
carbohydrates from stored grain (→ 04-03). **Cook rabbit to 160 °F** (FoodSafety.gov).

### Children and the animals

Children bond with animals. What works for many families: keep breeding stock as named animals
and the young raised for meat unnamed; involve the children in care but not slaughter; be
honest and matter-of-fact (→ 20-03). Do the processing out of sight (→ 05-16).

**Scavenge or substitute:**

- Hutches build easily from salvaged lumber and welded wire.
- **Weeds and garden waste** are free feed all summer. Dandelion and plantain are excellent.
- Wild rabbits can be trapped (→ 05-15), with the same lean-meat caution and the tularemia
  check.

## When it runs out

- **Runway:** **hay and feed**. The herd renews itself; the feed does not.
- **Ration:** forage and garden waste first; sprout grain to stretch feed (→ 04-16).
- **Resupply:** rabbits are the most feed-flexible small livestock. Weeds, hay and garden
  trimmings go a long way.
- **Why it matters:** a breeding trio is **renewable protein**, and its manure feeds the garden
  (→ 05-11): two loops closed at once.

## Phase notes

- **A (at home):** often the best-fitting livestock for a small fenced yard.
- **B (moving):** caged rabbits can travel in a trailer or vehicle (→ 22-05). **Heat is the
  whole risk:** travel in the cool hours, never seal live animals in an unvented box in warm
  weather, shade at every stop, water at every stop. In a heat wave, honestly reassess whether
  they come.
- **C (establishing):** more forage and space; the stock you bring is the seed of the next herd.
